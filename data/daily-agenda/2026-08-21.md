## Slot 1 — Food

- **Edition slot:** 1
- **Status:** draft
- **Role:** flagship
- **Type:** daily
- **Target length:** 1,400–2,000 words
- **Working title:** *The Speed of a Meal: What Food Texture Can Tell Us About Overeating*
- **Reader question:** Can food texture and eating speed matter as much as the ingredient list for how much we eat?
- **Precise original angle:** Replace the moral binary of “processed versus unprocessed” with a testable two-axis model: eating rate and energy density. Compare controlled feeding trials to show why two meals with similar labels can produce different intake, then give readers a practical way to slow a meal without pretending that texture explains every effect of ultra-processed food.
- **Why it will remain useful in six months:** Eating rate, texture and energy density are stable mechanisms readers can apply across products, diets and changing headlines.
- **Mechanism the writer must explain:** Softer textures and fewer required chews can increase grams consumed per minute. When rapid intake combines with high energy density, calories may arrive faster than satiation signals can influence behaviour. Explain what the trials establish, what they merely suggest, and why palatability, processing and nutrient composition remain possible contributors.
- **Key uncertainties:** Controlled trials are short and often small; NOVA classifications remain debated; laboratory menus do not reproduce every household context; individual response varies; one newer study includes industry-affiliated authors. Do not turn an eating-rate finding into a weight-loss prescription.
- **Practical takeaway to earn:** A reader should be able to audit one regular meal for texture, chew demand, energy density and pacing, and make one low-risk change such as adding a minimally processed, slower-to-eat component.
- **Candidate sources (re-open before writing):**
  1. [Ultra-Processed Diets Cause Excess Calorie Intake and Weight Gain](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31105044/) — Cell Metabolism via U.S. National Library of Medicine; published online 2019-05-16; **primary randomised controlled feeding study**.
  2. [Oral processing characteristics of solid meals: effects on food intake](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35285882/) — The American Journal of Clinical Nutrition via U.S. National Library of Medicine; published online 2022-03-14; **primary randomised crossover trial**.
  3. [Food texture-based differences in eating rate influence energy intake](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41314613/) — The American Journal of Clinical Nutrition via U.S. National Library of Medicine; published online 2025-11-26; **primary randomised controlled feeding trial**.
  4. [Ultra-processed or minimally processed diets following healthy dietary guidelines](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03842-0) — Nature Medicine; published 2025-08-04; **primary randomised crossover trial**.

## Slot 2 — Sport

- **Edition slot:** 2
- **Status:** draft
- **Role:** evidence
- **Type:** analysis
- **Target length:** 1,000–1,500 words
- **Working title:** *Cold Plunges After Training: Recovery Today, Adaptation Tomorrow*
- **Reader question:** Does a cold plunge help recovery, or can it blunt the gains a workout is meant to create?
- **Precise original angle:** Treat “recovery” as several outcomes on different clocks: soreness and perceived readiness over hours, performance over days, and strength or muscle adaptation over weeks. Build a decision map for competition, closely spaced training and long-term resistance programmes rather than declaring cold water simply good or bad.
- **Why it will remain useful in six months:** The trade-off between immediate comfort and training adaptation applies regardless of the latest recovery trend or athlete endorsement.
- **Mechanism the writer must explain:** Cooling changes tissue temperature, blood flow, pain perception and post-exercise signalling. Those effects may relieve soreness while repeated immediate immersion after resistance exercise may attenuate parts of the cellular response involved in adaptation.
- **Key uncertainties:** Studies use different temperatures, durations and exercise types; many samples are small and male-heavy; subjective soreness is not the same as recovered capacity; endurance and resistance training may differ; evidence cannot justify individual medical advice.
- **Practical takeaway to earn:** Readers should leave with a context table: when rapid turnaround may justify cold water, when adaptation is the priority, and when ordinary cool-down, sleep and nutrition are the more relevant levers.
- **Candidate sources (re-open before writing):**
  1. [Post-exercise cold water immersion attenuates acute anabolic signalling and long-term adaptations](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26174323/) — The Journal of Physiology via U.S. National Library of Medicine; published online 2015-08-13; **primary randomised training study**.
  2. [Cold-Water Immersion and Resistance Training Adaptations: A Meta-Analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-022-01644-9) — Sports Medicine; published 2022; **systematic review and meta-analysis**.
  3. [Effects of cold-water immersion compared with other recovery modalities](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/effects-of-cold-water-immersion-compared-with-other-recovery-moda/) — Macquarie University research repository / Sports Medicine; published online 2022-12-17; **systematic review and meta-analysis**.
  4. [Cold Water Immersion for Recovery and Its Effect on Physical Performance](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.660291/full) — Frontiers in Sports and Active Living; published 2021; **narrative evidence review**.

## Slot 3 — Safety

- **Edition slot:** 3
- **Status:** draft
- **Role:** practical
- **Type:** analysis
- **Target length:** 900–1,400 words
- **Working title:** *The Ten-Minute Charging Audit: Reducing Lithium-Ion Fire Risk at Home*
- **Reader question:** Which everyday charging habits turn a battery fault into a household fire risk, and what can a family safely check?
- **Precise original angle:** Separate the probability of a battery failure from the choices that amplify its consequences. Organise a room-by-room audit around charger compatibility, physical damage, heat, charging location, escape routes, supervision, recalls and end-of-life disposal.
- **Why it will remain useful in six months:** Rechargeable devices, e-bikes and power tools will remain common, while the audit works across brands and avoids dependence on a single recall.
- **Mechanism the writer must explain:** Damage, manufacturing defects, unsuitable chargers or unsafe charging conditions can initiate overheating and thermal runaway. Heat and flammable gases can propagate between cells; charging near combustible material or in an exit route increases consequences even when the initial fault is rare.
- **Key uncertainties:** Risk varies sharply by product quality and use; certification lowers but does not eliminate risk; official recommendations vary by product and jurisdiction; incident statistics evolve. Do not give improvised firefighting advice—direct readers to local fire-service instructions.
- **Practical takeaway to earn:** A printable five-point household check: correct charger, intact battery, hard ventilated surface, clear exit route, and safe recycling/recall check.
- **Candidate sources (re-open before writing):**
  1. [Battery Fire Safety](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/prevention/home-fires/prevent-fires/batteries/) — U.S. Fire Administration; undated, accessed 2026-08-21; **official safety guidance**.
  2. [Micromobility Information Center](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Micromobility-Information-Center) — U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission; undated, accessed 2026-08-21; **official regulator guidance and records**.
  3. [Learn to Take C.H.A.R.G.E. of Battery Safety](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/blog/learn-to-take-charge-of-battery-safety/) — U.S. Fire Administration; published 2023-11-30; **official safety guidance**.

[Safety Standard for Lithium-Ion Batteries Used in Micromobility Products](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/24/2026-12749/safety-standard-for-lithium-ion-batteries-used-in-micromobility-products-and-electrical-systems-of) — U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission / Federal Register; published 2026-06-24; **primary regulatory record**.

## Slot 4 — Soul

- **Edition slot:** 4
- **Status:** draft
- **Role:** memory
- **Type:** analysis
- **Target length:** 1,000–1,600 words
- **Working title:** *How the Five Stages of Grief Became a Checklist*
- **Reader question:** Must grief move through denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance in that order?
- **Precise original angle:** Trace the model from Elisabeth Kübler-Ross’s work with dying patients, through its popular transfer to bereavement and organisational change, to later criticism and the modern distinction between normal grief variability and prolonged grief disorder. Preserve the humane achievement of listening to dying people while correcting the rigid checklist.
- **Why it will remain useful in six months:** Readers repeatedly encounter the five stages during bereavement, illness and major life change; understanding their history can reduce self-judgment long after a news cycle ends.
- **Mechanism the writer must explain:** A simple sequence offers narrative control during uncertainty, so culture repeats it even when individual adaptation oscillates. Rigid expectations can make normal variation feel like failure and can obscure persistent impairment that deserves professional assessment.
- **Key uncertainties:** Grief is culturally and individually variable; stages may function as metaphors without being a universal sequence; diagnostic boundaries are clinical tools, not self-tests; the article must not medicalise ordinary sorrow.
- **Practical takeaway to earn:** Give readers a non-linear reflection tool—what comes in waves, what support helps, and whether daily functioning remains persistently impaired—plus clear language for seeking qualified help without diagnosing themselves.
- **Candidate sources (re-open before writing):**
  1. [Stanford acquires archive of Elisabeth Kübler-Ross](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2019/03/stanford-acquires-archive-of-elisabeth-kubler-ross.html) — Stanford Medicine; published 2019-03-26; **primary institutional archive record**.
  2. [Cautioning Health-Care Professionals: Bereaved Persons Are Misguided Through the Stages of Grief](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28355991/) — Omega via U.S. National Library of Medicine; published 2017-05; **critical analysis**.
  3. [Stages of grief portrayed on the Internet](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34925174/) — Palliative & Supportive Care via U.S. National Library of Medicine; published online 2021; **content analysis**.

## Slot 5 — Good News

- **Edition slot:** 5
- **Status:** draft
- **Role:** evergreen
- **Type:** analysis
- **Target length:** 1,000–1,500 words
- **Working title:** *The Library Beyond Books: When Borrowing Things Really Beats Owning Them*
- **Reader question:** Can a community library of tools and household items save money, waste and loneliness without creating new logistical costs?
- **Precise original angle:** Audit the model rather than celebrate it. Explain the conditions under which borrowing displaces a purchase: sufficient utilisation, convenient access, maintenance, repair, transport distance and member trust. Pair environmental claims with their stated assumptions.
- **Why it will remain useful in six months:** The make-or-borrow decision applies to drills, carpet cleaners, camping equipment and future categories; it gives readers a durable way to judge local sharing schemes.
- **Mechanism the writer must explain:** Shared utilisation spreads an item’s embodied material and manufacturing cost over more uses. Benefits occur only when a loan replaces ownership and when transport, cleaning, breakage and operating overhead do not erase the savings.
- **Key uncertainties:** Impact calculations often rely on self-reported avoided purchases; substitution rates are hard to verify; small schemes face financing, liability and inventory challenges; access may be uneven. Label operator reports as such.
- **Practical takeaway to earn:** A four-question borrowing test: frequency of use, round-trip distance, rental/repair terms and whether the loan truly replaces a purchase.
- **Candidate sources (re-open before writing):**
  1. [Library of Things Impact Report 2023](https://participate.libraryofthings.co.uk/impact-report-2023) — Library of Things; published 2023; **operator primary report / self-reported evidence**.

## Slot 6 — Travel

- **Edition slot:** 6
- **Status:** draft
- **Role:** human
- **Type:** analysis
- **Target length:** 900–1,400 words
- **Working title:** *The Bookseller Who Gave a Welsh Town a New Plot*
- **Reader question:** How did a fading Welsh market town turn secondhand books into a durable reason to visit?
- **Precise original angle:** Begin with the documented choices of Richard Booth, then explain how a cluster of booksellers, a castle, a market and a festival created a destination ecology that survived its founder. Contrast authentic local identity with publicity stunts and extract lessons without presenting cultural tourism as an easy template.
- **Why it will remain useful in six months:** It is a human story about place-making and local economic identity, useful beyond any one festival date.
- **Mechanism the writer must explain:** A specialist cluster lowers search costs for visitors, increases dwell time and gives complementary businesses a shared identity. Repeated events and institutions reinforce the story, but seasonality and local capacity determine whether benefits endure.
- **Key uncertainties:** Historical accounts blend documentation and legend; current shop counts vary by definition; tourism effects are difficult to isolate from wider trends; festivals also create congestion and housing pressure. Do not romanticise decline or imply every town can copy Hay-on-Wye.
- **Practical takeaway to earn:** Give travellers a respectful “visit deeper” guide: use independent shops, explore beyond the headline venue, travel off-peak when possible and learn which local institutions maintain the place.
- **Candidate sources (re-open before writing):**
  1. [The King of Hay](https://www.haycastletrust.org/the-king-of-hay.aspx) — Hay Castle Trust; undated, accessed 2026-08-21; **primary institutional history**.

## Slot 7 — Mysteries

- **Edition slot:** 7
- **Status:** reserve
- **Role:** reserve
- **Type:** feature
- **Target length:** 1,000–1,600 words
- **Working title:** *The Face in the Clouds: Why the Brain Finds People in Things*
- **Reader question:** Why does a socket, tree or cloud look like a face even when we know it is not one?
- **Precise original angle:** Follow roughly the first quarter-second of perception: a coarse face-like pattern receives privileged processing, then later object recognition corrects the interpretation. Frame pareidolia as a useful false-positive tendency, while carefully separating everyday perception from hallucination or diagnosis.
- **Why it will remain useful in six months:** Pareidolia is a timeless doorway into neuroscience, art, design and human pattern detection; it does not depend on current events.
- **Mechanism the writer must explain:** A minimal arrangement resembling eyes above a mouth can recruit face-selective visual regions and top-down expectations. Fast sensitivity may favour false positives because missing a real face can carry a larger social cost than briefly seeing one in an object.
- **Key uncertainties:** Evolutionary explanations are plausible but partly inferential; laboratory images simplify real scenes; studies disagree on some individual differences; neural activation does not mean the brain fully believes an object is alive.
- **Practical takeaway to earn:** Offer a safe observation exercise using ordinary objects, then show how to test a perception by rotating, cropping or changing context—an approachable lesson in how expectations shape seeing.
- **Candidate sources (re-open before writing):**
  1. [Neural correlates of face pareidolia](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6290235/) — PLOS ONE via PubMed Central; published 2018; **primary fMRI study**.
  2. [Face pareidolia recruits mechanisms for detecting human social attention](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-18325-8) — Nature Communications; published 2020-09-07; **primary experimental research**.
  3. [Why we see faces in objects](https://news.mit.edu/2011/face-perception-0109) — MIT News; published 2012-01-09; **institutional reporting and analysis of primary research**.
  4. [Do you see faces in the clouds?](https://www.unsw.edu.au/newsroom/news/2026/04/do-you-see-faces-in-the-clouds) — UNSW Sydney; published 2026-04-07; **institutional reporting on primary research**.

## PERSPECTIVES

Two readings of this week sit next to each other, and both have some evidence.

The first is structural. The Champions League now runs a "league phase" in which clubs face a mix of opponents instead of a small home-and-away group, and the top sides tend to meet weaker ones first. The France 24 and Al Jazeera reports fit this reading: Real Madrid, Aston Villa and others are rolling through fixtures that would once have been drawn from a tighter pool. A Guardian week-roundup headline — "No jeopardy but loads of fun" — captures the same impression from a different angle: more spectacle, less tension.

The second reading is individual. Mbappe scored again. Guler scored again. Bernardo Silva scored his first for Madrid since his summer move from Manchester City. Harry Kane, interviewed in effect by his own goals, scored once more against Arsenal — the Guardian match report notes he is still the top scorer in north London derby history with 14 goals. Great forwards are simply in form.

The two readings are not in competition. A format that creates easier early fixtures also hands sharp forwards softer defences to shoot at. What is worth resisting is the claim that this week proves anything about which teams will win in April. Three matchdays into eight — and then into knockouts — is still an early reading.

## CONTEXT

The league phase is young. In its current form, each club plays a run of league-phase matches against a varied set of opponents, with the aim of producing more fixtures between elite clubs. Three matchdays in, that aim is partly undermined by its own seeding: a top-tier club tends to meet two or three mid-table opponents before it meets another top-tier side.

The result, so far, is what viewers are seeing — scorelines of 4-1, perfect starts for the usual names, and a Guardian roundup willing to use the word "record".

Of the clubs the sources name as being on their third win, Real Madrid and Aston Villa stand out. ## DEEPER

A football match used to be, at its best, a kind of civic emergency. Something could go wrong. A giant could fall. A small club could, for one evening, be the city's whole mood.

What this week is quietly doing is testing a different idea: that European football can be more constant, more watchable, more weekly — and in that bargain give up a little of the fear.

The Guardian roundup names it with an almost rueful honesty: no jeopardy, loads of fun. That sentence is funnier than it looks. It describes the trade a new format has made, and it asks a question that will keep returning in sport over the next decade. Can an entertainment product that promises always-good matches also deliver the thing that made great matches great — the sense, in the ninetieth minute, that something you cannot pay for might actually happen?

The honest answer is that nobody yet knows. Three matchdays is three matchdays. Mbappe scoring in all three of them is a story that will look different if, in February, he scores against Bayern or Paris Saint-Germain in a tie that genuinely hangs.

Until then, it is worth sitting with the feeling that this autumn is giving European football watchers. The scores are gaudy. The storylines are tidy. And the pleasure, like weather in October, might turn without warning.

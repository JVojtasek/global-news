## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** “Net 30” on a business invoice normally means the full amount is due within 30 calendar days of the agreed starting point, commonly the invoice date.

**What it means.** The supplier has delivered before receiving cash. For those 30 days, the seller is financing the buyer through trade credit, usually without calling it a loan.

**Risks and impact.** Ambiguous dates, missing purchase-order numbers, approval delays and deliberate late payment can turn 30 days into 45 or 60. A profitable business can still struggle if wages and suppliers must be paid first.

**What can be done.** Agree the clock, acceptance process, invoice route and dispute contact before work begins. Put one unambiguous due date on the invoice and confirm that the customer's system accepted it.

**What to watch.** Track actual days to payment by customer, not merely the terms printed on invoices. The gap between promised and real payment is the useful number.

## FACTS

Net payment terms state when a business customer must pay after receiving goods or services. Net 15, Net 30 and Net 60 generally mean that the full balance is due within 15, 30 or 60 days. Unless the contract says otherwise, the count normally uses calendar days, including weekends.

The expression does not mean “30 percent,” and the word *net* does not make the amount after tax or after an imagined discount. It identifies the full amount due under the invoice. A separate notation such as “2/10, Net 30” offers a 2 percent discount for payment within ten days; otherwise the full amount remains due at 30 days.

The starting point must be explicit. It might be the invoice date, receipt of a valid invoice, delivery, or formal acceptance of the work. Those dates can differ. If an invoice dated Monday sits in the wrong inbox until Friday, buyer and seller may begin counting from different days.

The legal default also varies by jurisdiction and contract. UK guidance says businesses may set their own terms; without an agreed payment date, payment is generally due within 30 days of the later relevant event. EU rules establish rights around late commercial payment, but they do not turn every “Net 30” printed unilaterally on a document into a universal global contract.

## EVIDENCE

UK government research published in 2024 found that 54 percent of surveyed businesses with business customers offered 30-day terms as their standard. Yet only 52 percent said customers typically paid in line with contractual terms, while 36 percent said payment took longer. Small businesses were more likely to report delay beyond the agreement.

The mechanism was not simply bad character. Among businesses explaining late receipts, 24 percent cited administrative errors. When businesses described their own late payments to suppliers, 36 percent cited administrative errors, 31 percent disputed invoices and 23 percent technical problems such as invoices becoming lost or failing to arrive.

There was evidence of power too. Eighteen percent of respondents said customers sometimes paid late deliberately, treating the delay as free finance. Microbusinesses were less likely than large businesses to pursue payment formally. The most common reason for avoiding formal action was fear of damaging the customer relationship.

These are survey responses, not audited transaction records for every industry. They do not prove that a particular unpaid invoice is deliberate. They do show why a due date alone is not a payment system: correct data, routing, acceptance and bargaining power sit behind it.

## PERSPECTIVES

For the buyer, Net 30 can be ordinary working-capital management. Goods may be checked, entered into inventory and sold before cash leaves the account. Larger organisations need time to match an invoice to a purchase order, confirm delivery and obtain approval. A defined cycle can reduce errors.

For the supplier, the same period is unsecured credit. Payroll, materials, tax and rent do not pause while the buyer's approval chain moves. A 30-day term that routinely pays on day 50 has shifted twenty extra days of financing onto the smaller firm.

The strongest case for standard terms is predictability. Everyone knows the timetable, and repeat buyers can purchase without arranging a card payment for every delivery. The strongest challenge is that “industry standard” can hide a one-sided bargain. The 2024 UK research found industry convention was the most common factor businesses named when setting terms; cash-flow management was mentioned less often.

Neither immediate payment nor long terms are automatically fair. Upfront payment can transfer performance risk to the buyer. Long terms transfer liquidity risk to the supplier. A workable agreement makes that allocation visible and prices it honestly.

## CONTEXT

An invoice records a claim; cash in the bank settles it. Accounting separates those moments through accounts receivable. A business can report revenue and profit after completing a project while still waiting for the customer to pay. That is why the sentence “sales are strong” can coexist with an empty operating account.

Consider a small studio that completes a £10,000 project on 1 March. It pays freelancers and software costs during March and invoices Net 30. If payment arrives on 31 March, the studio financed those costs for a month. If the buyer first rejects the invoice because a purchase-order number is missing, the clock may effectively restart. Nothing changed about the quality of the work. Everything changed about liquidity.

Late-payment law tries to limit the worst imbalance. EU Directive 2011/7 gives creditors rights to interest after contractual or statutory periods and sets reference rules when no date is fixed. UK law also allows statutory interest and recovery costs in qualifying business transactions. Exact rights depend on the contract, parties and jurisdiction, so a general article cannot decide a specific dispute.

### Practical takeaway

Before accepting terms, put six items in writing:

1. the event that starts the clock;
2. one calendar due date;
3. the legal name and billing address;
4. the required purchase-order or project reference;
5. who confirms acceptance and by when;
6. where a dispute must be raised.

After sending, confirm receipt in the customer's payment system. Then measure **days sales outstanding by customer**: actual payment date minus invoice date, interpreted alongside the agreed terms. A customer promising 30 days and averaging 48 is offering a different deal from the one on paper.

## DEEPER

The invoice is a small document with a large psychological trick. Once work is delivered, the seller feels finished. The buyer may feel the process has only begun. Between those two perceptions sits a quiet transfer of time.

Large organisations rarely experience the delay as one decision. One person requests the work, another approves it, software checks the purchase order, finance schedules a payment run. Each step can look reasonable from inside the building. The supplier experiences all of them as one missing sum.

That difference explains why polite reminders often sound strangely mismatched. The buyer hears an administrative query. The supplier may be deciding whether wages clear on Friday. Neither side needs to be dishonest for the relationship to become unequal; one side simply has more room for time to pass.

Clear terms do not remove trust. They make trust measurable. The best commercial relationship is not the one that never mentions payment. It is the one in which both sides can predict what happens after the work is done.

## REFLECT

- When a customer asks for longer terms, who is being asked to finance whom?
- Does your invoice state a payment period, or a due date that two people can read only one way?
- Which customer has the widest gap between promised and actual payment?

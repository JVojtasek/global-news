## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** In 1960, new FDA reviewer Frances Oldham Kelsey received an application to sell thalidomide in the United States and declined to let it take effect while safety questions remained unanswered.

**What it means.** Her achievement was not mystical foresight. It was disciplined review: the burden stayed with the sponsor to provide adequate evidence rather than with the regulator to prove harm in advance.

**Risks and impact.** Thalidomide exposure during early pregnancy caused severe fetal harm in countries where it was widely used. Some US patients were also exposed through an investigational distribution programme.

**What can be done.** Patients can ask what evidence supports a medicine's approved use, read official safety information and report suspected adverse effects without changing treatment on their own.

**What to watch.** Good regulation is visible in the questions, data requirements and post-market monitoring—not merely in the speed of an approval decision.

## FACTS

Frances Oldham Kelsey joined the US Food and Drug Administration in 1960 as one of a small number of medical officers reviewing new-drug applications. Her first assignment concerned Kevadon, the proposed US brand of thalidomide, a sedative already sold in many other countries.

The application did not satisfy her. The FDA and National Library of Medicine biographies describe Kelsey pressing the manufacturer for more evidence while the company sought approval. Reports of nerve damage among people taking the drug repeatedly added to her concern. She continued to withhold clearance.

By late 1961, physicians in Europe and Australia had connected thalidomide taken during pregnancy with severe birth defects. The drug was withdrawn from many markets. It had not received US marketing approval, although the sponsor had distributed investigational samples to physicians, and some American patients were exposed.

In 1962, Congress enacted the Kefauver–Harris amendments, strengthening requirements for evidence of effectiveness as well as safety and tightening oversight of clinical investigations. Kelsey continued a long FDA career in new drugs, scientific investigations and compliance.

## EVIDENCE

The central evidence is unusually well aligned. FDA's institutional history, the National Library of Medicine biography and historical medical reviews agree on the sequence: Kelsey received the application, found the safety evidence inadequate, resisted pressure and did not allow it to become effective before thalidomide's fetal harms were recognised abroad.

It is tempting to tidy that sequence into a lone-hero miracle. The record supports something more instructive. Kelsey had training in pharmacology and medicine, colleagues and legal authority. She did not need to demonstrate the drug's full mechanism of harm. She needed to decide whether the sponsor had met the existing standard.

Later research has explored several mechanisms behind thalidomide's teratogenic effects, including its binding to cereblon and downstream disruption of developmental processes. Mechanistic understanding arrived after the regulatory decision. That order matters: public-health protection sometimes depends on refusing to treat missing evidence as reassuring evidence.

The history also has an uncomfortable footnote. Lack of marketing approval did not mean zero US exposure. Investigational samples reached physicians under looser rules than would be acceptable today. The episode therefore supports stronger controls across the whole evidence pathway, not a simple claim that one border kept everyone safe.

## PERSPECTIVES

The heroic framing says Kelsey saved the United States. It rightly honours professional courage, especially the ability to withstand repeated commercial pressure. It can, however, make safety sound dependent on finding an exceptional person at exactly the right desk.

The institutional framing says the system worked. That is partly true: the law placed the burden of demonstrating safety on the applicant, and FDA leadership supported the reviewer. Yet the investigational distribution gap and the reforms that followed show that the system was unfinished.

A third framing sees thalidomide as the reason pregnant people were later excluded from research. The disaster certainly cast a long shadow over trials in pregnancy. But broad exclusion created another problem: medicines were still used without good pregnancy-specific evidence. Protection from research and protection through research are not identical.

Kelsey's durable contribution sits between hero worship and bureaucracy worship. A capable reviewer used a rule that permitted “not yet” when the file was inadequate. The institution backed the pause. Congress then changed the rules in response to what the episode exposed.

## CONTEXT

Drug regulation before 1962 focused more heavily on safety than on proof that a medicine actually worked. Review deadlines and evidentiary standards were less developed, while clinical testing and informed-consent practices were changing unevenly.

Thalidomide was marketed in parts of Europe and elsewhere as a sedative and used for nausea in pregnancy. The timing of exposure proved crucial: fetal development can be disrupted during narrow windows early in pregnancy, sometimes before a person knows they are pregnant. That is why a reassuring experience in a non-pregnant adult cannot answer every reproductive-safety question.

The 1962 amendments required “substantial evidence” of effectiveness from adequate and well-controlled investigations and strengthened FDA authority over trials. Modern approval is far more structured, but it is not a guarantee that every effect is known on day one. Trials have limited sizes, durations and populations. Rare or delayed harms may emerge only after wider use.

The proper comparison is therefore not “regulated equals risk-free.” It is a chain: preclinical work, phased trials, regulatory review, manufacturing controls, labelling, surveillance and revision when evidence changes. Kelsey's desk became famous because one link held under pressure.

## PEOPLE

Kelsey's story is often illustrated with the photograph of President John F. Kennedy giving her the President's Award for Distinguished Federal Civilian Service in 1962. The quieter image is more useful: a new employee reading an imperfect application and sending questions back.

Her position was not glamorous. Reviewers live inside tables, reports, correspondence and deadlines. Their public success may look like nothing happened—a medicine did not reach the market, an unsafe batch did not ship, a trial did not proceed unchanged.

That negative space makes prevention hard to value. Speed produces a visible date. A careful delay produces more questions. Kelsey's case reminds us that “still under review” can describe work rather than obstruction, provided the questions are specific, evidence-based and answered transparently.

## DEEPER

For ordinary readers, the practical lesson is not to second-guess individual prescriptions from a historical story. It is to understand what useful questions look like.

When reading about a newly approved medicine, distinguish approval for a particular use and population from a blanket declaration of safety. Check the regulator's official label or public assessment, not just a manufacturer's announcement. Ask a clinician or pharmacist how the evidence applies to your circumstances, especially pregnancy, age, other medicines or conditions. If a suspected adverse effect occurs, use the appropriate national reporting system and seek professional advice; do not stop a prescribed medicine solely because of an article.

For policy claims, watch the burden of proof. “No harm has been proved” is not equivalent to “adequate safety evidence has been supplied.” Nor does every unanswered question justify permanent refusal. Good review is a disciplined pause with a route to an answer.

Kelsey became famous for saying no, but that is not quite right. Her answer was “show me.” The distinction is the whole architecture of evidence-based regulation.

## REFLECT

Why do institutions find a visible approval easier to celebrate than a well-judged delay?

Where else should “not yet” remain available when evidence is incomplete but pressure for a decision is intense?

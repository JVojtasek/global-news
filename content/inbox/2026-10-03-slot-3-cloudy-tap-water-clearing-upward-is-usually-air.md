## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Tap water can arrive looking as if a spoonful of milk has been stirred into it, then turn clear within a minute or two.

**What it means.** When the clear zone begins at the bottom and moves upward, the usual cause is tiny air bubbles rising out of water after pressure or temperature changes. It is the household version of opening a fizzy drink, only much less dramatic.

**Risks and impact.** Trapped air is harmless. Persistent cloudiness, colour, grit, an oily appearance, or an unusual taste or smell is a different observation and should not be waved away as bubbles.

**What can be done.** Fill a clean, clear glass from the cold tap, set it down and watch the direction in which it clears. Compare cold and hot water and more than one tap.

**What to watch.** If it does not clear, lasts beyond 24 hours, or comes with taste or odour, contact the water supplier. If only one tap or the hot supply is affected, the plumbing may be the better place to investigate.

## FACTS

Cloudy or milky tap water is commonly a crowd of bubbles too small to see separately. The U.S. Geological Survey explains that water inside a supply pipe is under pressure. Dissolved gases can stay in that pressurised water, then come out of solution as the water enters a glass at ordinary atmospheric pressure.

Temperature matters too. Cold water can hold more dissolved air than warmer water. Water that was cold in a reservoir or main may warm on its way indoors, leaving some of that air with nowhere to remain except as bubbles. The UK Drinking Water Inspectorate says air can also enter or become noticeable after a burst main or when a main is returned to service after work.

The direction of clearing is the useful clue. Bubbles rise, so the bottom of the glass becomes transparent first and the boundary moves upward. The Inspectorate says this sort of white water is normally short-lived and should usually clear within 24 hours.

That description does not cover every strange-looking glass. Rust or sediment can produce brown, yellow or gritty water. White particles may be mineral scale or material from plumbing. Appearance is evidence, not a laboratory analysis.

## EVIDENCE

Three public water authorities describe the same mechanism and the same simple test. USGS connects cloudiness to dissolved air, pressure release and cold water. The Drinking Water Inspectorate tells consumers to watch a glass clear from the bottom upward. San Francisco's Public Utilities Commission likewise says temperature and pressure changes can form bubbles that rise and dissipate after a couple of minutes.

The agreement matters because these sources serve different systems on two continents. None depends on a product being sold to fix the problem. Their advice converges on observation before intervention.

There are limits. The glass test identifies behaviour consistent with air; it does not certify every aspect of water quality. It cannot detect an invisible contaminant, and it does not explain coloured water or particles that settle, float or smear. A utility's water-quality report and service alerts tell you about the public supply, while conditions inside a building can still affect an individual tap.

The safest conclusion is therefore narrow: quick bottom-to-top clearing strongly supports the air explanation. Failure to clear, or the addition of colour, taste, odour or material, changes the question.

## PERSPECTIVES

To a householder, a white glass can look like contamination because “clear” is part of what potable water is supposed to look like. That instinct is useful; noticing a change is the first step in reporting a genuine problem. The mistake is to stop at appearance and assume that every white cloud has the same cause.

For a water supplier, trapped air is an aesthetic issue rather than a health failure. Network pressure, cold weather and maintenance can explain a cluster of calls even when testing shows the water itself remains within standards. That framing is technically sound, but it needs the practical test attached. “Harmless” is not reassuring when the customer has not been shown what harmless air does in a glass.

A plumber sees a third boundary: public main versus private plumbing. The Inspectorate notes that repeatedly cloudy water can be linked to warmed cold-water pipes, a partly closed stop tap or a tap aerator. SFPUC notes that white particles, especially on the hot side, can come from mineral scale or a deteriorating water-heater component. The useful question is not simply “Is the water cloudy?” but “Where, when and how does the cloud disappear?”

## CONTEXT

Water looks transparent when light mostly passes through it. A cloud of microscopic bubbles creates thousands of boundaries between water and gas. Those boundaries scatter light in many directions, making the glass look white. As the bubbles merge, rise and escape, there are fewer boundaries to scatter light and the water appears clear again.

That is why the test works without equipment. Use a clean glass and the cold tap, because hot-water systems introduce their own tank, pipes and scale. Let the glass stand rather than stirring it. If a clear layer appears at the bottom and climbs, air is leaving. If particles fall to the bottom, remain suspended or collect on the surface, you are observing something else.

Next, compare locations. One tap points toward its aerator or nearby plumbing. All cold taps point more toward the building supply or public network. Cloudiness appearing after announced street work fits the trapped-air explanation; an unusual smell, taste or colour does not become harmless merely because work happened nearby.

The final check is administrative, not chemical: look for a utility notice and call the supplier when the pattern does not resolve. The Inspectorate advises making contact when white water persists beyond 24 hours, fails to clear, or accompanies unusual taste or odour.

## PEOPLE

The one-minute test is especially useful for tenants, travellers and anyone who does not know the plumbing history of the building. It produces a description that a landlord, plumber or water company can act on: cold or hot, one tap or all taps, bottom-up clearing or persistent material, with or without a smell.

That is better than buying a filter on sight. A filter aimed at a contaminant that has not been identified may do nothing about pressure, a damaged heater part or work on the main. It can also make the original pattern harder to describe.

Record a short video if the effect vanishes quickly. Note the time, taps tested and any nearby maintenance. The aim is not to turn the kitchen into a laboratory. It is to replace “the water looks wrong” with a small set of observations that separates the ordinary from the unresolved.

## DEEPER

Many domestic mysteries become frightening because they arrive without scale. A cloud appears in a glass, a pipe knocks once, a light flickers. The mind supplies a story before the evidence supplies a mechanism.

The answer is not to distrust that alarm. It is to give it a sequence. Observe the direction. Change one condition. Compare another tap. Check the public record. Escalate when the pattern refuses the ordinary explanation. This is modest detective work, and modesty is the point: the test should narrow a question, not pretend to settle every possible one.

The moving line in the glass is unusually generous evidence. It lets the cause reveal itself in real time. Air rises; sediment behaves differently. A pressure effect passes; an unresolved taste or odour remains a reason to ask for help.

Good practical knowledge often has this shape. It does not promise certainty from the sofa. It tells us which observation earns reassurance and which one earns a phone call. The glass becomes not a verdict but a sorting tray for possibilities.

There is a quiet discipline in stopping at the boundary of what a test can show. The bubbles can explain the whiteness. They cannot tell you everything about the water, the pipes or the day ahead.

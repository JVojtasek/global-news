## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Nearly four decades after countries signed the Montreal Protocol, controlled ozone-depleting chemicals are declining in the atmosphere and the stratospheric ozone layer is showing recovery.

**What it means.** This is not a lucky turn in the weather. It is the delayed result of a treaty that converted scientific warning into chemical phase-outs, reporting, finance and repeated revisions.

**Risks and impact.** The Antarctic ozone hole still appears each spring, varies sharply from year to year and is not expected to return to its 1980 state until around 2066.

**What can be done.** Governments must maintain compliance, find unexplained emissions, manage old chemical “banks” in equipment and preserve the observing network that makes backsliding visible.

**What to watch.** Follow the long trend in ozone and atmospheric chlorine and bromine, not one dramatic annual map; also watch gaps in satellite monitoring and new sources of ozone-changing emissions.

## FACTS

Ozone near the ground can harm lungs and plants. High in the stratosphere, the same three-oxygen molecule absorbs ultraviolet-B radiation that would otherwise reach living things. The Antarctic “hole” is not an empty opening but a large seasonal region where total ozone falls below 220 Dobson Units.

The crisis developed because durable industrial chemicals carried chlorine and bromine into the stratosphere. There, sunlight and cold polar conditions helped release reactive forms that could destroy ozone repeatedly. Chlorofluorocarbons were useful in refrigeration, foams, solvents and aerosols precisely because they seemed stable near the surface. Their stability was the route to the problem above it.

Countries signed the Montreal Protocol in 1987; it entered into force in 1989. The [UNEP Ozone Secretariat](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol) describes a global agreement to phase out the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances. Parties meet annually, and the agreement has been adjusted or amended six times. The 2016 Kigali Amendment added a phase-down of powerful greenhouse gases used as replacements, even though those HFCs do not themselves deplete ozone.

## EVIDENCE

The strongest evidence is chemical, observational and modelled. The [2022 WMO/UNEP scientific assessment hosted by NOAA](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/) drew on hundreds of international experts. Its executive summary reports that atmospheric abundances of long-lived ozone-depleting substances continued to fall, including total tropospheric chlorine and bromine.

The assessment finds progress in upper-stratospheric ozone and recovery in Antarctic total-column ozone, while stressing large year-to-year variation. Outside Antarctica, evidence of total-column recovery since 1996 remains limited and is rated low confidence. Lower-stratospheric ozone outside the polar regions has not shown the recovery that models simulate. A success story can contain unresolved chapters.

Under current controls, total-column ozone is expected to return to 1980 values around 2040 for the near-global average, around 2045 in the Arctic and around 2066 in the Antarctic. These are conditional projections, not appointments in a celestial diary.

[NASA Ozone Watch](https://ozonewatch.gsfc.nasa.gov/) supplies the visual antidote to oversimplification. Its annual records show fluctuations sitting on a multi-decade trend. The 2026 season was still developing when the site was updated on 9 September, so it cannot yet be used as a verdict on the year.

## PERSPECTIVES

The cheerful version says humanity solved the ozone hole. It captures something important: governments accepted a measurable target, industry changed technologies, developing countries received time and support, and controls tightened as science improved. Few global environmental agreements have produced such a legible chain from policy to falling atmospheric concentrations.

The sceptical version points to each large annual hole as evidence that recovery was exaggerated. That confuses weather with climate’s atmospheric cousin. Polar temperature, sunlight, circulation, wildfires and volcanic material can change a season’s size and duration. A large year does not cancel a declining chemical burden; a small year does not prove the job complete.

The more accurate view is patient and demanding. In 2025, the Antarctic hole was the smallest and shortest-lived since 2019 according to Europe’s Copernicus service, reaching a maximum area of about 21 million square kilometres, compared with 26 million in 2023. [The Guardian’s report](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2025/dec/01/hole-in-ozone-layer-antarctica-smallest-since-2019-scientists-copernicus-eu) also noted that NASA and NOAA ranked it the fifth smallest since 1992. That is encouraging. The sequence of larger holes from 2020 to 2023 is a reminder not to turn encouragement into a straight line.

## CONTEXT

The important dates form a chain rather than a single heroic moment.

In the 1970s, scientists worked out that long-lived CFCs could reach the stratosphere and release ozone-destroying chlorine. In 1985, British Antarctic observations revealed a severe seasonal loss that was larger and faster than many had expected. The Vienna Convention that year created a framework for cooperation; the Montreal Protocol in 1987 supplied controls.

The original bargain was not frozen. The treaty’s parties reviewed science and technology, added chemicals, accelerated schedules and built a financial mechanism to help countries meet obligations. That capacity to learn mattered. A weaker agreement treated as sacred text could have preserved consensus while preserving the damage.

What failed from earlier expectations was the idea that useful, apparently inert chemicals were harmless once released. Later, some replacements solved the ozone problem but carried a climate cost, prompting the Kigali Amendment. Another failed expectation was that treaty compliance could be assumed once production shifted. Unexpected CFC-11 emissions detected last decade triggered scientific investigation and a policy response; the 2022 assessment says emissions fell, but gaps remain and not all unexplained sources are understood.

What did not change is the underlying chemistry. Chlorine and bromine compounds can survive for decades, so the atmosphere responds slowly even after factories change. The lag between action and recovery is not evidence that action failed. It is the bill presented by long-lived molecules.

## PEOPLE

The Montreal Protocol is often remembered as a rare afternoon when “the world” agreed. In practice, its achievement belongs to people who kept returning to less cinematic work: technicians replacing refrigerants, customs officers identifying controlled chemicals, governments reporting production, engineers redesigning equipment and scientists maintaining instruments in difficult places.

It also depended on fairness. Countries did not begin with the same industries, money or access to alternatives. Different timetables and financial support made participation more than a moral demand from richer states. Cooperation held because the agreement gave governments reasons and means to keep it.

For households, the victory became almost invisible. Refrigerators still cooled, inhalers still worked and spray cans still sprayed, but the chemistry behind them changed. Successful prevention often leaves no monument except an ordinary life in which the feared damage did not arrive.

## DEEPER

The ozone story offers a useful test for claims about collective action. A good-news headline is strongest when four links can be observed: the harmful activity declines; the relevant pollutant falls; the physical system responds; and institutions remain capable of detecting reversal. Here, controlled substances have been phased down, atmospheric chlorine and bromine are declining, ozone shows signs of recovery, and global measurements continue to test the result.

Each link still needs maintenance. The assessment identifies emissions from old chemical banks, feedstocks and by-products; unexplained emissions of several substances; growth in short-lived chlorine compounds; and regional monitoring gaps. Several satellite instruments that provide vertically resolved measurements are also approaching retirement without assured replacements. A treaty cannot respond to what nobody measures.

There is a broader lesson, but not a simplistic template for every problem. Ozone-depleting chemicals came from a limited set of industrial uses, alternatives could be developed, and the harm had an unusually clear chemical fingerprint. Carbon dioxide is woven much more deeply into energy, transport, buildings and land use. The Montreal Protocol proves that international cooperation can alter the atmosphere. It does not prove that every atmospheric problem is equally easy.

Its most hopeful feature may be less glamorous: success was treated as a process, not a declaration. Science revised the rules; finance widened participation; monitoring exposed anomalies; patience allowed slow chemistry to answer. The ozone layer is healing because the agreement did not merely happen. It kept working.

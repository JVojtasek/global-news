## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Family photographs often survive for decades in the wrong box, then lose their names, context or only digital copy.

**What it means.** Preservation has two jobs: slow physical damage and keep the story attached to the image. A perfect scan of an unidentified person is still an incomplete record.

**Risks and impact.** Heat, damp, light, adhesives and rough handling can damage prints. Hard-drive failure, account loss and poor file names can make digital copies disappear just as effectively.

**What can be done.** Start with one small box. Move it to a stable room, photograph its original order, label what is known, scan without forcing fragile items, and make three copies of the files.

**What to watch.** Stop if photographs are stuck together, mouldy, badly curled, flaking or giving off a strong vinegar smell; those are reasons to seek conservation advice, not to pull harder.

## FACTS

The simplest preservation step costs nothing: take family photographs out of the attic, basement or garage. The [Smithsonian Institution Archives](https://siarchives.si.edu/blog/six-tips-preserving-family-archives) warns that rapidly changing temperature and moisture accelerate deterioration. It recommends a stable, lived-in part of the home, away from sunlight and water.

Physical protection comes next. The [Canadian Conservation Institute](https://www.canada.ca/en/conservation-institute/services/preventive-conservation/guidelines-collections/photographic-materials.html) advises handling photographs as little as possible, supporting large or fragile prints on rigid board and housing suitable items in chemically stable folders, boxes or polyester and polyethylene sleeves. A sleeve is not automatically safe: static can lift loose hand-colouring or damaged emulsion.

Digitization creates access, not immortality. The [U.S. National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/preservation/family-archives/digitizing) says to keep originals after scanning because digital files can be lost. Its practical guidance includes using a flatbed only when the whole item fits, avoiding automatic feeders for fragile papers, adding basic who-what-where-when metadata and following the 3-2-1 backup rule: three copies, on two kinds of media, with one stored elsewhere.

## EVIDENCE

Preservation authorities agree on the main hazards even though family collections contain many photographic processes. Heat accelerates chemical decay. Moisture encourages mould and can soften gelatin layers until photographs stick to nearby surfaces. Light damage accumulates and cannot be reversed. Fingers, tape and stiff album pages add mechanical or chemical harm.

They also agree that prevention is safer than amateur repair. The National Archives frames preservation as avoiding damage in the first place. The Canadian guidance recommends sleeves and boxes for stable material but adds exceptions for fragile surfaces. Smithsonian advice says bonded photographs may be safer left in an old album than forcibly removed. “Archival” is therefore not a magic adjective; the material and the photograph must be compatible.

The evidence is strongest for these mechanisms and professional handling practices, not for one perfect home temperature or one universal scanning resolution. Photographs may be paper prints, glass plates, nitrate or acetate negatives, colour dyes or newer digital prints. Their vulnerabilities differ. A household plan should therefore aim for a cool, dry, stable environment and reversible actions rather than imitating a museum vault with false precision.

## PERSPECTIVES

One approach treats the project as scanning: buy a device, feed in the pile and finish with a folder of images. It wins on speed and sharing. It fails when an automatic feeder tears a weak print, when the file names become a row of meaningless numbers, or when the single drive dies. The scanner solves access, not custody.

A second approach treats every print as untouchable. It protects against reckless cleaning and forced removal, but it can leave the collection inaccessible and undocumented. If the only person who recognises “Aunt May at the old shop” is alive now, cautious handling and a recorded conversation may preserve more than another decade of perfect darkness.

The useful middle is a family archive, not a restoration laboratory. Make low-risk improvements first. Preserve original order before rearranging. Record uncertainty instead of guessing. Create working copies for relatives while keeping originals protected. For valuable, mouldy, stuck or chemically unstable material, the practical decision is to pause and consult a conservator.

## CONTEXT

A family photograph carries at least three layers. The image shows faces and places. The physical object may carry a studio stamp, handwriting, cropping or an album sequence. The surrounding memory explains why the moment mattered. Scanning captures the first layer well, the second only if both sides and the album context are recorded, and the third not at all unless somebody writes or speaks it.

That is why a soft pencil can matter more than an expensive scanner. Smithsonian archivists recommend recording full names, nicknames, dates or date ranges and locations. They suggest writing lightly near the back edge when the material is sound, or numbering items and keeping descriptions on a separate sheet. Pens can bleed; pressure can leave an impression.

Original arrangement is evidence too. A developer’s envelope may connect negatives to prints. An album page may place a wedding beside a journey or a new house. Photograph the box, envelope and page before separating anything. Keep doubtful groups together until their relationship is understood.

Digital order needs the same care. Use consistent names such as `1978-06-family-picnic-001-front.tif` and `...-back.tif`, then keep a simple index with names, place, source and uncertainty. The filename does not have to contain the entire story; it has to point reliably to it.

## DEEPER

Here is a safe one-hour rescue, deliberately smaller than “organise all the family photos.”

First, choose one envelope or a group of no more than 25 items. Photograph how it arrived. Do not clean with liquid, peel apart stuck prints or remove photographs that resist an album page.

Second, move the group to a stable room away from direct light, pipes and the floor. Use a clean table and support fragile pieces from underneath. If mould is active, isolate the item and get advice before spreading spores through the collection.

Third, capture knowledge. Ask one person to identify names, relationships, place and approximate date. Record “possibly” and “about 1965” honestly. An uncertain label is more useful than a confident invention.

Fourth, scan or photograph the front and any meaningful back. Keep the entire object in frame. Do not force a bound album flat, and do not put fragile material through an automatic feeder. Save a high-quality master and a smaller sharing copy.

Fifth, make the digital archive survive you. Keep three copies on two types of storage, with one copy in another location. Open a sample from each copy. A backup that has never been read is only a promise.

Then stop. Return next week if the system still makes sense. Family archives are often damaged by two kinds of haste: the hurry that throws context away and the grand project that becomes so large nobody begins. Preservation is quieter. It turns one anonymous box into a chain of custody between people who may never meet.

## REFLECT

Which photograph in your family would become meaningless if one living person could no longer name the people in it?

Are you preserving only the image, or also the handwriting, sequence and story that tell a future viewer what they are seeing?

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** A 2024 systematic review found that homework can improve primary pupils' academic performance, especially some arithmetic skills, but the best daily duration remains unknown and much of the experimental evidence is old.

**What it means.** “Does homework work?” is too broad. Age, subject, task design, independence and what the teacher does with the completed work all change the answer.

**Risks and impact.** Extra time can become mental fatigue, family conflict or hidden parental labour. Unequal access to quiet space, devices and adult help can also turn one assignment into very different tasks.

**What can be done.** Parents can log actual time and sticking points, encourage a genuine attempt, and ask the teacher about purpose and feedback without becoming the child's second teacher.

**What to watch.** Notice whether homework rehearses something taught, can be started independently, ends within the intended time and produces useful feedback. A growing pile is not evidence of growing learning.

## FACTS

The most recent systematic review in this article searched for experimental evidence on homework time and achievement across K–12 education. It found only 11 eligible publications. Ten articles produced 14 independent comparisons of homework with no homework, and the pooled result favoured homework. Benefits were clearest in arithmetic computation and arithmetic problem-solving, not arithmetic concepts.

That encouraging result comes with a large label marked “handle carefully.” Only one included study was recent, from 2021; the rest dated from 1939 to 1995. Just two experiments compared different homework durations. The review concluded that homework can supplement primary education, while also stating that the optimal amount of time is unknown.

The Education Endowment Foundation's broader toolkit reports a positive average impact, larger in secondary than primary education. It rates the evidence security as low. The strongest patterns concern quality: assignments linked to classroom learning, with a clear purpose and high-quality feedback, tend to perform better than homework treated as an add-on.

## EVIDENCE

Homework can work through an ordinary learning mechanism. Retrieving a method from memory and practising it strengthens access to that method. A short set of problems can show both pupil and teacher where understanding has failed. Feedback then closes the loop: the child learns whether the method was sound, not merely whether a box was filled.

The same mechanism explains why quantity can fail. Repetition after a skill is secure offers diminishing returns. Repetition before the child understands the method can rehearse mistakes. Mental fatigue reduces attention, and a long struggle may measure persistence or household support more than subject knowledge.

The 2006 research synthesis found generally positive evidence but design flaws across every study type it reviewed. Simple homework–achievement correlations were stronger in grades 7–12 than K–6 and changed depending on whether pupils or parents reported time. Correlation also leaves a stubborn question: do pupils achieve more because they do homework, or do pupils who are already more organised and successful complete it more reliably?

## PERSPECTIVES

**The practice case** is straightforward. Children do not become fluent at multiplication, spelling or reading only by understanding an explanation once. Purposeful, manageable practice can free attention for harder work later. The 2024 Campbell review gives this view experimental support, particularly in primary mathematics.

**The childhood-time case** asks what the assignment displaces. A worksheet is not free simply because no school budget records the cost. It uses time that might otherwise go to sleep, play, movement, reading for pleasure or family life. The review could not identify an optimal duration, so a precise universal minutes-per-grade rule carries more confidence than the experimental base warrants.

**The teacher's case** focuses on information. Homework that returns full of answers supplied or corrected by adults tells the teacher very little. A partial attempt with a visible sticking point can be more useful because it reveals what needs reteaching.

**The equity case** notes that homes do not offer equal working conditions. OECD data on 15-year-olds—not primary pupils—found that advantaged students tended to spend more time on homework. The EEF likewise warns about differences in quiet space, suitable devices, internet access and adult support. That does not prove homework causes inequality, but it does show why identical assignments can impose unequal burdens.

## CONTEXT

Arguments about homework often turn a design problem into a moral referendum. One side treats the completed page as evidence of discipline; the other treats its abolition as evidence that a school respects childhood. Neither position tells us whether a particular task helps a particular child learn something.

The useful unit is the assignment. Is it practising a skill already taught, preparing for a lesson, or asking a child to discover new material alone? Can the pupil understand the instructions and begin without an adult translating them? Does the teacher see the errors and respond? Is the intended duration remotely close to the actual one?

Age matters because independence changes. A teenager revising for an exam and a seven-year-old learning a new written method are not doing the same cognitive or family task. Subject matters too: brief retrieval practice behaves differently from an open-ended project that quietly requires transport, printing, craft supplies and parental project management.

This is why headline effect sizes should not be read as promises. The EEF's “months of progress” figures are comparative estimates across studies, not a clock that awards every child three extra months for carrying a worksheet home.

## DEEPER

Parents need not choose between enforcing every task and declaring homework useless. A better approach is to protect the evidence in the child's work.

For one week, record four things: when the child started, when they stopped, what they could do alone and the first point where they became stuck. Do not turn the record into surveillance or a score. Its value is diagnostic. “Tuesday's sheet took 47 minutes and the instructions required three explanations” gives a teacher more to work with than “homework is always a nightmare.”

Help with conditions before content: a place to work, the necessary materials, a short plan and a clear stopping point. If the child asks what an instruction means, clarify the language without solving the intellectual step. If the school uses a method you do not recognise, resist teaching a competing one from memory. Write a note or encourage the child to mark the problem for discussion.

Four questions can improve the conversation with the teacher:

1. What exact skill is this assignment practising?
2. How long do you expect it to take?
3. What should the child do after a genuine attempt gets stuck?
4. How will errors inform the next lesson?

The practical threshold is not one universal number. It is a repeated mismatch: work routinely takes far longer than intended, requires sustained adult teaching, causes missed sleep or returns without feedback. That pattern deserves adjustment because it suggests the task is no longer measuring independent practice.

## PEOPLE

Homework sits at an awkward border between school and home. Teachers may see ten minutes of consolidation. A parent may see the forty-minute negotiation that precedes it. A child may see the moment an already tiring school day follows them to the kitchen table.

All three views can be true. The aim is not to decide who is insufficiently committed. It is to make the task legible. Children benefit when adults agree that struggle can be information rather than disobedience, that an honest incomplete attempt may teach more than a polished parental rescue, and that the teacher remains responsible for teaching the method.

## REFLECT

If the purpose of homework is learning, which result should worry us more: an unfinished page that reveals confusion, or a perfect page that conceals who actually did the thinking?

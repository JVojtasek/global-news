## BRIEFLY

- Meaningful work is not the same thing as a dream job, constant enjoyment or a single life calling.
- Research separates at least three elements: significance in the work itself, meaning made through work, and contribution to a greater good.
- In a three-wave study of Chilean workers, autonomy and beneficence—the sense that one has real choice and helps others—predicted later meaningfulness more clearly than competence or relatedness did.
- A meta-analysis of 44 articles and 23,144 participants found strong associations with engagement, commitment and job satisfaction, but association is not a promise that meaning cures a bad workplace.
- Calling can be "binding and ennobling": it may deepen commitment while also making low pay, lost time and discomfort easier to accept.

## FACTS

The phrase *meaningful work* often arrives carrying a cinematic burden. It suggests a revelation, a perfect fit and perhaps a resignation letter delivered before lunch. The research is less theatrical. Michael Steger and colleagues' Work and Meaning Inventory treats meaningful work as three related experiences: the work feels significant, it helps a person make sense of life, and it contributes to something beyond the self.

That definition leaves room for ordinary jobs and mixed days. A payroll clerk can protect colleagues from avoidable chaos. A cleaner can make a hospital safer. A manager can give people enough discretion to solve a problem properly. None has to declare the occupation a destiny.

The distinction matters because "Do I love this job?" is an unstable test. Enjoyment varies with sleep, workload, colleagues and Tuesday afternoon. A better question is whether the work contains repeatable sources of value—and whether its costs remain tolerable.

## EVIDENCE

The strongest useful clue comes from a 2021 three-wave study led by Frank Martela. Researchers followed Chilean workers, beginning with 631 participants; 240 took part in the second wave and 148 in the third. They tested four possible routes to meaningfulness: autonomy, competence, relatedness and beneficence. Autonomy and beneficence predicted later meaningful work even after accounting for baseline meaningfulness and the other needs.

That does not prove that two office policies will transform every job. Attrition reduced the later samples, and the study concerned one national workforce. It does improve the mechanism: work is more likely to matter when people have some authorship over it and can see who benefits.

A 2018 meta-analysis pooled 44 articles with 23,144 participants. Meaningful work correlated strongly—at 0.70 or above—with engagement, commitment and job satisfaction. Links with life satisfaction, life meaning, general health and intentions to withdraw were moderate to large. Most underlying evidence cannot fully settle direction: healthier, more engaged people may also find meaning more readily.

## PERSPECTIVES

There are two bad simplifications here. The first belongs to the motivational poster: find your calling and work will stop feeling like work. The second belongs to the cynic: a salary is the only honest meaning a job can have. Real lives occupy the broad country between them.

For an employee, meaning may come from craft, service, comradeship or a problem that is worth solving. For an employer, those sources cannot substitute for pay, safety, manageable demands or fair treatment. Telling exhausted staff that their mission is noble can be a way of declining to fix the rota.

The warning is visible in J. Stuart Bunderson and Jeffery Thompson's study of zookeepers. Interviews and a survey spanning 157 zoos in the United States and Canada found that workers who experienced zookeeping as a calling gained identity and significance. They were also more likely to treat it as a moral duty and sacrifice pay, personal time and comfort. Deep meaning was not fake; that was precisely why it could be used against them—or why they might use it against themselves.

Managers therefore have two different jobs. One is to connect tasks to people and outcomes. The other is to keep that connection from becoming emotional collateral for poor conditions. A mission statement cannot perform either job by itself.

## CONTEXT

Older career stories assumed a relatively stable ladder: enter an occupation, accumulate expertise, rise and retire. Many workers now move between employers, contracts, caring periods and fields. Under those conditions, demanding one permanent calling can turn normal adaptation into a personal failure.

Meaning is more portable if it is described as a pattern rather than a title. Someone may repeatedly value making complicated things understandable, repairing what others discard, reducing fear, building reliable systems or helping younger colleagues grow. Those themes can survive a change of employer—and sometimes a change of occupation.

They can also live outside paid work. Family care, friendship, citizenship, art and voluntary activity are legitimate homes for purpose. Expecting employment to supply income, identity, community, moral worth, creative expression and self-transcendence is asking one institution to carry the furniture of an entire life.

This wider view is not an argument for indifference at work. It is protection against monopoly. A job can be important without being allowed to define the whole person. When work changes or disappears, other sources of meaning keep the loss from becoming total.

It also helps to separate the meaning **of** work from meaning **at** work. The first asks whether the activity serves a worthwhile end. The second includes relationships, routines and moments that make a workplace human even when the product itself is unremarkable. A warehouse shift may not express a personal philosophy, yet mutual reliability among colleagues can still matter. Conversely, an admired public mission can coexist with humiliating management. Asking both questions prevents the organisation's purpose from swallowing the employee's experience.

There is a temporal distinction too. Some work feels meaningful while it is happening; some gains significance only when a project is finished or a beneficiary responds. A dull hour can belong to a worthwhile whole. But endlessly postponing meaning—"after this quarter," "after the promotion"—deserves suspicion. The pattern, not the exceptional peak, is what should guide the audit.

## DEEPER

A practical audit begins with evidence, not inspiration. Choose one recent working week and make four short lists.

First, note the moments of **authorship**: where could you decide the method, sequence, wording or standard? Do not count cosmetic choice. A menu of two equally impossible deadlines is not autonomy.

Second, trace **benefit**. Write the name or role of the person who was better off because the work was completed: a customer who avoided confusion, a colleague who could proceed, a patient whose record was accurate. If the chain is invisible, ask for feedback or follow the output one step farther.

Third, identify **craft**. What became slightly better because you exercised judgement rather than merely endured time? Progress does not have to be grand, but it should be observable.

Fourth, record **cost**: unpaid hours, physical strain, moral discomfort, interruptions, sleep and relationships displaced. Meaning and cost belong on the same page. A job that scores highly on contribution and badly on sustainable conditions presents a bargaining or design problem, not a character test.

Then choose one experiment small enough to run for two weeks. Ask to own a task from beginning to end. Arrange one conversation with the person who uses your output. Reduce one recurring piece of avoidable rework. Protect one boundary that prevents the job consuming the rest of life. The result may be "this can improve," "this cannot improve here," or simply "this is decent work that funds meaning elsewhere." All three are adult answers.

## PEOPLE

The language of calling tends to flatter those who can afford risk. A person with savings, secure housing and no dependent care may experience a lower-paid vocation differently from someone whose household needs every reliable hour. The same occupation can contain autonomy for a senior professional and rigid surveillance for a contractor.

That is why meaningful-work advice should never become an instruction to quit, accept less or feel grateful. Structural constraints are real. The useful promise is smaller: naming the ingredients helps people distinguish a temporary dull patch from a role whose design consistently blocks agency, contribution or dignity.

## PRACTICAL TAKEAWAY

Do not ask whether your job is "the one." For two weeks, track authorship, visible benefit, craft and cost. Try one reversible change that increases real choice or contact with the beneficiary. If meaning rises only when your boundaries collapse, the job has not passed the test.

**Which change is best supported as a route toward more meaningful work?**

A. Wait until the job feels like a lifelong calling.

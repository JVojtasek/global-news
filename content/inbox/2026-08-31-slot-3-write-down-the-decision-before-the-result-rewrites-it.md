## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Decades of psychology research show that once people know how a story ended, they tend to remember that ending as more predictable than it really was.

**What it means.** We often judge a decision by its result, even though a careful choice can go badly and a careless one can get lucky.

**Risks and impact.** This quietly corrupts experience. We reward luck, punish reasonable uncertainty and learn rules that may fail the next time conditions change.

**What can be done.** Before a consequential choice, spend ten minutes recording the options, assumptions, probabilities, unknowns and the evidence that would change your mind.

**What to watch.** When you review the choice, score the process before looking at the result. A decision journal is a memory aid, not a scientific instrument or a courtroom exhibit.

## FACTS

In 1975, psychologist Baruch Fischhoff reported three experiments involving 479 college students. Participants read historical accounts and, in some conditions, were told how events had turned out. Knowing an outcome made that outcome seem more likely in retrospect. People also struggled to reconstruct how the situation would have appeared without the answer in hand.

Later researchers separated two related errors. **Hindsight bias** is the feeling that an outcome was foreseeable after it occurs. **Outcome bias** is judging the quality of a decision partly by how it ended, even when the information available to the decision-maker was the same.

A preregistered 2023 replication of a classic outcome-bias experiment found the pattern again: otherwise identical decisions received better ratings when they produced success than when they produced failure. The effect survived even among participants who said outcomes should not influence the judgment.

That distinction matters. Results are evidence about the world. They are not, by themselves, a fair transcript of the reasoning that came before them.

## EVIDENCE

A 2012 review by Neal Roese and Kathleen Vohs describes hindsight bias as a combination of memory distortion, a sense that an event was inevitable and an inflated belief that one foresaw it. Several mechanisms can contribute. People selectively recall facts that now fit the ending, build a coherent story around those facts and mistake the ease of that story for proof that it was obvious all along.

The bias is not destiny. Deliberately considering plausible alternative explanations can reduce it. Yet the review cautions that debiasing is rarely complete. Once the ending is known, simply telling yourself to “ignore it” is a weak remedy.

A contemporaneous note offers a practical advantage: it moves part of the evidence outside memory before memory has a result to organise around. But the limit should be stated plainly. Research establishes hindsight and outcome bias far more firmly than it establishes personal decision journaling as a proven cure. The journal is an evidence-informed audit trail, not a clinically validated treatment for judgment.

## PERSPECTIVES

The strongest case for a decision record is not that it makes people less emotional. It is that it creates a fairer comparison. You can ask whether the original assumptions were reasonable, whether important evidence was ignored and whether the uncertainty range was honest.

There is also a sensible objection: recording everything would turn an ordinary life into a compliance department. The answer is selectivity. Use the method for choices that are costly, repeated or hard to reverse—not for selecting lunch.

Another danger is organisational. A journal designed for learning can become ammunition in a blame culture. If every uncertain forecast is later treated as a broken promise, people will stop writing candidly and start writing defensively. Teams need to distinguish a probability from a guarantee and a bad process from an unlucky draw.

Finally, a written forecast can create false precision. “About 60%” is useful as a statement of confidence; it is not a magic number. The aim is calibrated humility: specific enough to review, modest enough to admit that the future still has elbows. That is a better standard than pretending confidence can abolish chance.

## CONTEXT

Experience is often described as a patient teacher. In reality, it teaches through a noisy marking scheme. A manager approves a risky project and it succeeds; a family chooses a careful route and still encounters trouble; an investor follows a poor thesis into a rising market. If outcome and process are fused, each episode can teach the wrong lesson.

The reverse error is equally costly. After a failure, every warning sign suddenly looks luminous. We forget the evidence pointing the other way, the alternatives that carried different risks and the forecasts that were genuinely uncertain. A coherent retrospective story replaces the untidy decision that actually existed.

This is why useful review needs two passes. First ask, “Given what was reasonably knowable then, was the process sound?” Only afterward ask, “What did the result reveal that should change the next decision?” The first question protects people from hindsight. The second prevents process language from becoming an excuse to ignore reality.

A good decision record preserves that boundary. It lets an unlucky good choice remain unlucky—and still makes room to notice an assumption that failed. Over several reviews, patterns become more informative than any single win or loss: perhaps estimates are consistently overconfident, inconvenient evidence is omitted, or reversible experiments work better than large commitments.

## DEEPER

Use one page and ten minutes. Write it before acting, while uncertainty is still alive.

1. **Name the decision and the review date.** Choose a date when enough evidence should exist to learn something.
2. **List the real options.** Include “wait” or “do nothing” if either is genuinely available.
3. **State the objective.** What are you trying to improve, protect or avoid?
4. **Record three important assumptions.** Give each a rough confidence level or plausible range.
5. **Name the largest unknown.** What information is missing, and why are you deciding without it?
6. **Write the change-of-mind test.** What new fact would make another option preferable?
7. **Choose the smallest reversible step.** When possible, buy information before buying commitment.

At review time, cover the outcome and grade the process first: evidence gathered, alternatives considered, incentives checked and uncertainty stated. Then uncover the result. Ask which assumption moved and update one decision rule—not your entire philosophy of life.

The practical payoff is not a folder full of correct predictions. It is a more honest loop between judgment and learning.

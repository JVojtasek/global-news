## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Before a child makes a familiar trip alone, a parent must decide whether the child, the route and the day's conditions are ready together.

**What it means.** There is no universal birthday for independent travel. Readiness is better shown through repeated practice: noticing traffic, choosing crossings, recovering from a wrong turn and following an agreed check-in plan.

**Risks and impact.** Granting freedom too quickly can expose an unpractised child to avoidable danger; delaying every step can also deny practice in judgment, navigation and confidence.

**What can be done.** Walk the route in stages, let the child lead, rehearse disruptions and increase distance only after observable skills become consistent.

**What to watch.** Judge the hardest decision on the route—not its average easy minute—and reassess after construction, winter darkness, a timetable change or a new distraction.

## FACTS

The final practice journey contains a revealing decision. The adult can keep giving directions, making the walk look smooth, or fall half a step behind and discover what the child notices without prompting.

Independent mobility is the research term for a child's freedom to travel or play without adult supervision. It includes different “licences”: crossing a main road, walking home from school, visiting a nearby place or using public transport. Those are not one skill and need not arrive together.

The American Academy of Pediatrics tells parents of five-year-olds that children are not ready to cross streets alone until age ten or older. That is a broad safety boundary for young children, not a certificate that every ten-year-old can manage every road.

The UK government's THINK! campaign focuses on the transition to secondary school. It asks families to make practice journeys and teaches three concrete habits: eyes up, slow down, cross safely. Its cited casualty data show child pedestrian casualties doubling between ages nine and eleven, when many children begin travelling more independently.

Age matters. The route and demonstrated behaviour matter too.

## EVIDENCE

A 2022 systematic review examined 53 studies of independent mobility among people aged five to seventeen. Factors consistently associated with greater freedom included age, children's perceived competence, parents' attitudes and safety perceptions, siblings, access to destinations, traffic concerns and features of the built environment. The review did not reveal a single readiness score.

It also found that independently mobile children tend to accumulate more physical activity and may gain environmental knowledge, peer interaction, wayfinding and decision-making practice. Most studies were observational, however. Families and neighbourhoods that permit mobility differ from those that do not, so association is not proof that solo travel alone caused every benefit.

A 2026 qualitative study interviewed 18 parents of seven- to twelve-year-olds in southern Alberta. Parents described weighing the child's maturity and traffic skills against distance, heavy traffic, social norms and infrastructure. Parental confidence appeared important. The sample was small and unusually highly educated, so it offers detailed mechanisms rather than a population estimate.

The strongest evidence supports a system view: child, adult, route and community interact. It does not support a universal age, app or checklist that makes risk disappear.

## PERSPECTIVES

One parent may frame solo travel as a danger to postpone. Traffic speed, poor crossings and harassment are not imagined, and an adult should not use a child to test an unsafe street design. The weakness in this frame appears when every uncertainty becomes proof that no practice is possible.

Another may frame independence as something childhood now lacks. Research does document a long decline in independent mobility in several countries and plausible benefits from movement and navigation. But nostalgia can hide the differences between a quiet route remembered from decades ago and a present route with heavier vehicles or fewer familiar adults nearby.

Technology offers a third frame: the phone as safety. A child can call, receive a changed plan or share a location. A phone can also distract at the exact moment attention is needed, lose power or create false confidence in a signal that updates late. THINK! explicitly identifies friends and mobile distraction as a pedestrian risk.

The useful middle is staged freedom. It treats safety as a skill-and-environment problem, not a choice between surveillance and a sudden leap.

## CONTEXT

Readiness is route-specific because routes contain decisions. A ten-minute walk along a separated path with one signalised crossing is not equivalent to five minutes across a fast junction hidden by parked vehicles. Daylight and winter darkness can turn the same map into a different task.

Children also develop unevenly. One remembers landmarks but rushes when friends appear. Another follows rules yet freezes when a bus stop changes. Disability, sensory needs, local law and access to transport can change the appropriate training without lowering the child's claim to growing independence.

Parents often look for reassurance in the child's promise: “I know.” Practice replaces that weak signal with observable behaviour. Can the child explain why a crossing is safer, resist following a friend into the road, name a trusted place for help and return to the plan after a disruption?

No rehearsal can cover every rare event. Its job is to reveal the routine thinking underneath confidence and to create a simple response when the routine breaks.

## PEOPLE

The child is not the only person being trained. The adult must learn to distinguish discomfort from evidence. Watching a child pause at a crossing can feel slower and less safe than taking over, even when the pause is exactly the judgment the adult hoped to teach.

The first solo journey also affects siblings, school staff, neighbours and separated caregivers. Everyone needs the same route, timing and fallback plan. A child should not carry the burden of reconciling contradictory instructions in traffic.

For some families the safer answer will remain accompaniment, specialised travel training or a different route. Independence is not a competition between households. The meaningful question is whether the next step is both genuinely developmental and proportionate to the real environment.

## DEEPER

Use a five-stage route rehearsal.

First, choose one familiar destination and map the safest practical route, not merely the shortest. Identify the hardest crossing, places where visibility is blocked and one staffed place where the child could ask for help.

Second, travel together while narrating decisions. Explain why you wait, where you look and what would make you abandon the crossing. Keep emergency language calm and concrete.

Third, let the child lead while you observe. Ask questions after the decision rather than supplying every answer before it. Repeat at the real time of day and, where relevant, in different weather.

Fourth, rehearse ordinary disruptions: the usual crossing is closed, the bus does not arrive, a phone battery dies, a friend proposes a shortcut. Agree on a small number of rules, including when to return home and whom to contact. Never ask a child to approach traffic or strangers to make the rehearsal realistic.

Fifth, grant a limited licence. That might be the final block alone, walking with a sibling, or one complete daylight route with a check-in at arrival. Review what happened without turning every imperfection into a trial.

Freedom grows best when the child can see what earned the next step. “Because I said so” is easy to administer. A visible ladder of skill is harder—and far more transferable.

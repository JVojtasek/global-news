## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Extreme heat is becoming a normal planning problem across many popular destinations, not an exceptional footnote in the forecast.

**What it means.** A holiday can be unsafe even when every booking works perfectly. The important question is how much heat exposure the itinerary creates, especially during long walks, queues and nights without effective cooling.

**Risks and impact.** Older people, young children, pregnant travellers and people with some illnesses or medicines can have less room for error. Anyone exercising hard before becoming acclimatised can be affected.

**What can be done.** Audit the day before leaving: move strenuous outdoor time, check shade and transport, confirm that the room can cool at night, and know where local medical help is available.

**What to watch.** Follow the destination's official heat warnings and overnight forecast, then change the plan if the promised cooling, transport or shade is no longer there.

## FACTS

The temperature printed beside a sun icon is only one part of the problem. The body loses heat through radiation, moving air and the evaporation of sweat. According to the [CDC Yellow Book](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/environmental-hazards-risks/heat-and-cold-illness-in-travelers.html), those routes become much less effective when air temperature is above 35°C and humidity is high. Exercise creates additional heat from inside the body.

The same guidance says that dehydration is an important contributor to heat illness, but it also warns against forcing excessive water intake. Risk is affected by exertion, acclimatisation, cardiovascular health, diabetes, kidney disease, age, body size, infection and some medicines.

Cities add another layer. Roads and buildings absorb energy and release it later, helping an urban heat island remain warmer after the sun goes down. The [ECMWF and Copernicus explanation](https://stories.ecmwf.int/urban-heat-islands-and-heat-mortality/index.html) describes how built surfaces, limited vegetation and human activity can intensify heat.

Confusion, loss of coordination, collapse or seizures in a hot person are emergency signs. Heat stroke is a medical emergency; local emergency services should be contacted while cooling begins.

## EVIDENCE

The four sources agree on the part that matters most to a traveller: exposure can often be reduced before the body has to cope with it. The [WHO heat-and-health guidance](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/climate-change-heat-and-health) emphasises heat action plans, warnings, cooling and protection of vulnerable people. [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheets/extreme-heat) similarly advises travellers to check destination conditions, limit strenuous activity and plan for people whose health increases their risk.

The CDC provides the clearest physiological account. It says that most heat-acclimatisation changes occur within about ten days when people exercise daily in the heat, and advises travellers without prior acclimatisation to reduce exercise intensity and duration during their first week. That is a useful warning against treating arrival day as a fitness test.

What the evidence does not provide is a single temperature at which every trip becomes dangerous. A shaded visit with transport, rest and effective cooling is not the same exposure as a two-hour uphill walk over stone streets. Forecast temperature, humidity, radiant heat, wind, duration and the traveller all interact. The honest unit of planning is therefore not simply the city. It is the person, place, activity and time together.

## PERSPECTIVES

There are three common ways to frame a holiday heatwave, and each sees only part of the picture.

The first says: cancel. That may be sensible when official warnings are severe, essential services are strained or a traveller is particularly vulnerable. It correctly treats heat as a health hazard. But it can flatten a whole destination into one daily maximum and ignore the possibility of moving exposure safely.

The second says: locals manage, so visitors can too. It notices something real—buildings, routines and public life in hot places often evolved around the climate. Yet visitors may arrive without acclimatisation, choose hotels designed more for photographs than ventilation, and schedule midday walking because a booking app offered a slot.

The third says: drink water and carry on. Hydration matters, but this frame turns an environmental problem into a bottle problem. Water cannot create shade, shorten a queue or cool a bedroom. The CDC also cautions that forced over-drinking during prolonged exertion can contribute to dangerously low blood sodium.

A better frame is quieter: preserve the purpose of the trip while redesigning its heat load. Keep the museum, dinner and neighbourhood. Move the hill, the exposed ruins and the long transfer. A holiday is not defeated because lunch becomes the main event at 1 p.m.

## CONTEXT

Travel planning was built around distance. How far is the hotel from the station? How many landmarks fit between breakfast and dinner? Heat changes the geometry. Five shaded kilometres with frequent transport may be easier than one exposed kilometre across a square at noon.

Start the audit at night, not midday. Check the overnight minimum and the room, not just whether the listing says “air-conditioned.” Is cooling available throughout the night? Is it controlled in the room? Do recent reviews mention failure during hot weather? A body that cannot shed heat overnight begins the next day with less recovery.

Then mark the exposure you cannot quickly escape: airport aprons, outdoor queues, steep streets, open archaeological sites, theme parks and remote trails. Put these in the coolest workable hours. Keep indoor sights, shaded meals and short transport links for the hotter part of the day. Confirm opening hours before building the plan; a beautifully cool dawn visit is useless if the gate opens at eleven.

Next, identify the traveller with the narrowest safety margin. That is not a label of weakness. It is ordinary logistics, like choosing a route everyone can walk. People taking medicines should ask a clinician or pharmacist before travel whether heat, dehydration or storage temperature changes anything; they should not stop or alter treatment from an internet article.

Finally, build an exit. Save the local emergency number. Pin an air-conditioned public place. Know which outdoor booking can be changed. The best itinerary is not the fullest one. It is the one that still works when the afternoon is hotter than advertised.

## PEOPLE

Heat changes a family trip unevenly. One person may still be discussing the next landmark while another has stopped sweating efficiently, become dizzy or started answering strangely. A group that waits for everyone to describe the same discomfort can miss the person who needs help first.

Children generate heat while running and may not recognise the warning early. Older travellers may have chronic conditions or medicines that affect heat tolerance. A fit adult can also become seriously ill during hard exertion, especially soon after arriving from a cooler climate.

This is why the plan should be agreed before the pavement begins to shimmer. Decide that anyone can call for shade, transport or a return to the hotel without having to win a debate. Decide who carries the address and emergency information. The point is not to make the most cautious person responsible for spoiling the day. It is to stop pride, sunk costs and a non-refundable ticket from making a health decision.

## DEEPER

Travel often turns time into a scarce commodity. We have three days, so we must see twelve things. The schedule becomes a small moral test: a good traveller is energetic, adaptable and never defeated by weather.

But weather is not an opponent. The old rhythm of hot places—work early, withdraw, return when the light softens—contains a kind of intelligence that an optimised itinerary forgets. It does not surrender the day. It changes the shape of the day.

That distinction matters beyond heat. Good preparedness is not a bunker mentality in miniature. It is the habit of noticing which assumptions hold a plan together. Here, the assumptions may be that the room cools, the bus arrives, the queue is shaded and every member of the group can tolerate the same pace. Once named, those assumptions can be checked.

There is also a gentler definition of value. The afternoon removed from the sightseeing list may become the part people remember: a long lunch, a conversation, a nap behind closed shutters, a walk after dark. Efficiency counts places. Experience notices the day.

The heatwave test is therefore simple. Ask not only, “Can we still do this?” Ask, “What are we trying to preserve?” Usually it is not the timetable. It is curiosity, company and the chance to return home well.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** In Tilonia, Rajasthan, Bunker Roy helped build Barefoot College around a stubborn idea: rural people already possessed knowledge and capacity that conventional institutions routinely ignored.

**What it means.** Its best-known solar programme trains rural women—many with little formal schooling—to assemble, install and maintain small solar systems in their own communities.

**Risks and impact.** Inspiring totals can conceal differences among programmes, funding arrangements and long-term maintenance. A founder's story can also overshadow the women who make the system work.

**What can be done.** When importing technology, budget for local selection, hands-on learning, spare parts, payment and repair—not just installation.

**What to watch.** The meaningful test is not the launch photograph. It is whether systems still work, technicians are paid, replacement parts arrive and new learners can be trained locally.

## FACTS

Sanjit “Bunker” Roy founded the Social Work and Research Centre in Tilonia in 1972. It became known as Barefoot College. The institution challenged a familiar development sequence in which an outside professional diagnoses a village, installs a solution and remains the source of expertise.

The solar programme reverses the direction. Communities select women for practical training. Instruction can use colour-coded components, demonstration and repetition rather than written examinations. The trainees learn to assemble, install, test and maintain solar lighting equipment, then return with both equipment and a role.

UNDP Ghana described five women in their sixties who trained at Barefoot College and helped electrify 150 households in off-grid communities. Its 2023 annual report said more than 3,500 rural women from 93 countries had become “Solar Mamas” and linked the wider small-grants programme to 175,000 installed solar systems. Those are institutional figures, useful for scale but not a substitute for independent evaluation of every site.

## EVIDENCE

The mechanism is more important than the slogan. A 2011 WIRED visit to Tilonia documented trainees using soldering irons, voltmeters and partially assembled lanterns. Because several participants could not rely on text-heavy manuals, components and sequences were identified visually. The report also described a maintenance model in which households contributed money previously spent on kerosene toward spare parts and service.

Fourteen years later, reporting from Zanzibar showed the model adapting rather than merely repeating. The Guardian found a local college training women for three months. Graduates received solar kits for household installation and charged a monthly service fee. The report said 65 women trained since 2015 had connected 1,858 homes in 29 villages. Earlier cohorts trained in India; local graduates were now teaching later ones.

That continuity is evidence of institutional learning, though not proof that every claimed benefit is permanent. The strongest verifiable claim is narrower: people excluded by formal credentials can master specific technical work when teaching, tools and responsibility are designed around practice.

## PERSPECTIVES

### Roy's challenge

Roy argued that poverty programmes often mistake certificates for competence and visitors for experts. His provocation was useful because it forced institutions to notice knowledge already present in communities. Yet the programme still depends on technical standards, finance and partnerships; “bottom-up” does not mean isolated from outside resources.

### The technicians' achievement

The story belongs most concretely to women such as Salamatu, Dakota and Afia in Ghana, and Fatma Haji and Arafa Khamis in Zanzibar. They learned circuits, climbed roofs, taught peers and became the people neighbours called when equipment failed. Calling them beneficiaries understates the change in role.

### The funder's view

Funders are attracted to replicable numbers: trainees, systems and households. Those metrics matter, but they can reward expansion faster than support systems grow. Repair times, technician income, battery replacement and customer retention reveal more about durability.

### The sceptic's view

Charismatic-founder narratives can flatten history and governance. Barefoot College, Barefoot College International and country organisations are not always the same legal or operational entity. The Guardian added a correction clarifying the distinction between the Indian institution and the Zanzibar organisation. Readers should keep those boundaries visible.

## CONTEXT

Development policy has long wrestled with a transfer problem. A technically sound project may fail when its expertise, procurement or authority sits elsewhere. Equipment becomes an orphan: impressive at commissioning, unusable after a small fault. Training a local operator helps, but only if that person has standing, tools, income and a supply route.

Barefoot College addressed several of those constraints together. Communities selected trainees who were likely to remain. Practical instruction lowered the credential barrier. Maintenance became part of the job rather than an afterthought. Women's participation changed who could be recognised as technically capable, especially in places where older rural women had few formal employment routes.

None of this makes small solar systems equivalent to a national grid. They provide limited power and require components that eventually wear out. Their value is specific: lighting, phone charging and other modest uses in places where grid connection is absent or unaffordable. Good news becomes credible when its boundary is clear.

## DEEPER

Roy's lasting contribution is not a single gadget. It is an institutional question: what would education look like if its first task were to reveal usable capability rather than filter people by prior schooling?

The answer in Tilonia was not to pretend theory did not matter. Electricity remains unforgiving. It was to translate technical knowledge into visual, repeated, supervised practice and attach it to real responsibility. Competence was demonstrated in a working circuit, a safe installation and a successful repair.

That design has implications beyond energy. An organisation introducing water sensors, agricultural tools or digital systems can ask four questions. Who will still be here when the vendor leaves? Can that person diagnose a fault? Is there money and authority for maintenance? Can the next learner be trained locally?

Those questions resist the theatre of delivery. A donated object is visible on day one. A maintenance network is slower and less photogenic. It is also what converts an object into a service.

## PRACTICAL IMPACT

For any community technology project, add a “year-two test” before approval. Name the local maintainer, the source and price of common spare parts, the payment route, the fault-reporting channel and the person authorised to train a replacement. If any field is blank, the project is not finished.

## READER OUTCOME

You should be able to explain Barefoot College as a capability-and-maintenance model, not merely a solar donation programme, and judge similar projects by who can operate, repair and teach after outsiders depart.

## REFLECT

Which qualification in your own organisation is acting as a useful safety threshold, and which is merely a familiar gate? The distinction matters because lowering the wrong standard creates risk, while removing an irrelevant barrier reveals talent.

When a project is described as empowering, ask a practical follow-up: who gained authority, income and the ability to fix what breaks?

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Ten human cases of Guinea worm disease were reported worldwide in 2025, the lowest annual total on record.

**What it means.** A parasite estimated to infect 3.5 million people a year in 1986 is now close to becoming the second human disease eradicated after smallpox—and the first removed without a vaccine or curative medicine.

**Risks and impact.** Ten is not zero. The same worm infects dogs and other animals, insecurity can obstruct surveillance, and an infection remains hidden for roughly a year before a worm emerges.

**What can be done.** Endemic communities and health programmes interrupt the cycle with safe water, filters, rapid reporting, case containment, treatment of selected water sources and measures that keep infected people and animals out of them.

**What to watch.** The decisive signal is not one record-low year. It is sustained zero transmission in both people and animals, backed by nationwide surveillance strong enough for independent certification.

## FACTS

The number is so small that it fits around a kitchen table: four reported human cases in Chad, four in Ethiopia and two in South Sudan during 2025. Worldwide, there were ten.

That is the historic low recorded by the World Health Organization and the Carter Center. In 2024 there were 15 reported cases; in 1986, when the global campaign gathered force, the estimate was 3.5 million cases a year across 20 African and Asian countries. WHO now reports that 200 countries and territories have been certified free of the disease.

The achievement did not come from a new injection or tablet. Guinea worm disease, or dracunculiasis, still has no vaccine and no medicine that removes the mature worm. Programmes instead attack the parasite's route through water and its long period inside a host.

The final obstacle is larger than the human count suggests. CDC recorded 664 animal infections in 2024 and 550 during the first half of 2025. Dogs have complicated eradication since 2012, and infections have also appeared in cats and wild animals. Insecurity has made access and surveillance harder in parts of Mali, South Sudan and Sudan.

Ten cases therefore mean extraordinary progress. They do not yet mean the chain has been broken everywhere.

## EVIDENCE

The strongest evidence comes from national programmes reporting through WHO, laboratory confirmation described by CDC, and operational data from the Carter Center. These sources agree on the central facts: reported human disease has fallen by more than 99.99% from the 1986 estimate, the 2025 human total was ten, and animal infections are now a central barrier.

The mechanism is unusually visible. Infection usually begins when a person drinks stagnant water containing tiny copepods—often called water fleas—that carry Guinea worm larvae. CDC also notes a possible route through inadequately cooked aquatic animals or raw fish entrails. The larvae mature inside the body. Roughly 10 to 14 months later, a female worm can emerge through a painful skin blister, commonly on a leg or foot. If the burning limb is placed in water, the worm releases larvae and the cycle begins again.

Each intervention blocks a link: cloth or pipe filters remove copepods; safer water removes exposure; case detection and containment prevent an emerging worm from reaching water; selected ponds may be treated with temephos to kill copepods; fish entrails are disposed of safely; dogs at risk may be tethered during transmission periods.

The evidence is not a perfect census. The 2025 total is provisional. Remote villages, conflict and the long silent interval can delay detection. That is why a small number is encouraging evidence, not proof of absence.

## PERSPECTIVES

The celebratory frame is compelling: humanity is about to erase an ancient source of pain without a vaccine. It correctly recognises decades of village-level work that rarely receives the attention given to a laboratory breakthrough. It can, however, make the finish line look closer and simpler than it is.

The cautious frame begins with the animals. In 2024, reported animal infections outnumbered human cases by more than forty to one. That does not mean hundreds of hidden human cases exist. It means the parasite has routes through the environment that a human-only campaign cannot ignore. The same species, *Dracunculus medinensis*, infects both.

A third frame is political. Eradication depends on reaching every suspected case quickly, maintaining safe-water work and checking places where conflict interrupts access. A disease can become statistically tiny while the cost of finding each remaining infection rises. The last mile is not merely biology; it is logistics, trust and security.

The programme view adds an important correction to all three. Health workers do not wait for a patient to appear at a distant clinic. Community volunteers teach prevention, rewards encourage reports, and national programmes investigate rumours. The Carter Center says more than one million reports were investigated in endemic countries in 2025, nearly all within 24 hours. That figure comes from the programme itself, so it describes operational reach rather than an independent audit. It still shows the scale hidden behind ten confirmed cases.

Good news survives these qualifications. In fact, it becomes more credible when the unfinished work is visible.

## CONTEXT

Smallpox was declared eradicated in 1980 after vaccination stopped person-to-person transmission. Guinea worm is a different kind of test. There is no quick biological shield to deliver. The campaign has had to make an entire transmission route fail, village by village and season after season.

The parasite's delay makes that difficult. A person infected today may not show the characteristic emerging worm until next year. A village can look quiet while infection is developing. One contaminated pond can serve people, dogs and wildlife. A detected animal can contaminate water even when human case containment is excellent.

Certification is therefore deliberately slower than celebration. CDC says villages remain under surveillance for three years after their last known infection. WHO certification of a country requires adequate nationwide surveillance and at least three consecutive years without an indigenous infection. Sudan has not completed certification amid insecurity, while six countries still had indigenous transmission as of June 2025: Angola, Cameroon, Chad, Ethiopia, Mali and South Sudan.

The geography also changes. Cameroon had been certified free of Guinea worm in 2007, but infections were detected again near the border with Chad from 2019 and indigenous transmission was later re-established. That episode is a warning against treating a national border as a biological wall.

There are reasons for confidence. The campaign has repeatedly adapted: rewards make surveillance more sensitive; laboratories identify whether submitted worms are truly *D. medinensis*; programmes filter water and treat ponds; cooking and disposal advice addresses possible aquatic-animal routes; dog tethering seeks to prevent both exposure and contamination.

There are reasons for patience. In the first half of 2025, CDC reported more animal infections than in the same period of 2024. Full-year Carter Center data later described improvement in Chad but increases elsewhere. Different reporting windows can point in different directions without either being false.

Eradication is not a photograph. It is a film assembled from years of surveillance.

## PEOPLE

The global chart ends in a single digit. The work beneath it does not.

A family filtering pond water is performing one layer of disease control. A volunteer walking to investigate a rumour is performing another. A person who reports an emerging worm before entering water protects people they may never meet. A household that keeps an infected dog away from a pond closes a route that did not fit the campaign's original human-centred story.

The burden is also concrete. An emerging worm can be about a metre long and may take weeks to leave the body. The blister burns. Walking, farming, attending school and caring for children can become difficult. The disease rarely dominates international headlines because it is concentrated in remote communities and kills far less dramatically than many infections. Its damage is measured in pain, lost movement and interrupted days.

That is why “only ten” must be read both ways: astonishingly few compared with millions, and ten people who still carried the whole disease in their bodies.

## DEEPER

Eradication changes the meaning of success.

In ordinary public health, a steep decline can be a victory. In eradication, 99.99% is a staging post. The only acceptable endpoint is a trustworthy zero—one that includes the places that are hardest to reach and the hosts the original plan did not expect.

That creates a useful five-question test for every “nearly eliminated” claim.

**What is being counted?** Confirmed human cases, suspected cases, infections in animals and laboratory-negative specimens are different measures.

**How long can the problem remain invisible?** Guinea worm's 10-to-14-month development means surveillance must outlast the excitement of a low year.

**Where else can the agent persist?** Dogs and wildlife turned a once straightforward human interruption strategy into an ecological one.

**How complete is the search?** A zero produced by weak access is not equivalent to a zero produced by active nationwide surveillance.

**Who verifies the finish?** Independent certification matters because programmes, donors and governments all have understandable reasons to want the campaign declared complete.

The last cases are expensive in attention. They appear after the public story has moved on, when teams must keep filters supplied, investigate false alarms and maintain trust for a disease most people will never see. The work becomes less visible precisely when vigilance matters most.

There is a deeper kind of hope here. It is not optimism that assumes the remaining problem will disappear. It is disciplined refusal to let a rare case become an acceptable case. Ten is a reason to celebrate the distance travelled. Zero, carefully verified, is the promise still being kept.

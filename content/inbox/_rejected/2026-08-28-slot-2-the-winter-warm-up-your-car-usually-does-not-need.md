## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** The winter ritual of starting a car and leaving it to idle for several minutes survives from an earlier mechanical era. Official US guidance says modern vehicles generally do not need that extended stationary warm-up.

**What it means.** A cold engine is genuinely different from a warm one: oil is more viscous, combustion is less efficient and the emissions-control system has not reached its effective temperature. The useful response is usually a gentle drive, not a long wait in the driveway.

**Risks and impact.** Unnecessary idling burns fuel, produces pollution and warms the rest of the vehicle slowly. Driving hard immediately is the opposite mistake. Visibility, extreme weather, vehicle age and the manufacturer's instructions can change the decision.

**What can be done.** Start the car, fasten the seat belt, clear or defrost every window, then set off smoothly. Keep engine speed and load moderate until the vehicle is warm. Never run an engine in a garage or another enclosed space.

**What to watch.** Watch the road, not a ceremonial number of minutes. Hesitation, warning lights, unusual noise or an owner's manual with a special cold-start procedure deserve more attention than folklore.

## FACTS

The old advice was not invented from nothing. Carbureted engines often needed time for stable fuel delivery in cold weather. Lubricants were less capable across a wide temperature range, and drivers could feel an engine stumble if they asked too much too soon.

Most modern petrol cars use electronic fuel injection and engine computers that meter extra fuel during a cold start. They can generally move away soon after starting. The archived US Environmental Protection Agency guidance puts the point plainly: modern vehicles do not require winter warm-up, and unnecessary idling wastes fuel and adds pollution.

That is not permission for a full-throttle exit. The engine, transmission, bearings, tyres and cabin are still cold. A restrained first part of the journey lets several systems warm together under modest load.

There is also a distinction between warming an engine and making a car safe to see out of. Ice, snow or condensation may require time. The car should not move until the windscreen, mirrors and other necessary glass provide adequate visibility. That wait is a safety task, not proof that an engine benefits from ten minutes of idling.

## EVIDENCE

A cold start is an awkward phase because the machine is designed to work best within a temperature range.

Cold oil flows less readily than warm oil. Modern multigrade oils are engineered to limit that problem, but they do not repeal temperature. Fuel can evaporate and mix less effectively when surfaces are cold, so the engine management system enriches the mixture. Meanwhile, the catalytic converter controls pollutants most effectively only after it has heated.

Stationary idling supplies little useful load. The engine creates some heat, but the transmission, wheel bearings and tyres are doing almost nothing. Gentle movement asks the engine for more work than idle and distributes warming through more of the car. That is why “drive gently” is not merely an environmental slogan; it matches the mechanism.

Argonne National Laboratory examined a related idling question with a 2011 Ford Fusion fitted with a 2.5-litre engine. At about 21 degrees Celsius, its tests found that idling for more than roughly 10 seconds consumed more fuel and produced more carbon dioxide than shutting the warm engine off and restarting it. The laboratory also noted that a vehicle warms more quickly while being driven.

That experiment should not be stretched into a universal cold-start timer. It involved one vehicle, a limited number of runs and mild ambient conditions. The researchers explicitly said results could differ for cold or hot engines, older vehicles and diesels. Its strongest lesson is narrower: idling has a measurable fuel cost, and restarting a modern warm engine is not the enormous fuel event many drivers imagine.

AAA gives the scale rather than a commandment. Its 2022 guidance says idling can consume about a quarter to half a gallon of fuel per hour, depending on engine size and accessories. Ten minutes sounds small; repeated every working morning through winter, it becomes hours of fuel used without travel.

## PERSPECTIVES

One camp says never idle at all. That is memorable but too absolute. A driver may need to demist glass, scrape ice, secure a child or confirm that the road is clear. In severe cold, a manufacturer may specify a procedure, and a block heater can change the starting conditions without burning fuel at the kerb.

The opposing camp says the engine must be fully warm before the car moves. That confuses caution with stillness. The US Department of Energy's Alternative Fuels Data Center discourages unnecessary idling and remote-start habits for personal vehicles. A long idle is not a complete warm-up because the systems that move the car remain cold.

The useful middle position has two parts: do not demand high power from a cold machine, and do not assume standing still is the gentlest way to warm it. Light, smooth driving usually satisfies both.

Diesel engines, hybrids, plug-in hybrids, classics and vehicles in exceptionally low temperatures deserve model-specific advice. A hybrid may start and stop its combustion engine according to its own control logic. An older carbureted car can behave unlike a modern injected one. The owner's manual outranks a general article.

## CONTEXT

Idling advice is often muddled because three separate questions get compressed into one.

The first is mechanical care after a cold start. The answer for a typical modern car is gentle use while fluids and components warm.

The second is stopping with an already warm engine—for example, while waiting outside a shop. DOE and AAA guidance focuses heavily on this avoidable idling. Argonne's restart comparison also belongs mainly here.

The third is cabin comfort and visibility. Heat for defrosting may be necessary, but remote-starting a car solely to make the seats comfortable carries a fuel and emissions cost. Comfort can be a choice; clear glass is a requirement.

Automatic stop-start systems add another clue. Manufacturers design modern vehicles to shut down and restart at junctions under suitable conditions. The control system may decline to stop the engine when it is cold, the battery needs charge or climate control demands more energy. That is evidence of conditional engineering, not a rule that every driver should manually cycle the ignition.

## PEOPLE

Picture two neighbours after an overnight frost.

One starts the car, returns indoors and lets it run for 12 minutes. The cabin is warmer when they leave, but fuel has been burned in place and the unattended car has spent those minutes producing exhaust.

The other clears the roof and glass, starts the engine, buckles up, checks the mirrors and moves away without hard acceleration. The cabin may be chilly for the first few streets. The engine and drivetrain warm under modest work, and the journey has already begun.

The second routine is usually the better mechanical and environmental bargain. It is not a contest in toughness. Heated clothing, a scraper, covered parking or a properly used block heater can reduce the discomfort without turning the driveway into a warm-up bay.

## DEEPER

A practical cold-morning sequence is short.

**Before starting, clear what you can.** Remove snow from the roof, lights and glass. Check that the exhaust outlet is not blocked by deep snow.

**Start and observe.** Warning lights should complete their normal checks. Use the few moments needed for the seat belt, mirrors and controls. If the engine runs roughly, a warning remains illuminated or the manual instructs otherwise, do not substitute a generic rule.

**Make visibility non-negotiable.** Use the demister and scraper until the necessary field of view is clear. Never idle in a closed garage, even with the door open; carbon monoxide can accumulate and enter connected living space.

**Drive gently.** Avoid high engine speed, sudden acceleration and heavy load while the car is cold. Smooth driving is the warm-up. A temperature gauge that has begun to rise does not necessarily mean every fluid and component is fully warm.

**Separate waiting from warming.** If the engine is already warm and you expect to be parked, consider switching off where it is safe, lawful and consistent with the manual. The exact threshold varies, but the belief that every restart consumes minutes of idling fuel is not supported by the Argonne test.

No universal stopwatch can cover a new hybrid, a 30-year-old classic and a diesel at minus 25 degrees Celsius. The durable rule is simpler: respect the cold machine, prioritize visibility and replace ritual with light load plus vehicle-specific instructions.

## REFLECT

Much car advice survives because it once described a real problem. The mistake is not remembering the past; it is assuming the machine never changed.

On the next cold morning, notice what your warm-up is actually trying to achieve. Is it protecting the engine, clearing the glass or warming the driver? Once those jobs are separated, the sensible choice is easier to see.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Two unrelated bodies of research — a factory troubleshooting technique from Toyota and decades of psychology on ambivalent relationships — point to the same conclusion about why relationships fail.

**What it means.** Breakups rarely come from one dramatic betrayal. They usually come from a smaller, unaddressed pattern that repeats until nobody wants to keep repeating it.

**Risks and impact.** Anyone in a long relationship — romantic, family, or otherwise — is affected, because the pattern applies to any bond that swings between closeness and resentment.

**What can be done.** A manufacturing technique called Five Whys, used to trace a factory defect to its real cause, works surprisingly well on a recurring argument, if both people ask the questions honestly.

**What to watch.** Whether the same fight keeps returning in a new outfit. That is usually the sign the real cause was never named.

## FACTS

Picture two people nine years into a relationship who have stopped raising their voices at each other. No slammed doors, no dramatic exits — the fights about money and time together just quietly stopped happening. From the outside that can look like peace. Often it is the opposite: the sound of two people who have stopped trying to be heard.

Relationships rarely fail in one clean break. Psychologists studying what they call "love–hate relationships" — bonds that carry real love and real resentment at the same time — describe the ending as something that builds for years before it becomes visible. The same pattern shows up somewhere unexpected: the 100-year history of Fianna Fáil, one of Ireland's two dominant political parties, which was born out of a split, spent decades as the country's most powerful "marriage," survived its own version of infidelity, divorced entirely, and eventually remarried its oldest rival. And a Toyota factory technique called Five Whys, built to find why bolts were cross-threading on an assembly line, turns out to be one of the more useful tools for finding what a couple is actually fighting about.

## EVIDENCE

What is reasonably well documented: psychologists describe "love–hate relationships" as involving simultaneous or alternating love and hostility, most visible when emotions run high. One line of research, attributed to Yale University in the source material reviewed for this article, links these relationships to poor self-esteem — though the original study is not detailed enough in that citation to know its size or method, so treat it as a single finding rather than a settled consensus.

More specific is the connection to early bonding. Weak attachment to a parent in infancy, a parent's depressive symptoms, and traits associated with borderline or narcissistic personality patterns in an adult child are all linked, in the literature summarized here, to love–hate dynamics and even full estrangement between adults and their parents. One psychiatric description quoted in that research characterizes the pattern as "unstable and intense interpersonal relationships characterized by alternating between extremes of idealization and devaluation."

On the practical side, the Five Whys technique — developed at Toyota and credited to engineer Taiichi Ohno — is well documented as a real, widely used method, not a self-help gimmick. It has since spread well beyond car factories into Kaizen, lean manufacturing, lean construction and Six Sigma programs, which is a decent sign it survives contact with real, messy problems rather than only tidy textbook ones. It also has well-documented critics, which matters for how honestly it can be applied to something as personal as a relationship.

## PERSPECTIVES

Four honest ways to explain relationship failure, each with a blind spot.

The **behavioral view** looks at how couples fight, not what they fight about. It is strong at spotting specific corrosive patterns — contempt, stonewalling, refusing to be reachable — as they happen. Its blind spot: it describes the symptom well without explaining why the pattern started in the first place.

The **developmental view**, drawn from the research above, traces adult relationship trouble back to early bonding and family patterns. It is strong at explaining why some people swing hard between idealizing and resenting a partner. Its blind spot is treating ambivalence as inherently pathological, when the same source notes it is "particularly common" in any relationship where the emotions are simply intense.

The **root-cause view**, borrowed from Five Whys, treats failure as an unresolved chain of smaller issues rather than one big event. It is fast and concrete. Its own critics — quoted directly in the technique's own documentation — warn it tends to stop at the first plausible answer and produces different "root causes" depending on who is asking.

The **institutional view**, visible in Fianna Fáil's history, treats failure and repair as phases that alternate over decades, shaped as much by circumstance and necessity as by feeling. Its blind spot is the mirror image of the others: it can make reconciliation look inevitable, when in a personal relationship — unlike a parliament, where two parties may simply need each other's votes — nothing forces two people back together at all.

None of these four views cancels the others out. A couple's fights can be genuinely explained, at the same time, by how they argue, what happened to them growing up, what they never actually named, and how much practical reason they still have to stay in the room together.

## CONTEXT

The psychological term for what happens inside a love–hate bond is "splitting" — thinking in all-good or all-bad terms about the same person, usually under stress. It is not exotic; a milder version shows up whenever two reasonably content people, mid-argument, briefly experience each other as entirely the villain. The research reviewed here ties the more extreme, persistent version to poor early bonding and to personality patterns that make ordinary ambivalence — being angry at someone you love without wanting to stop loving them — feel intolerable.

Five Whys works by refusing to accept the first answer. Ohno's own factory example: bolts were cross-threading on the line. Why? The threads weren't cut cleanly. Why? The cutting tool hadn't been changed. Why? The replacement bin was empty. Why? Its contents had fallen. Why? A rusted shelf foot had failed. The fifth answer, not the first, is the one worth fixing. Ohno called repeating the question five times "the basis of Toyota's scientific approach."

The common mistake is assuming the first stated complaint — dishes, money, being late — is the real issue. It rarely is. But Five Whys has genuine limits, and its own critics say so on the record: investigators tend to stop at a symptom, different people asking the same questions land on different "root causes," and a single cause is assumed when several may be operating together. Applied to a relationship, that means Five Whys works best as a shared conversation, not a verdict one partner hands down alone.

## PEOPLE

Fianna Fáil was founded in 1926 when Éamon de Valera split from Sinn Féin over whether elected members should take their seats in the new Irish parliament. The party he built went on to hold power for 61 of the following 79 years, dominating Irish politics for decades. Then, in 1970, the Arms Crisis broke that trust: cabinet ministers Charles Haughey and Neil Blaney were dismissed over accusations of seeking arms for paramilitaries, a rupture that shadowed the party for years. Haughey later rose to lead it anyway, before phone-tapping revelations forced his resignation in 1992.

The clearest divorce came in 1985, when a large share of the membership walked out entirely to found a rival party, the Progressive Democrats — a full split, not a disagreement. Yet by 1989 the two were governing together in coalition. Bertie Ahern, once Haughey's protégé, went on to help broker the Good Friday Agreement in 1998 before his own career ended over a financial scandal in 2008. In 2020, Fianna Fáil did something it had never done in its history: it entered government with its oldest rival, Fine Gael, rotating the office of Taoiseach between them. A century of rupture and reconciliation, driven less by resolved feelings than by shared necessity.

## DEEPER

The honest question under all of this is not "how do you avoid conflict" — every source above agrees you cannot, and shouldn't try. The real question is what actually makes reconciliation possible after a genuine rupture: feeling differently, or choosing to act differently while the feelings are still unresolved.

One of the older texts on this, read here as one source of wisdom among several rather than a final answer, is the story of Joseph and his brothers in Genesis. Years after they sold him into slavery, Joseph reveals himself to them — not by pretending the betrayal never happened, but by naming it plainly: "I am Joseph your brother, whom ye sold into Egypt" (Genesis 45:4). The reconciliation that follows does not erase the years of estrangement or excuse what caused them. It starts by both sides agreeing on what the real "why" actually was — which is, oddly enough, exactly what Five Whys asks two people at a factory, or a kitchen table, to do together.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Phishing — tricking people into handing over money, passwords, or access by pretending to be someone trustworthy — is now the most common form of cybercrime, and generative AI is making the fake messages harder to spot.

**What it means.** The share of businesses hit by phishing attempts rose from 72% in 2017 to 94% in 2023, and the same tricks now work over email, text, QR code, and phone calls that clone a real person's voice.

**Risks and impact.** Anyone can be targeted, but older adults are disproportionately hit by phone scams, and finance staff at ordinary businesses are the preferred target of the most costly, tailored attacks.

**What can be done.** Never act on a message that creates urgency. Hang up or close the message, then contact the organization yourself using a number or address you already had — not one the message gave you.

**What to watch.** Whether banks and phone carriers roll out caller-ID verification and non-SMS two-factor authentication fast enough to outpace scams that already know how to defeat both.

## FACTS

In the 2024 film *The Beekeeper*, a retired schoolteacher loses her life savings — and the funds of a charity she runs — after clicking on a warning about her accounts. It's fiction, built for an action thriller. But the scam underneath it is not: phishing is a form of social engineering where attackers pose as a trusted source — a bank, a government office, a colleague — to get people to hand over money, passwords, or remote access to their devices.

It's also, by most measures, the most common cybercrime there is. The term itself dates to 1995, coined in a hacking toolkit called AOHell, when the "lure" was a fake AOL support message. Three decades later, the scale is enormous: the share of businesses reporting phishing attempts climbed from 72% in 2017 to 86% in 2020 and 94% in 2023, according to the tracked figures on Wikipedia's phishing entry. Generative AI is a big reason the recent jump is steeper — it lets attackers write convincing, personalized messages automatically, at a scale no human scam operation could match by hand.

Phishing isn't one technique. It covers email lures, voice calls (vishing), text messages (smishing), fake QR codes (quishing), and tools that intercept a login in real time even when a victim does everything "right."

## CONTEXT

The oldest version is bulk email phishing: a fake message from "your bank" links to a cloned login page that captures whatever you type. Most of these are sent to huge, untargeted lists, hoping a small percentage bite.

Spear phishing is the tailored version, and it's more dangerous because it works. Attackers research a specific person or company and reference real details — a real vendor, a real invoice, a real coworker's name — to seem credible. Accounting and audit firms are named as especially vulnerable, because employees there routinely handle exactly the kind of sensitive financial requests a scammer wants to imitate. It isn't just criminal gangs: in 2016, Russian government-linked hackers ran spear-phishing campaigns against more than 1,800 Google accounts tied to a US presidential campaign, using a lookalike domain built to resemble Google's own login page.

Voice phishing, or vishing, exploits something email can't: people still trust a human voice, especially one that sounds official. Modern vishing calls use spoofed caller ID, so a scammer can make a phone display "your bank" or a real government agency, paired with automated text-to-speech systems or live callers running a script. Common versions impersonate tax authorities threatening arrest, tech-support staff asking for remote access, or — increasingly — a trusted person's actual cloned voice, generated from a short audio sample.

The most technically advanced attacks now defeat two-factor authentication (2FA) entirely, using tools that sit invisibly between a victim and the real website, passing information back and forth in real time. Rather than stealing a password to use later, they steal the live session itself — the login code included — the moment a victim enters it. That's why security researchers now consider SMS-based 2FA, alone, weaker protection than it used to be.

Why does any of this work on careful people? A study on spear-phishing susceptibility found that over 21 days of everyday email, 43% of people aged 18–25 clicked a simulated phishing link, and 58% of older participants did — with older women showing the highest susceptibility of any group tested. Every method above leans on the same three levers: urgency ("your account will be locked in one hour"), authority (a badge, a bank logo, a familiar voice), and fear of consequences (an unpaid tax bill, a missed delivery, a hacked account). None of that requires technical sophistication. It requires a moment when someone is distracted, worried, or in a hurry — which is to say, most moments in most days.

## PEOPLE

Vishing scammers disproportionately target older adults and immigrants — groups more likely to trust a formal-sounding voice on the phone and less likely to have encountered the specific script before. Threats of arrest or deportation are common levers used against immigrant callers specifically, pushing people to wire money before they have time to check the claim with anyone else.

At the other end, spear phishing targets people with financial authority inside ordinary businesses — the employee who can actually approve a wire transfer or change a payment account. A single well-researched email, timed to look like it comes from a real boss or vendor, has repeatedly been enough to move real company money out the door in one transaction.

## DEEPER

Underneath the technology, phishing runs on something much older than the internet: we're built to extend fast, automatic trust to voices, logos, and familiar formats, because for most of human history that trust was a reasonable shortcut. A voice on a phone used to mean a real, locatable person. A letterhead used to be hard to fake. Those shortcuts evolved for a world where forgery took real effort.

Technology removed the effort without removing the shortcut. Caller ID can be spoofed by anyone. A voice can now be cloned from seconds of audio pulled off a public video. A login page can be copied pixel-for-pixel in minutes. The tools for forgery became nearly free; the instinct to trust the result didn't update at the same speed.

That gap — between how convincing a fake can be and how quickly we're wired to believe it — is really the whole story of why phishing keeps working on smart, careful people. It isn't a character flaw. It's an old trust system meeting a new forgery cost, and the system hasn't caught up yet. The one thing that reliably closes that gap isn't cleverness or suspicion of everyone; it's a single habit — pausing to verify through a channel you already trust, instead of the one the message just handed you.

## REFLECT

Think of the last message or call that made you feel a flash of urgency. Did you verify it independently before acting, or after?

Who in your life — a parent, a grandparent, an employee — is most likely to trust a voice on the phone, and have you ever actually talked with them about how to check one?

One thing to do today: save the real phone numbers for your bank, your card issuer, and one government office you deal with, straight from your card or their official website — so you're never in a position where a caller has to tell you who to call back.

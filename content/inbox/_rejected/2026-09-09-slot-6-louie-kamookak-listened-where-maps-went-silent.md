## BRIEFLY

For more than a century, searches for Sir John Franklin's lost expedition repeatedly returned to naval records, European maps and the last written note left by the crews. Louie Kamookak began from another archive: what Inuit elders around Gjoa Haven knew, remembered and called the places where events had happened.

Kamookak gathered accounts, compared names and routes, and worked with researchers and search teams. Parks Canada says his knowledge helped redirect attention toward the area where HMS *Erebus* was found in 2014. HMS *Terror* was located two years later. The discoveries did not turn oral history into a magical shortcut, nor did they prove every remembered detail. They showed why a search becomes weaker when it excludes the people whose families had observed the landscape and its traces.

His contribution offers a disciplined lesson about mysteries: testimony becomes more useful when it is preserved in its own geography, compared across tellers and tested against physical evidence.

## FACTS

Franklin left Britain in 1845 with *Erebus*, *Terror* and 129 officers and men to seek a Northwest Passage. The ships became trapped in ice in September 1846. A note later found on King William Island recorded that the crews abandoned them in April 1848. None of the expedition members survived.

The official search that followed was enormous. Parks Canada's historical designation records more than 32 British, Canadian and American search or relief expeditions between 1847 and 1859. Searchers recovered documents, objects and human remains, but the ships themselves remained missing.

Inuit witnesses had supplied accounts of encounters, deaths, objects and a ship sinking. Yet the long public history of the mystery often treated those accounts as secondary to written naval evidence. Kamookak, an Inuk historian from Gjoa Haven, spent decades collecting knowledge from elders, studying the expedition and connecting descriptions to Inuit place names.

Parks Canada says it involved Inuit knowledge holders in Franklin searches beginning in the 1990s. In 1997, Kamookak joined a search with historian David Woodman near the western shore of the Adelaide Peninsula. The agency later consulted him regularly. In 2014, evidence and Inuit knowledge helped shift the search area; *Erebus* was found in Wilmot and Crampton Bay. *Terror* was located in Terror Bay in 2016.

## EVIDENCE

Kamookak's method mattered because a place can disappear when its name is translated badly or not at all.

A European chart is organised around coordinates, surveyed coastlines and names imposed by explorers. Oral geography may encode who used a site, what happened there, how ice or currents behave and how one place relates to another by travel. If an interviewer extracts only a dramatic sentence — “a ship sank here” — while losing the original place name and route, much of the navigational information vanishes.

Parks Canada describes Kamookak sharing locations and names he had gathered from elders. It also records a decisive 2014 chain: archaeologists found objects associated with Franklin on an island; team members linked the site with earlier Inuit testimony; the search zone moved south; sonar then detected *Erebus* near the location where an Inuk named Inukpujijuk had reported seeing a ship sink.

This was convergence, not substitution. Testimony narrowed and interpreted the search. Archaeological objects and sonar supplied different kinds of confirmation. Divers and subsequent investigation established that a wreck was really present and helped identify it.

The Canadian Museum of History's Franklin exhibition made the archival dimension visible. It included an audio station featuring Kamookak and noted that 81 Northwest Passage interviews were transferred to the museum's archives. Preserving the recordings matters because future researchers can hear more than a summary: emphasis, sequence, uncertainty and the relation between different accounts.

## PERSPECTIVES

There are two equally misleading versions of this story.

One says modern technology solved the mystery: aircraft, sonar, remotely operated equipment and underwater archaeology finally found what nineteenth-century searchers could not. Those tools were indispensable, but a sonar operator must still decide where to look. Search area is an intellectual choice before it becomes a technical one.

The opposite version says Inuit oral history had always supplied an exact wreck coordinate and outsiders simply refused to listen. That makes a more satisfying morality tale than the evidence supports. Memories travelled across generations; place names required careful interpretation; accounts differed; shorelines, sea ice and search conditions complicated the work. Kamookak's achievement was not possession of a secret answer. It was sustained comparison.

Nunatsiaq News described him as instrumental to the finds and reported that Parks Canada's Robert Grenier began a renewed search with Kamookak in 2008. Parks Canada's own account places him within a larger team of Inuit knowledge holders, historians, archaeologists, hydrographers and government agencies. Giving him credit does not require erasing that collaboration. It requires recognising that the collaboration changed what counted as evidence.

## CONTEXT

The Franklin story was shaped by the hierarchy of nineteenth-century archives. Written documents produced by officers entered imperial collections. Inuit testimony was often filtered through translators, searchers and published retellings. Later readers could mistake the survival of a paper record for proof that paper was the only serious record.

Kamookak worked against that hierarchy without pretending that memory is flawless. He interviewed elders, traced kinship and transmission, examined written expedition literature and took knowledge back onto the land. His work joined archives that institutions had kept apart.

The wreck discoveries also changed stewardship. Parks Canada describes the Wrecks of HMS *Erebus* and HMS *Terror* National Historic Site as the first national historic site jointly managed by Inuit and Parks Canada. That does not settle every question about ownership, access or interpretation, but it moves local knowledge from the margins of a search into the governance of what was found.

## DEEPER

A mystery is not only a missing object. It is also a dispute over which signals deserve attention.

Search systems tend to privilege evidence that is easy to standardise: a coordinate, a written date, an instrument reading. Local knowledge may arrive as narrative, seasonal description or relational geography. Treating it respectfully does not mean lowering standards. It means doing the extra work required to preserve its structure and make comparisons possible.

Kamookak's example suggests four safeguards. Keep the original place names alongside translations. Record who learned an account from whom. Separate direct observation from later inference. Then let independent evidence pressure-test the resulting hypothesis.

That method avoids both dismissal and romanticisation. Oral history is neither an anecdote to decorate a technical search nor an oracle exempt from checking. It is evidence with provenance, transmission conditions and interpretive demands.

The human point is quieter. Kamookak listened for years before the world had a wreck to reward the effort. The discoveries made his contribution legible to distant audiences, but the intellectual labour came first: treating elders as holders of knowledge rather than colourful witnesses to somebody else's story.

## PRACTICAL TAKEAWAY

When a local account conflicts with an official map or written archive, do not ask only which source is “right.” Ask what each source was built to preserve.

A useful evidence note can contain:

- the speaker and how they know;
- the original place name and language;
- whether the account is firsthand or transmitted;
- the season, route and landmarks described;
- points shared with other accounts;
- points that remain uncertain;
- what physical or documentary evidence could test the claim.

For museums, journalists and researchers, retaining recordings and attribution is more valuable than extracting one convenient quotation. For readers, the same discipline applies: honour testimony by examining it carefully, not by believing or dismissing it automatically.

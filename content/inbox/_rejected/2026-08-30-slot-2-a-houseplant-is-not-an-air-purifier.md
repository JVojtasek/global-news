## BRIEFLY

**What happened:** The famous claim that houseplants clean indoor air grew from laboratory tests, including a 1989 NASA report, in which plants and their root-zone systems removed some volatile chemicals from sealed chambers.

**Why it matters:** A chamber proves that removal can occur. It does not show that a normal number of passive plants can compete with ventilation, filtration or a continuing indoor source.

**The mechanism:** Pollutants may deposit on leaves, enter through stomata or be processed by roots and microbes. But real rooms constantly gain and lose air, and often keep emitting pollutants from cooking, fuel, furnishings or cleaning products.

**The uncertainty:** Plant species, soil, light, pollutant and building conditions differ. Active green-wall systems that force air through roots are not equivalent to pots sitting on a sill.

**What you can do:** Keep plants for beauty and pleasure. For cleaner air, identify the source first, ventilate when outdoor conditions allow, and use appropriate filtration or official safety measures for the pollutant involved.

## FACTS

NASA’s work was neither silly nor fraudulent. It asked whether plants could help in tightly enclosed habitats and tested chemicals including benzene, trichloroethylene and formaldehyde in small, sealed experimental chambers. The observed declines helped establish that leaves, potting media and associated microbes can participate in pollutant removal.

The consumer leap came later: if a plant removes a chemical in a box, then a few plants must clean a home. That skips the conversion from a concentration decline to a clean-air delivery rate — the volume of pollutant-free air an intervention effectively supplies per hour.

A 2019 review re-analysed 196 results from 12 chamber studies. Its median clean-air delivery rate was about 0.023 cubic metres per hour per plant. The authors estimated that matching the pollutant removal already provided by typical building air exchange would require roughly 10 to 1,000 plants per square metre of floor space. At the high end, the living room has become a rainforest and the sofa has lost the argument.

## EVIDENCE

The strongest evidence against the household claim does not say plants absorb nothing. It says passive removal is usually too slow at realistic density. The review converted unlike chamber experiments into a common rate and compared that rate with the much larger flow created by ordinary ventilation and air leakage.

A 2022 experiment supplies the useful pressure test. Researchers placed three common plant systems in 150-litre chambers with nitrogen dioxide at a concentration representing a polluted urban environment. All reduced NO₂ to varying degrees. When the best result was modelled for a small 15-cubic-metre office, however, it amounted to about 0.62 parts per billion per potted plant over an hour. The authors stressed that the result would depend heavily on ventilation and the indoor-outdoor concentration gradient.

Both statements can therefore be true: the plant performs measurable chemistry, and one pot is not a practical air purifier.

## PERSPECTIVES

The confusion persists because “removes pollutants” sounds like a complete product claim. It is only the numerator. The denominator includes room volume, time, number of plants, the rate at which fresh pollution enters and the rate at which other processes remove it.

Imagine one towel absorbing water while a tap remains open and a drain is already carrying water away. Proving that the towel gets wet does not tell you whether it changes the water level. You need all three rates.

There is also a category error between passive pots and engineered botanical filters. A powered green wall may pull room air through a large root-and-microbe surface. That added airflow can change the clean-air delivery rate, just as a fan changes a filter. Evidence about such systems should be judged as evidence about a device, not quietly transferred to an unpowered fern.

None of this makes houseplants pointless. Their colour, care routine and presence can make a room more pleasant. A benefit does not become smaller because it is aesthetic rather than a substitute for mechanical ventilation.

## CONTEXT

Indoor air quality is not one problem. Particles from smoke require a different response from carbon monoxide, radon or gaseous pollutants. A HEPA filter is designed for particles; activated carbon and other sorbents may address some gases, with performance and replacement needs that vary. Carbon monoxide requires functioning alarms and correction of the combustion source, not decorative foliage.

The US Environmental Protection Agency organises indoor-air improvement around three strategies: source control, ventilation, and cleaners or filtration. It says source control is usually the most effective. That might mean exhausting cooking emissions, not smoking indoors, maintaining fuel-burning appliances, or removing a product that is releasing a pollutant.

Ventilation is conditional. Opening windows can help when indoor sources dominate and outdoor air is cleaner. During wildfire smoke or severe outdoor pollution, it may make matters worse; current local public guidance should decide that question. Filtration also needs to match the pollutant and the room rather than merely having an impressive plant-like casing.

EPA guidance further recommends working smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms in homes and radon testing. These are dull, proven acts. A glossy leaf is easier to photograph, which may explain part of the marketing imbalance.

## DEEPER

Use a source-first audit. Name the concern — cooking particles, dampness, smoke, solvents, an attached garage, combustion or outdoor pollution — because “stale air” is not a diagnosis. Then ask what creates it, what removes it and whether you can measure or reliably detect it.

For particles, compare a cleaner’s documented clean-air delivery rate with room size and keep filters maintained. For combustion risks, use alarms and qualified inspection. For radon, test. For dampness and mould, correct the moisture source. For any immediate hazard or symptom concern, follow local health, fire or building guidance rather than experimenting with plants.

Keep the houseplants if you like them. Just separate two promises. “This living thing makes my home feel better” is personal and perfectly respectable. “This pot cleans a room at a meaningful rate” is an engineering claim that needs airflow, volume and time attached.

That distinction is the durable lesson of the NASA story. Laboratory evidence is not disproved when it fails to scale; it is being translated. The plant did something in the chamber. The room asks whether it did enough.

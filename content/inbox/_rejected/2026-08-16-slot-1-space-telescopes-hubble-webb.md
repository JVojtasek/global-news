## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Nothing new — this is the story of how the two most productive telescopes ever built, Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope, came to exist at all, and why neither worked the way anyone expected on day one.

**What it means.** A telescope in orbit sees wavelengths of light the atmosphere destroys before they reach the ground. That single fact justifies missions that take decades to build and cannot be fixed once they fail.

**Risks and impact.** Both flagship missions nearly ended before they began: Hubble launched with a flawed mirror, and Webb's budget and schedule blew past every early estimate. Almost every other space telescope, unlike Hubble, cannot be repaired if something breaks after launch.

**What can be done.** Nothing readers need to act on. The honest takeaway is patience: the biggest discoveries in astronomy now come from instruments that took longer to build than most careers last.

**What to watch.** NASA's own 2023 statement flagged a possible gap between today's aging space telescopes and the next generation — worth watching if you follow deep-space discoveries.

## FACTS

In April 1990, the newly launched Hubble Space Telescope sent back its first images — and they were blurred. Engineers traced the problem to Hubble's primary mirror, ground very slightly the wrong shape, a flaw called spherical aberration that compromised the telescope's core mission. It took three years and a shuttle crew, flying as mission STS-61 in 1993, to fit corrective optics and bring Hubble's vision up to its intended quality.

That near-failure came after decades of advocacy. Astronomer Lyman Spitzer first proposed putting a telescope in space in 1946. Nancy Grace Roman, NASA's first Chief of Astronomy and its first female executive, spent years persuading NASA and the US Congress that the idea was, in her words, "very well worth doing." Hubble finally launched on 24 April 1990 aboard the Space Shuttle Discovery. It was not the first space telescope — the American OAO-2 (1968) and the Soviet Orion 1 aboard the Salyut 1 space station (1971) came earlier — but it became the one that changed public astronomy.

## EVIDENCE

The physics behind the whole enterprise is well established and not in dispute. Earth's atmosphere scatters and blurs starlight — the "twinkling" that makes stars shimmer — and limits ground telescopes to a resolution of roughly 0.5 to 1.0 arcseconds, compared with a theoretical limit near 0.05 arcseconds for a similarly sized mirror working outside the atmosphere. The atmosphere also blocks X-rays outright and heavily absorbs infrared and ultraviolet light, which is why instruments built to observe in those bands, including Webb, have to leave the ground entirely. The trade-off is just as well established: space telescopes cost far more to build than ground telescopes, and because of where they operate, almost none of them can be serviced once launched. Hubble, reachable by Space Shuttle in low Earth orbit, is the exception, not the rule.

What is genuinely uncertain is how long the current generation of instruments will last, and what replaces them. Hubble passed thirty years of operation in 2020 and is projected — not guaranteed — to keep working into the 2030s. NASA's own January 2023 announcement, floating early plans for programs such as a "Habitable Worlds Observatory," acknowledged that scientists are worried about a coverage gap if funding for the next generation of space telescopes doesn't arrive on time. That is a stated concern from the agency itself, not a settled outcome.

## PERSPECTIVES

Seen one way, Webb's history looks like a case study in bureaucratic failure: initial studies in 1999 targeted a 2007 launch on a one-billion-dollar budget; the finished telescope launched in 2021 after a major redesign, at a total cost of ten billion dollars. Critics of large government science programs point to exactly this kind of overrun as evidence that big missions need tighter oversight before approval, not after.

Seen another way, the same history is a story about doing something that had never been done, on a fixed budget estimated before the engineering problems were even known. Webb has no service missions planned; it sits roughly 1.5 million kilometres from Earth, far beyond the reach of any current spacecraft. Every stage of unfolding it in space, including a sunshield that had already torn once during 2018 ground testing, had to work correctly the first time, with no possibility of sending anyone to fix it.

A third framing, less dramatic than either, comes from how the project was actually built: NASA led the design and development, but partnered formally with the European Space Agency and the Canadian Space Agency, with Northrop Grumman as prime contractor. Webb's cost and delays were shared and debated across three space agencies and years of congressional and parliamentary budget cycles — a genuinely international, multi-decade undertaking, not a single agency's overrun.

## CONTEXT

The core design choice separating Hubble from Webb is which part of the light spectrum each one was built to see, and that choice explains almost everything else about them. Hubble observes mainly in ultraviolet, visible and near-infrared light — roughly the range human eyes and ordinary cameras work in, extended slightly. Webb was built for the red end of visible light through the mid-infrared, a much longer-wavelength slice of the spectrum that Hubble's instruments barely reach.

That difference is not a matter of preference. Light from the most distant galaxies has been stretched into infrared wavelengths by the universe's expansion — the further back you look, the more the light has shifted. Infrared also passes more easily through the clouds of dust that hide young star-forming regions, and it is the wavelength at which cold objects, such as debris disks and planets, radiate most of their own light. Hubble's furthest confirmed galaxy, GN-z11, dates to roughly 400 million years after the Big Bang; Webb was designed to see galaxies from as early as 180 million years after it, near the theorised era of the very first stars.

Seeing that far into the infrared has a physical cost: Webb's own instruments would swamp faint infrared signals with their own heat if allowed to warm up, so the telescope must stay below 50 kelvin — about minus 223 degrees Celsius. Its five-layer sunshield, each layer roughly a tenth of a millimetre of aluminium-coated film, blocks heat from the Sun, Earth and Moon so effectively that engineers describe its protection as equivalent to sun protection factor 1,000,000, against a sunscreen bottle's factor of 8 to 50. To fit inside its Ariane 5 rocket, that sunshield had to fold twelve times and then unfurl, untorn, in the vacuum of space.

Hubble's mirror is 2.4 metres across; Webb's, built from eighteen gold-coated beryllium hexagons, spans 6.5 metres and gathers about six times as much light — while weighing only about half as much as Hubble, thanks largely to that segmented, foldable design.

## PEOPLE

The people who fought hardest for these telescopes did most of that work long before either one flew. Lyman Spitzer proposed the idea in 1946 and spent much of the rest of his career pushing for it; Hubble did not reach orbit until 1990, forty-four years later. Nancy Grace Roman built her advocacy work into congressional testimony and steering-committee decisions through the 1970s, laying groundwork for a telescope that launched well after she had already shaped how NASA managed large science projects.

One exchange, recorded from NASA associate administrator Noel Hinners, shows how unglamorous that persistence could be. In 1977, facing a token five-million-dollar placeholder for Hubble's budget, Hinners instead recommended cutting the funding to zero — a deliberate gamble meant to energize the astronomy community into fighting harder for the telescope. By his own account, the decision "had the desired impact of stimulating the astronomy community to renew their efforts on the lobbying front," even though, as he admitted afterward, "I'm not sure I thought it through all that well." A telescope now credited with reshaping astronomy survived, in part, because one administrator bet that outrage would organise a community more effectively than a small consolation budget would.

## DEEPER

There is something worth sitting with in the sheer span of these timelines. Spitzer proposed a space telescope in 1946; Hubble did not reach orbit until 1990 — forty-four years later, long enough for the idea to outlast most of one career and start on another. Webb's concept, sketched in 1996 under the name "Next Generation Space Telescope," did not launch until 2021 — twenty-five years, and a full redesign, later.

Most human effort is not like that. We tend to work toward things we expect to see finished: a renovated kitchen, a finished degree, a promotion. Big science asks something rarer — sustained effort on a timeline longer than a career, handed forward to people who were still training, or not yet working in the field, when the project began. The engineers who carried out Webb's 2005 redesign inherited a decade-old concept; the ones who watched it finally unfold its mirror in 2022 inherited someone else's twenty-five-year wait.

That kind of patience is unfashionable. Almost everything in modern life is built to deliver feedback fast — a message, a purchase, a like. A space telescope offers none of that for years, sometimes decades, and then, if the mirror was ground correctly and the sunshield didn't tear, it offers something no one alive had ever actually seen before. The quiet argument these instruments make is not really about astronomy. It is that some of the most valuable things anyone builds cannot be rushed to fit inside how long any one person is willing to wait.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** EU, UK and US passenger-rights rules all give travellers specific cash or refund entitlements when a flight is delayed, cancelled or significantly changed. Most eligible passengers do not use them.

**What it means.** The gap between the entitlement and the claim is not a legal mystery. It is a design feature: the airline will not raise it, and it has one chance to say no before the regulator says yes.

**Risks and impact.** Direct money, often between €250 and €600 per passenger on EU and UK routes; automatic refunds in the US since 2024. Over a family of four, the sums are not small.

**What can be done.** Keep three documents, use the airline's own portal first, and if the first reply is a refusal, escalate to the national regulator — not to a court.

**What to watch.** Whether the EU's long-running revision of Regulation 261 — stuck in Council for several years — ever lands and changes the thresholds.

## FACTS

Three parallel systems protect air passengers on most of the routes a reader of this site is likely to fly.

**EU Regulation 261/2004.** Applies to any flight departing from an EU, Iceland, Norway or Switzerland airport, and to flights operated by an EU-licensed airline arriving in the EU. If the delay at arrival is three hours or more, you are entitled to cash compensation of €250 (flights up to 1,500 km), €400 (intra-EU flights over 1,500 km and other flights 1,500-3,500 km), or €600 (flights over 3,500 km between an EU and non-EU airport). Cancellations with less than 14 days' notice work similarly. The airline must also provide meals and, where relevant, accommodation during the wait. The only full escape clause is "extraordinary circumstances" — weather, strikes outside the airline, air-traffic control decisions, bird strikes. Routine technical faults do not count.

**UK Regulation 261 (post-Brexit retained law).** Mirrors the EU rules in sterling: £220, £350 and £520. Enforced by the UK Civil Aviation Authority (CAA). Covers flights departing from UK airports and UK-airline flights arriving in the UK.

**US Department of Transportation.** From 2024, the DOT's "Automatic Refunds" final rule requires airlines to issue automatic cash refunds for cancellations, "significant changes," and three-hour-or-more delays on domestic flights (six hours or more on international flights). The refund is to the original payment method and must be issued within seven business days for credit cards. Unlike the European system, there is no fixed cash compensation on top of the refund — but there is also no need to ask: the refund is supposed to appear.

## EVIDENCE

The regulations and the DOT rule are all public, consolidated and up to date on the regulators' own websites — the European Commission's Your Europe portal, the UK CAA, and the DOT's Fly Rights page and 2024 rulemaking archive. These are the primary sources; the many commercial claim-management sites that promise to help you get your money for a cut are secondary summaries, not an independent source of law.

What converges across all three systems: the entitlement is yours by default, not by negotiation, and the burden of proof for "extraordinary circumstances" sits with the airline, not with you.

What differs: the EU/UK model is a fixed-cash lump sum per passenger; the US model is a refund of what you paid, no more. For a cheap advance ticket on a short EU hop, EU compensation can exceed the ticket price; for an expensive last-minute US fare, the US refund can be bigger than any EU award.

What is uncertain: the EU's own data on compliance rates. Various enforcement reports over the years have suggested that only a minority of eligible claims are actually paid. The CAA has made similar observations about the UK market. The practical reading is that most passengers who are owed money do not get it unless they ask twice.

## PERSPECTIVES

**The passenger view.** The rules exist; the money is yours; take ten minutes to claim it. For a family of four on a long-haul delay, €2,400 of EU261 compensation is a meaningful sum.

**The airline view.** Airlines point out — not unreasonably — that compensation under EU261 is unrelated to the ticket price, so a €59 fare can trigger a €600 payout and distort the economics of low-cost flying. The industry has been lobbying for revision for over a decade, with limited success.

**The regulator view.** CAA and the EU national enforcement bodies have the authority to force payment but limited budget. Their preferred route is for passengers to use the airline's own process first, then escalate to approved Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) schemes, which are free to use and binding on the airline where it has signed up.

**The consumer-advocate view.** Encourage passengers to go direct and keep the money. The claim-management firms that take 25-35 per cent of your award exist mostly for passengers who have already tried and failed once; for a first clean claim, they are an unnecessary tax.

## CONTEXT

Three concrete steps make the difference between an eligible claim and a successful one.

**One: hold on to the documents.** You need three things. The booking confirmation (shows the schedule). The boarding pass or e-ticket (shows you were there). The actual arrival time (shows the delay). The arrival time is the one most people forget; airport websites and services such as FlightAware archive it, and screenshotting it on the day is the single most useful habit a traveller can form. For EU and UK claims, "arrival time" means the moment the first door of the aircraft is opened — not scheduled gate time, not touchdown.

**Two: start with the airline's own form.** Every major carrier has a passenger-rights or EU261 submission form on its website, usually under "customer relations" or "feedback." Submit the claim there first. Keep the reference number. Set a 14-day reminder; many carriers do not reply at all in the first round, and the clock on escalation starts when you have not had a substantive answer.

**Three: escalate once, correctly.** If the airline refuses or does not reply, use the national regulator or an ADR scheme — not the courts. In the UK, this means the CAA or an approved ADR body such as CEDR or AviationADR. In the EU, this means the National Enforcement Body of the country where the flight departed from (or arrived in, for EU-airline inbound flights). In the US, it means a DOT consumer complaint via the online portal. All of these are free. ADR and regulator decisions overturn a surprising share of first-round refusals, particularly those citing "extraordinary circumstances" that turn out to be routine technical issues.

A fourth, less-remembered point: compensation is per passenger, not per booking. Children on their own ticket are entitled too. Infants on an adult's lap usually are not, because they do not have their own seat. On a family trip, this detail roughly doubles the amount at stake.

Time limits vary. In the UK, the Limitation Act gives six years from the flight for a court claim; in the EU it depends on the country (two years in some, up to ten in others). In the US, DOT complaints do not have a statutory window but should be filed within a few months to be useful.

## DEEPER

There is a quiet pattern in modern consumer rights. The entitlements are written large, in regulations that governments like to announce. The claim process is written small, in forms that only exist because someone deliberately looked for them. The gap between the two is a kind of unofficial discount for the industry.

A thoughtful reader does not need to be angry about this to see what it asks of them. Rights that have to be claimed will mostly go unclaimed, and the people who do claim will on average be the ones who need the money least — those who travel often, who understand the forms, who are not exhausted at the baggage carousel. Rights that are delivered by default — like the US automatic refund — behave differently and reach more people.

In the meantime, the useful posture is a mild, informed persistence. The airline has written down what it owes you; you can read the same page. The regulator has written down who makes airlines pay; you can send it an email. None of this is adversarial. It is paperwork, done once, within the window, with the three documents in hand.

Most readers will fly a few times a year without a delay long enough to trigger any of this. One day, one of those flights will be the four-hour-late evening into Prague or Birmingham or Chicago, and the question will not be whether you had the right but whether you remembered.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Power banks have become routine travel kit, but aviation authorities classify them as spare lithium batteries rather than ordinary electronic devices.

**What it means.** A power bank belongs in the cabin, where overheating, swelling or smoke can be noticed and a trained crew can respond—not in an unattended aircraft hold.

**Risks and impact.** Damage, heat, a manufacturing defect or contact between a terminal and metal can trigger thermal runaway, an accelerating release of heat that may produce fire and toxic smoke.

**What can be done.** Read the watt-hour label, check the airline’s limit, protect exposed terminals, keep the battery from being crushed and remove it if a carry-on is gate-checked.

**What to watch.** Do not fly with a swollen, damaged or recalled battery. During the journey, unusual heat, expansion, odour, hissing or smoke should be reported to staff immediately.

## FACTS

The small brick that rescues a fading phone is not treated like the phone itself. Aviation rules place a power bank in the same category as an uninstalled, or spare, lithium-ion battery. That distinction decides where it travels.

The [FAA’s PackSafe guidance](https://www.faa.gov/hazmat/packsafe/lithium-batteries), updated on 11 August 2026, says spare lithium batteries and power banks must be in carry-on baggage only. If the bag is taken at the gate and sent to the hold, the passenger must remove them and keep them in the cabin. Terminals must be protected against contact with metal.

For ordinary rechargeable batteries, the common threshold is 100 watt-hours per battery. The FAA says batteries from 101 to 160 Wh may be carried only with airline approval, with no more than two larger spares per person. Above 160 Wh, ordinary passenger carriage is not permitted. Airlines can impose stricter quantity or capacity rules, so the operator’s current policy remains the final pre-flight check.

A battery’s label should state Wh. If it gives volts and amp-hours instead, watt-hours equal volts multiplied by amp-hours. If capacity is in milliamp-hours, divide by 1,000 first.

## EVIDENCE

Why the cabin? Lithium-ion cells store a useful amount of energy in a compact package. If internal damage, overheating, overcharging, water exposure or a short circuit destabilises a cell, it can enter thermal runaway: heat drives further reactions, which create still more heat.

The [FAA’s passenger battery guide](https://www.faa.gov/hazmat/packsafe/resources/airline-passengers-batteries) says this can happen without warning, including because of manufacturing defects. It also explains the operational reason for cabin carriage: flight crews are trained to recognise and respond to battery fires, while a passenger can notice an overheating or expanding device.

The [European Union Aviation Safety Agency](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/domains/air-operations/dangerous-goods) reaches the same practical conclusion. Spare batteries and power banks must never be in checked baggage and must be individually protected against short circuits. In May 2025, EASA said its strengthened recommendations followed an increase in reported lithium-battery safety events aboard passenger aircraft.

The evidence does not imply that every intact power bank is waiting to ignite. It shows a low-frequency hazard with serious consequences, made more manageable by visibility, separation and rapid response. Cabin carriage is a control measure, not a prediction about a particular battery.

## PERSPECTIVES

One traveller sees the rule as airport bureaucracy: the same battery chemistry is allowed inside a laptop, so why fuss over the loose power bank? The answer is partly physical. A fitted battery is enclosed and connected as the device was designed; a spare can be dropped, crushed or bridged by keys and coins. Installed devices may sometimes travel checked under conditions, but a spare battery cannot.

Another view is that capacity is the whole test. It is important, but incomplete. A 74 Wh power bank with a cracked case, swollen pouch or recalled model is not made acceptable by sitting below 100 Wh. Conversely, a sound battery between 101 and 160 Wh may be possible only after the airline approves it.

Regulators also differ slightly in presentation and airlines may be stricter. EASA’s 2025 bulletin says power banks should not be used to charge devices during flight; its public guidance says they should not be connected or recharged on board. The FAA emphasises carriage, capacity and protection, while reminding passengers to check their airline. The sensible response is not to hunt for the most permissive wording. It is to follow the operating carrier’s instructions for the actual flight.

## CONTEXT

The practical check starts at home, where there is time to read small print.

First, turn the power bank over and find its Wh rating. A typical label might show 20,000 mAh and 3.7 V. The calculation is 20 Ah multiplied by 3.7 V, or 74 Wh. Do not calculate with the USB output voltage unless that is how the manufacturer states the battery’s capacity; use the battery rating or ask the maker when the label is unclear.

Second, inspect the object rather than merely the number. Leave behind anything swollen, cracked, leaking, unusually hot, damaged by a fall or subject to a safety recall. The FAA says damaged or recalled batteries likely to spark or produce dangerous heat must not be carried unless made safe under applicable arrangements.

Third, pack each spare so metal cannot bridge its terminals. Original retail packaging, a battery case, terminal tape or a snug protective pouch can do the job. Keep it away from loose keys, coins and other batteries, and place it where luggage cannot crush it.

Fourth, check the airline, especially for large-capacity banks, multiple spares or an itinerary involving several carriers. Approval for 101–160 Wh should be obtained before reaching security, not negotiated beside the gate.

Finally, remember the gate-check trap. A cabin bag can unexpectedly become checked baggage on a full flight. Remove the power bank and all loose lithium batteries before handing the bag over.

## PEOPLE

Rules fail when they do not match the moment in which people need them. At a crowded gate, a traveller may be balancing a coat, passport and tired child when an agent asks for a roller bag. “Remove spare batteries” is easy to miss if the power bank is buried under two days of packing.

A small battery pouch near the top of the bag solves several problems at once. It protects the device, separates it from metal, makes the label available for inspection and lets the traveller retrieve it quickly during a gate check. This is safety designed for ordinary human attention rather than perfect memory.

Mobility equipment and medically necessary devices can follow different provisions, including higher limits and advance arrangements. Those cases deserve direct coordination with the airline; the ordinary power-bank checklist should not be stretched into advice for specialised equipment.

## DEEPER

The rule is really about observability. A battery problem is easier to manage when someone can see, smell or feel the first warning signs and summon people trained to respond. The cabin does not remove the chemistry. It moves the hazard into a monitored system.

That principle explains why switching a power bank off is not enough. A manufacturing defect or internal damage does not require the user to press a button. It also explains why wrapping a battery in clothing inside checked luggage is the wrong kind of protection: padding may prevent scratches, but it hides warning signs and can place combustible material around a heating cell.

During a flight, keep the battery accessible and follow crew and airline instructions about use. EASA advises against charging a power bank or using it to charge another device on board. If it becomes unusually hot, expands, hisses, smells odd, smokes or burns, do not improvise a private solution or carry it elsewhere. Alert cabin crew immediately.

The best pre-flight test therefore has four parts: **identity**—is it a spare lithium battery? **capacity**—what is its Wh rating? **condition**—is it sound and unrecalled? **control**—is it protected and kept where a problem can be seen? A familiar gadget becomes safer when those questions are answered before the suitcase closes.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened:** A preschooler's furious collapse can look like defiance with a plan. More often it is a young system losing access to skills that were only partly built in the first place.

**What it means:** In the peak of a tantrum, a long explanation is usually pitched to an audience that has temporarily left the building. Safety and co-regulation come before teaching.

**Risks and impact:** Treat every outburst as manipulation and adults may add heat, shame or an accidental reward. Treat every outburst as harmless and they may miss persistent aggression, self-injury or developmental concerns.

**What can be done:** Use few words, hold a reasonable boundary, block unsafe behaviour, and reconnect afterward. Prevention often begins with the unglamorous trio of sleep, food and predictable transitions.

**What to watch:** Look for patterns across weeks and settings: triggers, duration, recovery, aggression and what helps. One terrible supermarket afternoon is data, not a diagnosis.

## FACTS

Temper tantrums commonly begin around 18 months and are especially familiar between ages one and three. The NHS links part of the pattern to a simple mismatch: toddlers have strong wants and emotions but limited language for expressing them. As communication and self-regulation develop, tantrums generally become less common; by around age four, they are usually much less frequent.

Common triggers are not mysterious. Hunger, tiredness, frustration, overstimulation and abrupt transitions all reduce the margin a small child has for coping. None of this makes hitting, biting or throwing acceptable. The American Academy of Pediatrics advises adults not to ignore dangerous behaviour, while also warning against changing a clear “no” simply to end the scene.

The practical distinction is between accepting a feeling and permitting an action. “You are angry” can be true. “I will not let you hit” can be true in the same sentence. A boundary does not become cruel because a child dislikes it, and distress does not become calculated just because it is loud.

## EVIDENCE

Current CDC guidance says toddlers and preschoolers can become so overwhelmed by anger or sadness that they cannot yet manage the emotion. During the tantrum, the first jobs are safety, remaining nearby and allowing time and space. Emotion coaching comes after child and adult are calm enough to notice, name and discuss what happened.

Clinical research helps with the harder question: when is a tantrum pattern unusual? A 2008 study compared caregiver reports for 279 children aged three to six, including healthy preschoolers and children with depressive or disruptive disorders. Groups differed in patterns such as violence, self-injury, tantrums outside the home and difficulty recovering.

That finding is not a home diagnostic kit. The study examined group differences in a clinical research sample; it did not establish that one behaviour identifies one disorder. Its useful lesson is narrower: severity, setting and recovery matter more than the mere existence of a tantrum. Repeated patterns deserve attention; a single outburst deserves proportion.

## PERSPECTIVES

One familiar school of advice says to ignore tantrums. Another says to validate every feeling. Both slogans become misleading when stripped of context.

Withholding an argument or an audience can be sensible when a safe child is escalating for attention. It is not sensible to ignore hitting, running into danger or a child who is frightened by their own loss of control. Likewise, validation means recognising the emotional reality—“you wanted to stay”—not approving the demand or negotiating away the limit.

Parents also arrive with different nervous systems, homes and resources. A calm script is easier after sleep than at the end of a double shift. A child with language delay, sensory differences, pain or a major family disruption may need a different level of support. Advice that assumes unlimited time, privacy and serenity tends to collapse at precisely the moment it is needed.

The strongest counterargument to a regulation-first approach is that it can sound permissive. It is permissive only if calm replaces the boundary. Used properly, calm carries the boundary: the adult lowers the temperature without surrendering the rule.

## CONTEXT

A tantrum is often described as a behaviour problem, but its mechanism is partly developmental. Young children are still learning to inhibit an impulse, shift attention, tolerate delay and put internal states into words. Those abilities do not mature together or on schedule. A child may explain a rule beautifully at breakfast and be unable to use it when exhausted at five o'clock.

Adults make a similar mistake when they treat language as proof of capacity. A verbally quick three-year-old can sound like a miniature barrister. That does not mean the child can reason through peak distress like a calm adult. The better sequence is regulate, relate, then reason: first make the moment safe, then restore connection, then teach the skill when working memory has returned.

This is not a one-way transfer in which a perfectly calm adult installs regulation into a child. It is repeated practice in a relationship. At first the adult supplies almost all the structure: naming the transition, lowering stimulation, blocking a blow. Later the child begins to borrow the words, pause for a beat or ask for help. Progress is uneven because the skill competes with fatigue, novelty and temperament. The useful comparison is not with the quiet child at the next table but with this child's own recovery over time. Is the storm shorter? Is aggression less frequent? Can the child accept comfort sooner or use a word before the scream? Those small changes are the mechanism becoming visible.

Culture matters too. Expectations about public noise, obedience and parental authority vary, while bystanders see only the loudest two minutes. That audience can push a parent toward threats or bribes designed to end embarrassment. Unfortunately, relief can train both sides: the child learns escalation changes the outcome, and the adult learns capitulation stops the noise. Consistency is less dramatic and usually more useful.

## PEOPLE

The child is not the only person being regulated. Adults bring their own history to the floor beside the overturned cereal bowl. Someone raised with shouting may hear a toddler's noise as danger; someone punished for tears may feel an urgent need to stop them. Recognising that reaction does not excuse an adult's harmful behaviour. It creates a small choice before the next move.

It also helps to decide what success looks like. Success is not a child who stops crying on command. It is a situation that remains safe, a limit that remains intelligible and an adult who returns after a mistake. If you shout, the repair can be plain: “I was too loud. That was frightening. The rule about hitting still stands, and I will work on saying it safely.” Repair does not erase authority. It shows what accountability looks like when nervous systems are imperfect.

For two-parent or multi-caregiver households, agreement on two or three core responses matters more than identical personalities. Children can manage different voices; what confuses them is a safety limit that disappears whenever the more exhausted adult enters the room.

## DEEPER

The point is not to eliminate anger. It is to help a child move through anger without injury, humiliation or a boundary that changes according to volume. That takes repetition rather than one perfect performance.

A workable sequence is short. First, check safety and move breakable or dangerous objects. Second, regulate your own pace and voice; borrowed calm is still calm. Third, use one sentence for the feeling and one for the limit: “You are furious that playtime ended. I won't let you kick.” Fourth, reduce language and wait. Fifth, after recovery, reconnect and practise the missing skill—asking for one more minute, stomping safely, breathing, or helping with the transition.

For a week or two, keep a tiny pattern log: time, trigger, sleep or hunger, what happened, recovery time and what helped. The purpose is not surveillance. It is to discover whether the “behaviour problem” reliably arrives twenty minutes before dinner or after an unpredictable handover.

Speak with a paediatric or primary-care clinician when you are seriously concerned, especially about repeated self-injury, dangerous aggression, very difficult recovery, loss of skills, or persistent disruption across home and childcare. Bring the pattern log. It is more useful than “always” and “never,” two words that flourish under stress and rarely improve a clinical history.

The durable lesson is modest: a tantrum is not an argument to win. It is a limit to hold and a capacity to build—usually after the storm, not in its middle.

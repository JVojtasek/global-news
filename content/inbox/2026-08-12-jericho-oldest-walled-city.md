## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Nothing today — this is a history piece. Tell es-Sultan, beside a spring near modern Jericho in the Jordan Valley, holds the remains of a settlement with roughly ten thousand years of occupation layers.

**What it means.** People built a stone wall and a stone tower there thousands of years before pottery, writing or cities existed anywhere. Why they did is still argued over.

**Risks and impact.** None personal. The value is perspective: almost everything we call civilisation is younger than that tower.

**What can be done.** The excavation reports are published. Anyone can check which claims about Jericho come from digging and which come from repetition.

**What to watch.** Whether new radiocarbon work narrows the disputed date of the Bronze Age city's destruction. That single number is what most of the argument rests on.

## FACTS

Late afternoon in the Jordan Valley. The air is thick and warm even in winter, because you are standing more than 250 metres below sea level, at the lowest city on earth. Behind you the hills rise brown and empty. In front of you is water: a spring pushing out of the ground, steady, all year, every year, for as long as anyone has been counting.

Around the spring, a mound. Not a hill — a mound made of the rubbish and rubble of people, layer on layer, until the town was living on top of its own ancestors.

📖 **What the sources say:** The book of Joshua describes Jericho as a walled city closed up "because of the Israelites" (Joshua 6:1), entered first by two spies who lodge with a woman named Rahab whose house is in the city wall (Joshua 2). Later biblical texts refer to it as "the city of palm trees".

🏺 **What the evidence supports:** Tell es-Sultan, excavated most influentially by the British archaeologist Kathleen Kenyon in the 1950s, contains occupation layers spanning roughly ten thousand years. It has a stone wall and a stone tower from the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A period, conventionally dated to somewhere around 8000 BC — built by people who had no pottery, no metal, no writing and no wheel.

🎬 **Reconstruction (we are imagining):** The smell of the place. Woodsmoke, animals, wet clay, the dung of penned goats, bread baking on hot stone. None of that is preserved. It is a reasonable guess based on comparable sites, and it is a guess.

That distinction matters here more than almost anywhere else, because Jericho is a site where the digging and the story have been arguing with each other for a hundred years.

## CONTEXT

Start with the tower, because it is the strangest object in the ancient world that almost nobody has heard of.

It is about 8.5 metres high, roughly the same across at the base, built of undressed stones without mortar. Inside is a staircase of twenty-two steps. It was constructed against the inner face of a stone wall that ran around part of the settlement. Both are conventionally dated to around 8000 BC — which is to say more than five thousand years before the first Egyptian pyramid, and getting on for five thousand before the earliest writing.

The obvious reading is defence. A wall, a tower, therefore an enemy. But the obvious reading has problems that specialists have picked at for decades.

There is no clear evidence of large-scale organised warfare in the region at that date, and no other settlement of the period built anything comparable — so who, exactly, was the wall for? The archaeologist Ofer Bar-Yosef argued that the wall makes more sense as protection against flooding and mudflows coming off the slopes, with the tower serving some other purpose entirely. Others have proposed that the tower was a communal or ritual monument: the Israeli researchers Ran Barkai and Roy Liran suggested it was positioned so that the shadow of the nearby mountain fell across it at sunset on the summer solstice, making it a marker in time rather than a lookout.

None of these is proven. What is agreed is the labour. Cutting, hauling and stacking that much stone with no metal tools took a community that could organise itself, feed a workforce, and agree on something for long enough to finish it. That, and not the stone, is the discovery: cooperation on a civic scale, before farming was fully established.

**What they ate.** Charred plant remains from the Neolithic layers point to barley and emmer wheat, lentils, peas, and wild fruits including figs. Meat came at first from hunting — gazelle above all — and later increasingly from herded sheep and goats. The spring made all of it possible. Jericho is essentially an argument about what one reliable water source is worth.

**Who they were.** The most haunting objects from the site are the plastered skulls: human skulls with the flesh removed, the features rebuilt in lime plaster, and shells set into the eye sockets. They belong to the following period, the Pre-Pottery Neolithic B, and several were recovered by Kenyon. They were kept in and under houses, among the living.

**Who they worshipped.** Honestly: we do not know. The skulls are usually interpreted as ancestor veneration, but that is inference from practice, not from any text — there are no texts. Later, in the Bronze Age, Jericho would have shared the religious world of Canaan, with its household deities and its high places. The town's name has been linked by some scholars to the word for moon and by others to the word for fragrance, and neither etymology is settled.

**And the famous claim.** Jericho is routinely called the oldest continuously inhabited city on earth. That should be handled carefully. The occupation is very old and very long, but not unbroken — there are gaps, some of them centuries wide. Damascus, Aleppo and Byblos all have competing claims, resting on different definitions of "city" and "continuous". Jericho's strongest claim is narrower and more interesting: the oldest known town wall.

## PEOPLE

🎬 *Reconstruction (we are imagining, from the material record):* try to hold one life in mind. A woman living in the round mudbrick houses at the foot of that tower, somewhere around 8000 BC.

She has never seen a pot. Cooking is done with hot stones, baskets, skins and plaster-lined pits. She has never met anyone who could write her name, because names are not written anywhere on earth yet. Her horizon of travel is perhaps a few days' walk. The largest structure she has ever seen is the tower, and it was already old when she was born — built by people her grandparents could not have named.

Somewhere under the floor of the room where she sleeps, there may be a skull with plastered features and shell eyes, belonging to someone the household still counts as present.

She is not primitive. She is a member of one of the most successful cooperative experiments in human history, and her community solved a problem — living permanently in one place, in a desert, off a spring — that the rest of the species would take thousands of years to copy.

## DEEPER

Now the hard question, because avoiding it would be dishonest.

Millions of people know Jericho for one thing: a wall that fell down. The account in Joshua 6 describes a march repeated for six days, seven priests with rams' horns, seven circuits on the seventh day, a shout, and a collapse.

Does the archaeology support that? The frank answer is that it depends on a date, and the date is disputed.

John Garstang, digging in the 1930s, identified a destroyed and burned city he dated to around 1400 BC, which fitted one traditional chronology of the conquest. Kathleen Kenyon, using more refined stratigraphic methods twenty years later, concluded that the destruction he had found belonged to the end of the Middle Bronze Age, around 1550 BC, and that in the period around 1400 BC Jericho was small, poor, and possibly not walled at all. Her conclusion became the mainstream position. Her conclusion has been challenged since, most persistently by Bryant Wood, who argued from the pottery that Garstang's date deserved reconsideration. Readers weighing the two should know that Wood works with Associates for Biblical Research, an organisation founded to defend the historicity of the biblical text; that does not make the ceramic argument wrong, but it is part of the picture. Radiocarbon results published in the 1990s have generally supported the earlier, Kenyon-style dating.

So there was a walled Jericho, and it was destroyed by fire. What is contested is when, and therefore who. Even the rubble is read differently: Wood emphasised mudbrick fallen outward at the base of the wall, while Kenyon read the same debris as the collapse of a Middle Bronze Age revetment.

Then there is the grain. Excavation found storage jars still full in the burnt layer. That tells us something real: the destruction was sudden, and did not follow a long siege, because a besieged town eats its stores. It also means whoever destroyed the city did not carry off the most valuable portable commodity in the ancient world, which is unusual behaviour for raiders.

The grain is often cited in support of the Joshua account, and here it is worth being careful in a way that argument usually is not. The layer containing those jars is the layer Kenyon and the radiocarbon work date to around 1550 BC — which is *earlier* than the chronology the conquest reading needs. The grain tells us how the city fell. It does not tell us in which century, and the century is the entire dispute.

That is as far as the evidence goes. A destruction layer records that something ended, not who ended it, and the honest position — held by careful people on both sides — is that Jericho neither proves nor disproves the biblical account. It sits, awkwardly and permanently, in the space where the two do not quite line up.

What the site does offer is something the argument tends to drown out. Ten thousand years of people chose this spot, over and over, for the same unglamorous reason: the water did not stop. Empires, gods, languages and walls came and went above them. The spring is still running today, and the town is still there.

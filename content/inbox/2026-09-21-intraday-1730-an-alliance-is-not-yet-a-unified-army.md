## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Seven Ethiopian armed and opposition groups announced an alliance intended to remove Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed's government and establish a transitional administration.

**What it means.** The list joins movements from several regions and includes former battlefield enemies. It changes the political map, but a declaration is not evidence of a unified army.

**Risks and impact.** If coordination becomes operational, conflicts in Tigray, Amhara and Oromia could become more closely connected, increasing danger for civilians and pressure on the 2022 Pretoria peace agreement.

**What can be done.** Readers can separate the verified founding statement from unverified claims about the alliance's military capacity, territorial reach or foreign support.

**What to watch.** The strongest signals over the next weeks are a named joint command, independently confirmed joint operations and an African Union response to the TPLF's assertion that the Pretoria agreement is no longer valid.

## FACTS

The Ethiopian Peoples' Forces Alliance for Survival published a founding statement dated September 20. It names seven members: the Amhara Fano National Movement, Oromo Liberation Army, Tigray People's Liberation Front, Ogaden National Liberation Front, Afar Revolutionary Democratic Unity Front, Benishangul Peoples' Liberation Movement and Gumuz Peoples' Democratic Movement.

The statement says they will coordinate their organisations and supporters to remove the governing Prosperity Party system and create an inclusive transitional government. It also promises to resolve differences peacefully. Those are the alliance's claims and objectives, not independently established outcomes.

Reuters and Associated Press separately confirmed the announcement. AP reported that a TPLF spokesman described the cooperation as political and military and said joint operations might become necessary. Reuters reported that the groups are geographically dispersed and retain political and territorial differences. Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed's spokesperson told Reuters that the members had already cooperated with Eritrean financial backing; Eritrea has previously denied supporting Ethiopian armed groups. That allegation remains unproven in the public record cited here.

## EVIDENCE

The primary record proves that seven named organisations adopted a common declaration, a shared opponent and a proposed transitional destination. Independent reporting confirms that leaders publicly presented the alliance and that Ethiopia's media regulator reacted by prohibiting outlets from using Tigray TV, which broadcast the announcement, as a source.

The evidence is thinner on capability. No source reviewed here identifies a joint commander, common rules of engagement, pooled logistics or a verified operation conducted by multiple members. Al Jazeera reports that talks took roughly a year, which suggests preparation, but preparation for a declaration is not the same as an integrated field structure.

The sharpest institutional break concerns the TPLF. AP quotes its spokesman saying the 2022 peace agreement is no longer valid. The signed Pretoria agreement committed the federal government and TPLF to a permanent cessation of hostilities and peaceful resolution. One party's declaration does not erase the text, but it is evidence that the political foundation beneath it is failing.

### Where they agree

The alliance is more than a rumour but less than a unified army. Its formation raises the cost of assuming Ethiopia's regional conflicts will remain separate. Whether it becomes a durable force depends on cooperation among groups with different territories, histories and political aims.

## CONTEXT

Ethiopia's recent wars do not fit neatly into two permanent camps. The TPLF fought the federal government from 2020 until the Pretoria agreement in November 2022. Fano forces from Amhara fought alongside federal troops during that war, then turned against the federal government as conflict spread in Amhara. The Oromo Liberation Army has fought federal forces in Oromia.

That history makes the new coalition consequential and fragile. Tigrayan and Amhara actors still dispute Western Tigray. A shared opponent can postpone such disputes; it does not settle them. An earlier common front during the Tigray war also shows that announcing a coalition does not guarantee a lasting postwar political structure.

The Pretoria agreement is narrower than a national settlement: it binds the federal government and TPLF. It called for disarmament, restoration of federal authority, humanitarian access, political dialogue and constitutional treatment of contested areas. Several disputes remained unresolved, and recent clashes have weakened confidence in the arrangement.

This is why the new statement should be read as a warning indicator rather than a forecast of victory. It links conflicts politically, while the military chain connecting them remains unverified.

## DEEPER

Coalitions often begin by naming what their members oppose. That is the easiest sentence to write. The harder sentences describe how authority will be shared, whose losses count, which borders are recognised and what happens when victory revives the disagreement that opposition temporarily concealed.

The alliance's founding text acknowledges this problem by promising peaceful resolution of internal differences. The promise is important precisely because those differences are not theoretical. Former enemies carry memories of war, contested land and incompatible accounts of responsibility.

For observers, a name can create an illusion of solidity. Seven logos appear in one list, so seven command structures begin to look like one. Institutions become real through repeated coordination: people accept decisions they did not make, resources move across old boundaries, and disputes are settled without breaking the whole.

The durable question is therefore not whether the alliance sounds formidable. It is whether former enemies can build rules strong enough to survive their first serious disagreement.

## IMPACT

For civilians, the relevant risk is that political coordination broadens the geography or tempo of conflict. It is too early to claim that has happened. For regional governments and mediators, the alliance complicates a peace architecture built around separate agreements and conflicts.

The practical reading rule is simple: treat the statement as evidence of intent. Treat joint command, shared logistics and independently verified multi-group operations as evidence of capacity. Do not substitute one for the other.

## READER OUTCOME

**Why it matters** — The announcement may connect conflicts previously treated as regional, while the TPLF's position further weakens the Pretoria peace framework. It does not yet demonstrate an integrated national rebellion.

**What to watch, not what to do** — Over the next several weeks, watch for a named command structure, verified joint operations and a formal response from the African Union or Pretoria monitoring mechanisms.

**What would change our minds** — Evidence of pooled logistics and coordinated operations would strengthen the unified-coalition interpretation; public fractures over territory, leadership or strategy would weaken it.

## BRIEFLY

**Stopping a project does not prove that beginning it was foolish.** The original decision may have been reasonable with the information available then, even if the next investment is no longer justified now.

**Past effort matters emotionally but not automatically economically.** Time, money and reputation that cannot be recovered are sunk costs; they should not outweigh better evidence about what another hour or euro can achieve.

**Persistence and flexibility solve different problems.** Determination helps when a difficult goal remains attainable. Goal adjustment helps when the target or route has become unworkable.

**Research does not say that quitting is always healthy.** Studies mainly report associations, and their benefits depend partly on being able to redirect commitment toward meaningful alternatives.

**A clean review separates the person from the plan.** Write a forward-only case for continuing, define a stop rule and name where the freed resources would go before making the choice.

## FACTS

A sunk cost is an investment that cannot be recovered whatever happens next. The train ticket already bought, the six months already spent and the reputation already attached to a launch may all feel relevant. Yet none changes what the available options will cost or return from today onward.

Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer gave the pattern its classic experimental treatment in 1985. Their paper described the sunk-cost effect as a greater tendency to continue after money, effort or time had been invested. The error is not caring about loss. It is treating an unrecoverable loss as a reason to accept avoidable future losses.

That principle does not make every unfinished task a mistake. Completion may preserve trust, teach a skill or unlock a benefit that only appears near the end. Those are future consequences and belong in the decision. The useful question is narrower: if the past investment vanished from the story, would the remaining case still be good enough?

## EVIDENCE

Goal research adds an important distinction between stopping and becoming aimless. In a 2003 paper, Carsten Wrosch and colleagues reported three studies of goal disengagement, reengagement and subjective well-being. The samples included 115 undergraduates, 120 younger and older adults, and 45 parents of children with cancer or medically healthy children. Greater capacity to release unattainable goals and pursue alternatives was associated with higher well-being, with interactions between the two capacities.

A 2007 paper by Wrosch and colleagues used another three studies and examined self-reported health, cortisol rhythms and sleep efficiency. The researchers found associations between better goal disengagement and better self-reported health and more typical daily cortisol patterns. Reengagement was not independently related to the health indicators, but in some analyses it buffered difficulties with disengagement.

These were not instructions randomly assigning people to abandon major life commitments. Much of the evidence was correlational and used varied samples and outcomes. It supports flexibility as a capacity, not a rule that stopping causes health or happiness.

## PERSPECTIVES

The person closest to a project often possesses the best technical knowledge and the strongest motive to defend it. That is not hypocrisy. Ending can threaten identity: the founder who closes a product, the student who changes a degree or the volunteer who retires may hear a practical decision as a moral confession.

Outsiders face the opposite risk. They can call something a sunk cost while overlooking promises, relationships and learning that remain genuinely valuable. A contract may have exit costs. A colleague may depend on a handover. A difficult medical, financial or family decision may require professional advice and shared consent. “Ignore the past” is too crude when present duties were created by past choices.

There is also a difference between abandoning a method and abandoning a value. A community project can close while its records, contacts and remaining funds move to another form of service. A career plan can end without ending the wish to contribute. Reengagement matters because an empty space invites regret to retell the old goal as the only meaningful one.

## CONTEXT

Persistence earns moral praise because many worthwhile outcomes demand effort beyond the point of comfort. That lesson is useful until it becomes a ban on updating. The same culture that celebrates perseverance can make evidence of failure feel like a test of character rather than information.

The sunk-cost idea corrects that imbalance, but it can be misused in the other direction. The fact that earlier spending is irrecoverable does not prove a project is bad. It only removes one invalid argument for continuing. Decision-makers still need estimates of remaining cost, probability of success, opportunity cost, obligations and the value of information gained by one more step.

Timing changes the answer too. Early experiments can be cheap and reversible; late commitments can affect employees, partners or patients. A good stop rule is therefore not a dramatic threshold invented during a crisis. It is a condition set while thinking is calm: a date, budget, performance measure or new fact that triggers a formal review.

Seen this way, stopping is part of stewardship. It protects scarce attention for goals that are still alive. The past is not erased; it becomes evidence, experience and sometimes grief, rather than an invoice the future must keep paying.

## DEEPER

### Write a continuation memo

Begin with one sentence: “From today, continuing will require…” List only future time, money, risk and obligations. Then write the strongest current evidence that the project can still achieve its purpose. Do not include effort already spent as a benefit.

Next, compare three options, not two: continue unchanged, change the route, or stop and transfer the resources. For each, name the likely gain, worst credible downside and next review date. A smaller experiment may be better than either blind persistence or an irreversible exit.

Set one observable stop rule. It might be no paying customer after a defined trial, no clinical improvement by a clinician-agreed review, or no feasible way to meet a promised standard within the remaining budget. A rule is useful only if the people involved know it before more resources are committed.

Finally, name a replacement destination. Where will the next ten hours, the remaining funds or the recovered attention go? Ask a person who is informed but not rewarded by continuation to challenge both the estimates and the proposed exit. If the forward-only case survives that review, persistence has a reason. If it does not, stopping need not rewrite the beginning as shame. It records that the evidence changed—and the decision changed with it.

## REFLECT

Which unfinished commitment in your life is defended mainly by what it has already cost? What present obligation would remain even if you stopped? And what specific goal would receive the next unit of time, money or care?

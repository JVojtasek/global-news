## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Ubisoft shut down the servers for its 2014 racing game The Crew, and because the game required a permanent online connection, purchased copies stopped working entirely — not just the multiplayer parts. The shutdown fed a consumer campaign called Stop Killing Games, which has since gathered enough support to force a formal response from European Union lawmakers.

**What it means.** A purchase that looked like owning a game turned out to be a licence that could be switched off. That gap between what people think they bought and what the terms of sale actually promised is now a live regulatory question, not just a fan grievance.

**Risks and impact.** Anyone with a library of always-online games is exposed to the same risk: the game stays "owned" on paper while becoming unplayable in practice, with no resale value and no guaranteed refund.

**What can be done.** Check before buying whether a game needs a permanent server connection, and support the growing practice of publishers releasing offline patches or private-server tools when they end official support.

**What to watch.** Whether the European Commission's formal response to the petition leads to any binding requirement that publishers provide an end-of-life option, or whether it stays a recommendation with no teeth.

## FACTS

The Crew was a racing game Ubisoft released in 2014 that required players to stay connected to its servers at all times, even to drive alone with no other players present. In 2024, Ubisoft shut those servers down for good. Owners who had paid for the game found it would no longer start at all — not a loss of extra features, but a complete loss of a product they had bought outright.

The shutdown became the rallying point for a campaign called Stop Killing Games, started by the YouTuber Ross Scott. Its core demand is narrow and specific: when a publisher ends support for a game, it should leave players with some way to keep playing — an offline mode, a patch removing the server requirement, or licensed tools to run a private server — rather than switching the product off entirely.

The campaign filed a European Citizens' Initiative, a formal petition mechanism that obliges the European Commission to give a substantive response once it collects at least one million verified signatures from across a minimum number of EU member states. Stop Killing Games passed that threshold, which is why a fan complaint about one racing game has turned into an item EU institutions are now required to formally address.

## EVIDENCE

What is well documented: The Crew required a permanent server connection by design, its servers were shut down, and previously purchased copies stopped functioning as a direct result. Ubisoft's own terms of sale, like those of most digital storefronts, describe the purchase as a licence to use the software under stated conditions rather than a transfer of ownership of the underlying code — this licence-not-ownership structure is standard across the digital games industry, not unique to Ubisoft.

What is genuinely contested: whether requiring an end-of-life plan is technically and financially reasonable for publishers. The games industry has argued that releasing server code or building offline modes after the fact carries real engineering cost, and can create security and liability exposure the original design never accounted for. Preservation advocates argue that cost is a business decision publishers should plan for before launch, not an excuse available after the fact.

What remains unresolved: what the European Commission will actually do with the petition. A Citizens' Initiative guarantees a response, not a change in law. Similar past petitions have led to everything from new legislation to a written explanation of why the Commission declined to act, so the campaign's ultimate effect on consumer rights is not yet settled.

## PERSPECTIVES

Consumer advocates frame this as a truth-in-advertising problem: if a storefront sells something using the language of buying, a reasonable customer expects to keep it, and switching that off after the fact is closer to renting under a different name. This framing has broad public sympathy but sidesteps the fact that most storefronts do disclose licence terms, even if few buyers read them.

Publishers frame ongoing server costs as the real constraint: an always-online game generates real infrastructure bills for as long as it runs, and no company can commit to funding that indefinitely. From this view, shutting a service down is not betrayal but the ordinary end of a service's operating life, no different from a gym closing or a magazine ceasing publication.

Preservation researchers, building on the same institutional work covered in an earlier piece on who saves old video games, frame the dispute as evidence that the industry's preservation problem is not only about old, out-of-print titles in museum archives. It is also about recent, actively-sold games that can vanish overnight for reasons that have nothing to do with age or rarity — a much faster version of the same underlying issue.

## CONTEXT

The "you don't own it, you licence it" model is not new; it has applied to most commercial software since the 1980s and predates video games entirely. What changed is how visible the gap between licence and ownership has become. A single-player disc from the 1990s kept working after the publisher moved on, because nothing about playing it required the company's continued cooperation. An always-online game does require that cooperation, indefinitely, and the industry has moved toward that model partly to fight piracy and partly because live services can generate ongoing revenue that a one-time disc sale cannot.

The Crew is not an isolated case. Digital storefronts have removed purchased films and shows from customer libraries over licensing disputes in the past, and other online-only games have been discontinued with little more than a shutdown notice. What made The Crew unusual was the scale of public reaction and the decision to escalate the complaint into a formal EU petition rather than leave it as forum posts and reviews.

Europe is a natural venue for this fight because EU consumer-protection law already treats some aspects of digital sales more strictly than many other jurisdictions, including disclosure rules around digital content. Whether that existing framework stretches to cover "your paid game must remain playable somehow" is exactly the question the Commission has now been asked to answer.

## PEOPLE

Ross Scott built his following making video essays about old and obscure software long before this campaign, which is part of why Stop Killing Games reads as continuous with the preservation instinct rather than a one-off protest. The people who signed the petition are not, for the most part, arguing that Ubisoft owed them a working multiplayer mode forever. Many say they would have accepted losing online features and kept playing offline — the objection is to losing the game outright.

## DEEPER

Underneath the legal argument is an older question about what it means to own something in a world where more and more of what we buy needs permission, from somewhere else, every time we use it. A book on a shelf answers to no one once it is yours. A game that must phone home to a server answers to the company that built the phone line, for as long as that company chooses to keep it open.

That is not automatically sinister — plenty of useful things genuinely require ongoing maintenance, from apartment buildings to medical devices, and nobody expects those to run forever unsupported for free. The discomfort here is narrower: it is the mismatch between the language of the transaction, "buy now," and the reality of what changed hands, a conditional permission. People are not typically angry about paying for a subscription they know is a subscription. They are angry about discovering, after the fact, that something sold to them as a purchase behaved like one all along.

## REFLECT

- Think of something you have "bought" recently that only works because a company keeps a server running somewhere. Did you know that when you paid for it?
- If a service you rely on told you today it would shut down in a year, would you rather it vanish completely, or hand you a worse, offline version to keep?

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Nothing today — this is an explainer built around a well-known finding in social science. A 1973 study by the sociologist Mark Granovetter found that people were more likely to have learned about their current job from someone they saw only occasionally than from a close friend or family member.

**What it means.** Close friends and family tend to know the same people and hear the same news you do, so they rarely surface anything genuinely new. Acquaintances move in different circles, so they are more likely to be the ones who mention the opening, the introduction or the piece of information you didn't already have.

**Risks and impact.** Leaning entirely on your closest circle for advice, opportunities or new information can leave you circulating inside the same small pool of ideas without noticing.

**What can be done.** Deliberately maintain a wider circle of looser connections — former colleagues, classmates, neighbours — even though it takes less obvious effort than nurturing close friendships.

**What to watch.** Whether you can name five people you'd call an acquaintance, not a close friend, whom you've had a real conversation with in the past year. If the honest answer is close to zero, that is the gap the research points at.

## FACTS

In 1973, the sociologist Mark Granovetter published a paper called "The Strength of Weak Ties," built partly on a survey of professional, technical and managerial workers in a Boston-area suburb who had recently changed jobs through a personal contact rather than a formal application. The pattern he found was counterintuitive: most of these people had learned about the job from someone they described seeing only occasionally, not from someone they saw often.

Granovetter's explanation rests on the structure of social networks rather than anything about honesty or generosity. He argued that close relationships — strong ties — tend to cluster: your best friends often know each other and know largely the same people and the same news that you do. Weaker relationships — acquaintances, old classmates, a friend of a friend — are more likely to be what network researchers call a bridge: the only connection between your circle and an entirely different one, carrying information that would otherwise never reach you.

The paper became one of the most cited works in the social sciences, and its core idea outlived the original 1970s job-market study it was based on. It now gets applied well beyond employment, to how ideas, gossip, diseases and political movements spread through populations, precisely because the same bridging logic applies to information of almost any kind.

## EVIDENCE

What is well supported: the structural argument — that dense clusters of close ties circulate redundant information while weaker ties are more likely to bridge into new information — is a robust and widely replicated finding in network science, not a one-off result from a single 1970s survey.

What has been tested more rigorously since: a large-scale study conducted with a major professional networking platform, published in a leading scientific journal in 2022, used a randomized experiment rather than a survey, deliberately varying how many weak-tie connections different users were shown, and found that stronger exposure to weak ties causally increased job mobility. That matters because a survey can only show correlation — people with active acquaintance networks might simply be more career-active people generally — while a randomized experiment can show the tie itself is doing some of the work.

What is less settled: how far the finding generalizes outside job-seeking, to areas like emotional support, health outcomes or long-term wellbeing, where close ties plausibly matter more than weak ones for different reasons. The "weak ties win" finding is strongest for the spread of novel information specifically, and weakest as a general claim about which relationships matter most in life.

## PERSPECTIVES

Career advisors tend to treat the finding as a practical instruction: network deliberately, keep loose connections warm, don't let a LinkedIn contact go cold. This is a fair reading of the data but can flatten a structural insight about social networks into a transactional to-do list, which risks making people perform friendliness toward acquaintances purely for what they might provide.

Sociologists studying community and isolation read the same finding differently: as evidence that a healthy social life needs range, not just depth. A person can have several genuinely close friends and still be, in Granovetter's terms, informationally isolated if every one of those friends moves in the same narrow circle. From this view the finding is less about career hacking and more about the architecture of a person's whole social world.

A more skeptical reading points out that weak ties are unevenly available to begin with. People with more free time, more social confidence, or jobs that naturally generate loose professional contact have an easier time building the kind of wide, weak network the research favours. The advice to "network more" lands very differently for someone working two jobs with little spare time than for someone in a role built around meeting new people.

## CONTEXT

Granovetter's original 1973 study predates social media by decades, which makes its endurance somewhat unusual for a piece of 1970s sociology. Part of the reason is that platforms built explicitly around maintaining large numbers of loose contacts — professional networking sites in particular — turned his theoretical claim into something measurable at a much larger scale than a single Boston suburb, which is exactly what the 2022 randomized study exploited.

It is worth separating this from simple "networking" advice as popularly understood, because the mechanism is specifically about non-redundant information, not about the number of contacts a person has. A person with a thousand loose contacts who all know each other gains far less than a person with ten acquaintances scattered across genuinely different worlds — a workplace, a hobby group, a hometown, a professional association.

The finding also has a quieter, less career-focused cousin: research on urban life has long noted that casual, low-stakes interactions with acquaintances and even strangers — a regular barista, a dog-walking neighbour, a familiar face at the gym — contribute to a sense of belonging and daily wellbeing in ways separate from, and not replaced by, a person's closest relationships.

## DEEPER

There is a short, blunt piece of wisdom literature tucked into Ecclesiastes that has nothing to do with job markets and still lands close to the same point: "Two are better than one, because they have a good return for their labor: if either of them falls down, one can help the other up. But pity anyone who falls and has no one to help them up" (Ecclesiastes 4:9–10). The passage goes on to note that a cord of three strands is not easily broken — the image scales, deliberately, from a pair to something larger.

It would be a stretch to say this text is describing weak-tie network theory; it is plainly about companionship and mutual support, not information bridges between professional clusters. But the underlying instinct rhymes: a life built around exactly one kind of connection, however deep, is more fragile than one with range. The Ecclesiastes passage argues for more than one strand of relationship for resilience when you fall. Granovetter's research argues for more than one kind of relationship for reach, when you're looking for something you don't yet have.

Both point at the same quiet mistake: treating your closest circle as if it were your whole social world, when it was never built to be. The people who see you every day are, almost by definition, the people least likely to know something you don't already know.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** A persistent safety claim says carbon monoxide is heavy and therefore every household alarm belongs close to the floor.

**What it means.** Carbon monoxide is slightly lighter than air and, in a room, diffusion and ordinary air movement distribute it rather than preserving a tidy low layer.

**Risks and impact.** Arguing about one universal height can distract from failures that matter more: missing levels, an alarm too far from sleeping people, blocked devices, exhausted sensors or absent battery backup.

**What can be done.** Follow the instructions for the exact alarm, install coverage on every level and outside sleeping areas, test it as directed and record its replacement date.

**What to watch.** If an alarm sounds, leave for fresh air and call emergency services; do not search the home for the source or re-enter until responders allow it.

## FACTS

Carbon monoxide, or CO, is a colourless and odourless gas produced when fuel burns. Furnaces, generators, fireplaces, grills, vehicles and other fuel-burning equipment can produce it. The [CDC’s January 2026 guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html) lists headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion among common symptoms. High exposure can cause unconsciousness or death, and a sleeping person may not notice symptoms in time.

The familiar placement argument starts with a mistaken picture: CO as a heavy gas collecting across the floor like water. Its molecular mass is actually slightly lower than the average for air. More importantly, a lived-in room is not a still jar. Warm exhaust, heating systems, drafts, doors and human movement mix gases.

The [U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission](https://www.cpsc.gov/safety-education/safety-guides/carbon-monoxide/carbon-monoxide-fact-sheet) therefore does not prescribe one universal height. It says to follow the alarm manufacturer’s instructions. Depending on the listed product, an alarm may plug into a low receptacle or mount high on a wall. CPSC recommends alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, with battery backup for plug-in or hard-wired models.

## EVIDENCE

A small but unusually direct experiment tested the height question. Neil Hampson, Todd Courtney and James Holm placed monitors at the top, middle and bottom of an eight-foot airtight chamber, then introduced CO at each of those heights in separate trials. As reported in the [Journal of Emergency Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21536403/), concentrations equalized throughout the chamber every time. The authors concluded that normal home drafts and temperature differences would mix it faster, making any room height reasonable for a residential alarm.

That study is not a model of every house, fire or faulty appliance. Its value is narrower: it tests and rejects the claim that molecular weight makes CO form a stable floor or ceiling layer. Installation guidance supplies the practical half of the evidence. CPSC explicitly allows plug-in placement or high wall placement, while warning against heating vents, furniture or draperies that can obstruct an alarm, kitchens and positions directly above fuel-burning appliances.

Independent safety bodies converge on coverage rather than a magic height. The [National Fire Protection Association](https://www.nfpa.org/education-and-research/home-fire-safety/carbon-monoxide) calls for a central location outside each sleeping area and alarms on every level, plus other locations required by law, code or manufacturer instructions. The precise rules can vary by jurisdiction and model. That is why the manual is part of the safety system, not packaging to discard.

## PERSPECTIVES

The “CO sinks” rule is attractive because it is concrete. It turns an invisible hazard into a household picture and offers a single action. Its weakness is that the picture is wrong. Moving a correctly listed plug-in alarm from a low outlet merely to obey an internet rule may add no protection and may violate its instructions.

The opposite shortcut — “CO rises, so put every alarm on the ceiling” — makes the same error with the sign reversed. Warm combustion gases may initially rise, but the alarm is intended to detect CO after it enters the room’s moving air. Combination smoke-and-CO alarms can legitimately be ceiling mounted when their listing and instructions permit it. A dedicated unit may specify another position.

Official guidance frames placement as a system. Coverage must reach every storey and sleeping area. The alarm needs to be audible, powered, unobstructed and within its working life. Fuel-burning appliances and vents need maintenance. A detector adds warning; it does not make an indoor generator, charcoal grill or car in an attached garage safe.

Manufacturers necessarily frame the question product by product because sensors, housings and testing conditions differ. Local authorities frame it through codes because housing layouts and legal duties differ. Those are not signs that physics is undecided. They are reasons a universal social-media mounting rule is the wrong tool.

## CONTEXT

CO harms by binding to haemoglobin, reducing the blood’s ability to carry oxygen. Symptoms can resemble flu without fever, fatigue or food poisoning. That ambiguity makes an audible warning valuable, especially at night. The CDC reports that in the United States more than 400 people die each year from unintentional, non-fire CO poisoning, more than 100,000 visit emergency departments and more than 14,000 are hospitalized.

An alarm is timed protection, not a laboratory meter promised to sound at the first molecule. Product standards are designed to warn before potentially life-threatening exposure, while reducing nuisance alarms. The test button usually checks circuitry, not the accuracy of the chemical sensor. Sensors also age. CPSC advises replacing a unit at the age specified in its literature or when its end-of-life signal activates; the CDC tells consumers to follow manufacturer instructions.

The practical audit takes five minutes. Count levels, including the route to bedrooms. Confirm an alarm is outside each separate sleeping area and add locations required by local code or the manufacturer. Check that furniture, curtains and vents do not interfere. Find the manufacture or replace-by date. Use the test procedure in the manual. Confirm battery backup where applicable. Then put the next test and replacement check on a calendar.

Prevention remains upstream. The CDC recommends annual professional service for heating systems, water heaters and other gas-, oil- or coal-burning appliances. Generators belong outdoors, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents. Charcoal does not belong indoors. A gas oven is not a room heater.

## PEOPLE

Placement advice has to work for a person waking from sleep, not just for a diagram. That is why guidance emphasizes the area outside bedrooms and why interconnected alarms can help: a device near the source is of little use if nobody hears it.

Risk is not evenly distributed. The CDC says infants, older adults and people with chronic heart disease, anaemia or breathing problems are more likely to become ill. People who are asleep or impaired may die before recognizing symptoms. Renters may also be unable to service a furnace or change a hard-wired system themselves, but they can document a missing, expired or sounding alarm and contact the property manager or local authority.

When an alarm sounds, the human task is deliberately simple. CPSC says not to investigate. Move everyone outside, call emergency services, account for the household and wait for permission before going back in.

## DEEPER

Safety myths often survive because they compress a system into an object. Find the correct height, buy the correct gadget, press the button: problem solved. Real protection is less satisfying. It is a chain in which each link is ordinary — coverage, instructions, power, maintenance, an exit response — and failure can hide between them.

The floor myth also shows how a true-sounding scientific fact can become bad advice. Density does affect gases. But the fact that matters in a home is not a molecule’s mass in isolation. It is what happens amid heat, diffusion and moving air, combined with how a tested device was designed to operate. “Scientific” advice without the operating context is often just a detail wearing authority.

A useful rule for household safety is to ask three questions. What hazard is the device meant to detect? Under what tested conditions does this particular device work? What behaviour must follow its warning? For CO, those answers lead away from the floor-versus-ceiling argument and toward a complete loop: prevent combustion problems, place listed alarms as instructed, make sure sleeping people can hear them, and leave when they sound.

The best safety rule is not always the most memorable sentence. Sometimes it is the habit of keeping the whole chain intact.

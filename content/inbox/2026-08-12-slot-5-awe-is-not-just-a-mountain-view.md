## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Researchers increasingly study awe as an emotion that can arise when something feels vast and stretches our usual understanding.

**What it means.** Awe is not reserved for mountains, cathedrals or expensive journeys. Music, scientific ideas, skilled human cooperation, moral courage and details in an ordinary street can also shift attention beyond the self.

**Risks and impact.** Small experiments suggest that deliberately noticing awe may improve some positive emotions. They do not prove that awe treats depression, slows time or produces lasting change for everyone.

**What can be done.** Try one 15-minute walk with the phone away. Look for scale, pattern, history or an unexpected act of care, and notice what makes your first explanation feel too small.

**What to watch.** Judge the experiment by changes in attention and curiosity, not by whether it creates a dramatic feeling or fixes a health problem.

## FACTS

You leave home to buy milk. The route is familiar enough to walk without thinking: the same cracked pavement, the same bus stop, the same line of plane trees beside the road. Then late sunlight catches hundreds of leaves at once. For a few seconds, the trees stop being street furniture. You notice their height, their age and the hidden work of roots under the concrete.

Nothing supernatural has happened. You have not travelled anywhere. Your attention has simply met something larger than its usual frame.

Psychologists often describe awe using two ideas: perceived vastness and a need for “accommodation.” Accommodation means revising the mental boxes through which we understand an experience. Vastness may be physical, but it can also be social or intellectual. A night sky can do it. So can a choir, a difficult equation, coordinated movement in a crowd or watching somebody act with unusual courage.

That makes awe more available than its tourism-advert image suggests. The scientific question is not whether people report the feeling. It is which effects survive careful measurement, for whom, and for how long.

## EVIDENCE

One practical experiment involved 60 healthy older adults. In the [randomised awe-walk study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8034841/), both groups took weekly 15-minute outdoor walks for eight weeks, but one group received instructions to seek awe. Compared with the control walkers, they reported more awe during walks, more prosocial positive emotions and declining daily distress. Their photographs also placed the self progressively smaller within the scene.

The limits matter. Anxiety, depression and life-satisfaction scores did not significantly change from baseline in either group. The study tested a small, older sample and relied partly on self-report. It supports a low-cost attention exercise, not a universal mental-health treatment.

A 2025 randomised trial found reduced stress and depressive symptoms and improved wellbeing after an online awe intervention. But its 68 participants all had long COVID, most were women, and anxiety did not significantly improve. That result is promising for the population studied. It should not be expanded into “awe cures depression.”

The most defensible conclusion is modest: directed attention can increase the experience of awe, and some short-term emotional outcomes may improve. Durability and generalisation remain open questions.

## PERSPECTIVES

The wellness frame presents awe as a tool: take an awe walk, feel better, become kinder. This frame is attractive because the activity is cheap, gentle and easy to try. It correctly notices that attention is trainable. It overreaches when a small intervention becomes a prescription for illness or a promise of transformation.

The luxury frame treats awe as something delivered by scale and distance: glaciers, temples, launches, eclipses. Those experiences can be powerful, but this framing quietly turns wonder into a product. It overlooks music through headphones, an old tree behind a supermarket, a microscope image or the patience of a carer.

The sceptical frame asks whether researchers are measuring awe or simply expectation, novelty and positive mood. That challenge is necessary. Laboratory videos, questionnaires and culturally narrow samples cannot capture every form of the emotion. Yet dismissing the whole field would also ignore repeated evidence that awe has recognisable features and can shift attention.

A better frame is experimental rather than devotional. Awe is not automatically good and is not always pleasant. Storms, crowds and powerful leaders can evoke threatening awe. The useful question is narrower: what changes when attention encounters something that makes the self feel less central and the world less fully explained?

## CONTEXT

The phrase “small self” can sound like an invitation to low self-worth. In this research it means something different: a temporary change in scale. The self remains present, but it occupies less of the mental picture. Worries may not disappear; they share the frame with something larger.

That outward shift is one proposed route from awe to prosocial feeling. If attention moves away from constant self-monitoring, other people and wider systems may become easier to notice. But the route is a theory supported by partial evidence, not a settled biological mechanism.

Time offers a useful lesson in caution. Popular accounts often say awe makes time expand. Two experiments by van Elk and Rotteveel successfully induced awe with videos but found no consistent effect on implicit or retrospective time perception. A later three-experiment paper by Droit-Volet and colleagues did find distortions in duration judgements for some awe-provoking images. Different stimuli and measurements produced different answers.

Feeling that more time is available is not the same as judging whether a vibration lasted 500 milliseconds. Awe may alter attention, memory or impatience without changing every clock-like estimate. When studies disagree, the responsible response is not to select the prettier result. It is to narrow the claim.

Culture also shapes what counts as vast, admirable or frightening. A sacred building, a scientific explanation or collective ritual will not carry the same meaning for everyone. The emotion may be widespread; its doorway is personal and social.

## DEEPER

An awe walk needs no special landscape. Give it 15 minutes and one rule: do not hunt for a feeling. Hunt for something your attention normally edits out.

Look upward. Notice the age of a wall, the geometry of fire escapes, cloud layers or the way several birds turn as if sharing one thought. Listen for separate instruments in a familiar song. Read one paragraph about a scale that is difficult to imagine—the age of a fossil, the distance to a star, the number of cells cooperating inside a body.

Then ask two questions. What here is larger, more intricate or more capable than I usually admit? What part of my explanation needs to grow?

If nothing happens, the walk has not failed. Awe cannot be commanded like a timer. The quieter benefit may be noticing how aggressively a hurried mind reduces the world to obstacles, labels and errands.

There are also boundaries. Do not use wonder to deny pain, postpone medical care or tell an exhausted person to improve their attitude. A beautiful sky does not change an unsafe job or repair a relationship. It can, at most, interrupt the claim that the present problem is the whole horizon.

On the walk home, the cracked pavement is still cracked. The milk still needs buying. Yet the trees have recovered their height.

That may be awe's most honest gift: not an escape from an ordinary life, but the return of scale to it.

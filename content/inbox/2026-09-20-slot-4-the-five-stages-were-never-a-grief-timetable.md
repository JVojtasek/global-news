## BRIEFLY

**The familiar five stages did not begin as a timetable for bereavement.** Elisabeth Kübler-Ross developed her language while interviewing and observing dying patients in the late 1960s.

**Even that account was more flexible than the classroom diagram.** Her foundation says she described many responses beyond five and did not intend a rigid sequence; people could skip, repeat or experience several at once.

**Popular use turned description into prescription.** Bereaved people were told to expect denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance, sometimes as proof that they were progressing properly.

**Research does not support a universal five-step course.** Reviews find wide variation across people and time, with grief often arriving in waves and coexisting with positive feeling, ordinary functioning and continuing bonds.

**The practical alternative is attention without grading.** Ask what the person is experiencing, what support they want and whether daily life has become persistently impaired—not which stage they have reached.

## FACTS

Kübler-Ross’s 1969 book *On Death and Dying* grew from clinical conversations with people facing terminal illness. The five labels that became famous were denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance. They offered language for responses that medicine had often ignored while avoiding candid conversation with dying patients.

That history matters because a model for experiences near death was later widely presented as a map for survivors after a death. The Elisabeth Kübler-Ross Foundation now emphasises that her formulations changed between 1968 and 1974. She also wrote about shock, anxiety, hope, guilt, loneliness, relief and other responses. Hope, in particular, could remain alongside fear, anger, depression or acceptance.

The foundation states that the stages were descriptive rather than prescriptive and not a fixed order. By 1974, Kübler-Ross was explicitly describing two or three responses occurring together. The tidy staircase often reproduced in training slides is therefore not a faithful summary even of her evolving account.

## EVIDENCE

In 2017, bereavement researchers Margaret Stroebe, Henk Schut and Kathrin Boerner reviewed the stage model’s scientific and practical value. They concluded that there was little evidence for a sequential development of the five reactions and warned that prescribing stages can make mourners feel they are coping incorrectly. Their historical review traced the original work to conversations with more than 200 dying patients, not a systematic longitudinal study of bereaved people.

A 2007 JAMA study by Paul Maciejewski and colleagues did measure several grief indicators over time and was presented as an empirical examination of stage theory. But the indicators did not simply reproduce Kübler-Ross’s original five labels—yearning replaced bargaining—and later critiques challenged the claim that average peaks established an obligatory sequence for individuals. An average curve can conceal many different personal trajectories.

Sidney Zisook and Katherine Shear’s 2009 clinical review states the broader conclusion plainly: no stage theory accounts for how people cope with loss, why distress differs or when adjustment occurs. Grief varies in intensity and duration and commonly proceeds in fits and starts.

## PERSPECTIVES

The model survives because it answers a compassionate question: What happens next? A numbered sequence promises orientation when time has lost its ordinary shape. Its labels can also provide recognition. Someone who feels anger or numb disbelief may be relieved to learn that such reactions are not uniquely shameful.

The harm comes from converting recognition into requirement. A mourner who does not feel anger may wonder whether emotion is being suppressed. A person who laughs with friends may feel disloyal. A family may pressure someone toward “acceptance” because the calendar has advanced. Supporters may wait for a stage to pass instead of asking what would help today.

Researchers have proposed alternatives—trajectories, tasks, meaning-making, continuing bonds and a dual-process model that describes movement between attending to the loss and attending to changed life. None should become a new mandatory staircase. Their value is in making variation intelligible: remembering and rebuilding can alternate rather than replace each other.

There is also a clinical boundary to preserve. Rejecting a universal schedule does not mean that every level of suffering should simply be left alone. The American Psychiatric Association distinguishes ordinary grief from prolonged grief disorder through persistent, severe distress and impairment, not failure to complete five stages.

## CONTEXT

The durable achievement of Kübler-Ross’s work was larger than its most famous diagram. It challenged a medical culture in which dying patients could be isolated by euphemism, false reassurance or silence. Treating patients as teachers restored voice and relationship to end-of-life care. That legacy does not depend on proving five sequential stages.

The model’s afterlife shows how ideas change when they travel. A nuanced clinical vocabulary became a memory aid. The memory aid became a chart. The chart became an expectation. Each step made the concept easier to teach and harder to qualify. By the time a bereaved person encountered it, historical observation could sound like a personal deadline.

Evidence moved in a different direction. Longitudinal work identified multiple grief trajectories rather than one normal arc. The 2017 review cites a study in which 46 per cent of participants followed a low-distress, resilient pattern, while only 11 per cent showed the high-then-declining distress pattern often assumed to be common. Those labels do not rank love or measure the importance of the person who died. Visible distress and attachment are not interchangeable quantities.

Diagnostic thinking also changed. Prolonged grief disorder entered the DSM-5-TR in March 2022 after decades of research and review. For adults, the APA requires that the death occurred at least a year earlier; for children and adolescents, at least six months. The criteria also require persistent symptoms, distress or functional problems, and attention to social, cultural and religious norms. A duration threshold is not a deadline to stop grieving. It is one safeguard in deciding when a specific disabling pattern may need clinical care.

## PEOPLE

Elisabeth Kübler-Ross made an institution listen to people it had often spoken around. That contribution is compatible with revising the model attached to her name. Indeed, her foundation’s current explanation returns attention to the complexity in her own books: more than five responses, overlap, movement and no single classical pattern.

Stroebe, Schut and Boerner represent the next turn in the memory of the idea. Their criticism is not that denial, anger or acceptance never occur. It is that experiences do not become universal stages merely because their names are familiar. Their concern is practical as well as academic: a simple chart can become a standard against which real people are judged.

Remembering both contributions gives a more useful history. Kübler-Ross helped make dying speakable. Later research helped make grieving less prescriptive.

## DEEPER

### Replace the stage question

When supporting someone, replace “Which stage are you in?” with three smaller questions. What is hardest right now? What kind of contact or practical help would be welcome? What part of the day has become difficult to manage? Accept “I don’t know” and do not require a coherent story.

Notice function without policing emotion. Eating, sleeping, safety, caregiving, work and the ability to make basic decisions may reveal needs that a stage label misses. Offer a specific action—a meal, transport, paperwork, quiet company—rather than a theory of where the person should be.

Do not treat an anniversary, a calm week or renewed grief as a reset. Zisook and Shear describe grief as coming in waves, often reawakened by holidays, birthdays, another loss or stress. A continuing bond through stories, objects or rituals can coexist with a life that is moving again.

For your own reflection, record changes without turning them into grades: what is newly possible, what still hurts, what returns unexpectedly and what connection remains. The entries may form a pattern without forming a staircase.

If grief remains intense and disabling, or if safety is in question, encourage qualified help. That is not because the person has failed to reach acceptance. It is because evidence-based care exists for patterns that are persistent, painful and disruptive.

## REFLECT

Has a simple model ever helped you name an experience but then begun to judge it? When you offer support, are you listening for the person’s actual need or matching their words to a familiar chart? What would change if grief were understood less as a route to finish and more as a relationship to carry differently?

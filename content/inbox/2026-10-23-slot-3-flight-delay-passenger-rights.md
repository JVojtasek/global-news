## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** EU, UK and US rules all give travellers specific cash or refund entitlements when a flight is badly delayed, cancelled or significantly changed. Many eligible passengers never use them.

**What it means.** The gap between entitlement and claim is not a legal mystery. The airline is unlikely to raise the subject, and the passenger who does not ask usually gets nothing.

**Risks and impact.** Direct money: €250 to €600 per passenger on qualifying EU routes, £220 to £520 on UK ones, and in the US an automatic refund in many cases since 2024.

**What can be done.** Keep three documents, use the airline's own form first, and if the reply is a refusal, escalate to the regulator or an approved dispute scheme before thinking about court.

**What to watch.** Whether the EU's long-running revision of Regulation 261 finally lands and changes the delay thresholds.

## FACTS

Three parallel systems protect air passengers on the routes most readers of this site are likely to fly.

**EU Regulation 261/2004.** It covers any flight departing from an airport in the EU, Iceland, Norway or Switzerland, and flights by an EU airline arriving there. If the delay at arrival is three hours or more, compensation is €250 for flights up to 1,500 km, €400 for longer flights within the EU and other flights of 1,500–3,500 km, and €600 for flights over 3,500 km. Cancellations with less than 14 days' notice work similarly. The airline must also provide meals and, where needed, a hotel during the wait.

The only escape clause is "extraordinary circumstances": severe weather, air-traffic control decisions, security risks, strikes outside the airline. Routine technical faults generally do not count, and the airline has to prove its excuse.

**UK261.** After Brexit the UK kept the same rules in sterling: £220, £350 and £520. The Civil Aviation Authority (CAA) oversees them. They cover flights leaving UK airports and UK-airline flights arriving in the UK.

**US Department of Transportation.** A DOT rule announced in April 2024 requires airlines to refund cash automatically for cancellations, significant changes and long delays — three hours or more on domestic flights, six on international ones — when the passenger declines the alternative offered. The refund goes back to the original payment method, within seven business days for card payments. There is no fixed compensation on top. The refund is what you paid, no more.

## EVIDENCE

The regulations and the DOT rule are public and kept current on the regulators' own sites: the European Commission's Your Europe portal, the UK CAA, and DOT's Fly Rights page. These are the primary sources. Commercial claim sites that offer to chase your money for a cut are summaries, not independent law.

What converges across all three systems: the entitlement is yours by default, not by negotiation.

What differs: the EU and UK pay a fixed lump sum per passenger; the US refunds the fare. On a cheap short hop in Europe, compensation can exceed the ticket price. On an expensive last-minute US fare, the refund can be larger than any EU award.

What is uncertain: how many eligible claims are actually paid. Consumer groups and regulators have repeatedly suggested that a large share of passengers never claim. The practical reading is modest: if you do not ask, assume nobody will pay.

## PERSPECTIVES

**The passenger view.** The rules exist, the money is yours, and the claim takes ten minutes. For a family of four on a long-haul delay, EU compensation can reach €2,400.

**The airline view.** Airlines argue, not unreasonably, that EU compensation has nothing to do with the ticket price. A €59 fare can trigger a €600 payout, which they say distorts low-cost flying. They have lobbied for changes for more than a decade.

**The regulator view.** Enforcement bodies can push airlines to pay but have limited staff. Their preferred route is the airline's own process first, then a free Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) scheme where the airline belongs to one.

**The consumer-advocate view.** Go direct and keep the money. Claim firms typically take a quarter or more of the award. For a first, clean claim they are an unnecessary tax.

## CONTEXT

Three concrete steps make the difference between an eligible claim and a paid one.

**One: keep the documents.** You need the booking confirmation (the schedule), the boarding pass (proof you were there) and the actual arrival time (the delay). The arrival time is the one people forget. Flight-tracking sites archive it, but a screenshot on the day is the most useful habit a traveller can form. Under EU rules, as interpreted by the EU Court of Justice, "arrival" means the moment at least one aircraft door opens — not touchdown.

**Two: start with the airline's own form.** Every major carrier has a passenger-rights claim form, usually under customer relations. Submit there first and keep the reference number. Set a reminder for a few weeks; some carriers are slow to answer the first round.

**Three: escalate once, correctly.** If the airline refuses or goes silent, turn to an approved ADR scheme or the regulator. In the UK that means an ADR body the airline belongs to, or the CAA. In the EU it means the National Enforcement Body of the country where the flight departed. In the US it means a complaint through DOT's online portal. All are free. Refusals that cite "extraordinary circumstances" for what turns out to be a routine technical fault are often overturned.

One more point people forget: compensation is per passenger, not per booking. Children with their own ticket qualify too. Infants on a parent's lap are often excluded.

Deadlines vary. In England and Wales a court claim can be brought up to six years after the flight. In the EU it depends on national law, which differs widely. Check the national body's page before assuming you have years.

## DEEPER

There is a quiet pattern in modern consumer rights. The entitlements are written large, in regulations governments like to announce. The claim process is written small, in forms you find only by looking. The gap between the two works as an unofficial discount for the industry.

A thoughtful reader does not need to be angry to see what this asks of them. Rights that have to be claimed mostly go unclaimed. The people who do claim tend to be the ones who need the money least — frequent flyers who know the forms, not the exhausted family at the baggage carousel. Rights delivered by default, like the US automatic refund, reach more people.

Meanwhile, the useful posture is mild, informed persistence. The airline's obligations are written down; you can read the same page. None of this is adversarial. It is paperwork, done once, within the window, with three documents in hand.

Most readers will fly a few times a year without a delay long enough to trigger any of this. One day, one of those flights will be the four-hour-late evening into Prague or Birmingham or Chicago. The question then will not be whether you had the right, but whether you remembered.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** The Kosovo Specialist Chambers convicted former Kosovo president Hashim Thaçi and three other former Kosovo Liberation Army leaders of war crimes. Thaçi received 25 years; the other sentences were 18, 13 and 25 years.

**What it means.** The judges found personal responsibility for arbitrary detention, cruel treatment, torture and murder. They also acquitted the defendants of crimes against humanity and stressed that the trial did not judge Kosovo's independence struggle, the KLA as a whole or Serbian crimes against Kosovo Albanians.

**Risks and impact.** The verdict may deepen distrust of the court in Kosovo, where the defendants remain heroes to many. Witness intimidation also threatens the ability of any court to establish facts.

**What can be done.** The useful distinction is between the ruling, its political reception and the history it did not decide.

**What to watch.** Appeals, the public written judgment and a separate witness-intimidation case will test both the findings and the institution behind them.

## FACTS

On September 16, Trial Panel II found Thaçi, Kadri Veseli, Rexhep Selimi and Jakup Krasniqi criminally responsible for war crimes committed during Kosovo's 1998–99 conflict. The panel attributed arbitrary detention of 385 people, torture of 303, cruel treatment of 49 and murder of 96 to the criminal purpose in which the four participated.

Thaçi and Krasniqi received 25-year sentences. Veseli received 18 years and Selimi 13, with credit for time served. All four had pleaded not guilty.

The panel found the four not guilty of six crimes-against-humanity counts. Its summary said prosecutors had not proved beyond reasonable doubt that a widespread or systematic attack was directed against a civilian population, a required legal element. It also rejected liability for some alleged incidents.

The ruling is at first instance and can be appealed. Thaçi also faces separate proceedings concerning alleged obstruction and witness intimidation. Reuters and AP reported anger among supporters in Pristina, where thousands had gathered and large screens carried the judgment.

## EVIDENCE

The primary record is unusually explicit about its boundary. The panel said the case concerned four natural persons, not the legitimacy of the KLA's goal, not a comparison between the two sides and not the crimes Serbian forces and paramilitaries committed against Kosovo Albanians.

Within that boundary, the evidentiary record was large: 273 witnesses, 5,467 exhibits and a transcript exceeding 29,000 pages. The panel found that the defendants contributed in different ways to targeting perceived political or military opponents, including Kosovo Albanians linked to rival groups, people accused of ties to Serbian authorities, and some Roma and Serbs.

The acquittals matter as much as the convictions for understanding the legal result. The judges did not say no civilians were harmed. They concluded that prosecutors had not proved the particular population-directed element needed for crimes against humanity. They did find protected people were unlawfully detained, tortured, cruelly treated and killed in connection with the armed conflict.

AP and Reuters independently confirmed the sentences and public reaction. What remains unavailable in final public form is the complete redacted written judgment; the 39-page oral summary warns that only the full judgment is authoritative.

## CONTEXT

The Kosovo Specialist Chambers are part of Kosovo's legal system but sit in The Hague and use international judges and staff. That unusual design reflects a central problem: crimes alleged against members of a victorious liberation movement were difficult to investigate where former commanders retained prestige and power. Distance was intended to reduce local pressure without removing Kosovo law.

Thaçi moved from the KLA's political directorate to peace negotiations and later became prime minister and president. Western governments once treated him as a partner in building an independent Kosovo. His prosecution therefore unsettles a simple story in which wartime legitimacy passes untouched into peacetime authority.

The wider history remains essential. Serbian forces carried out mass displacement and grave crimes against Kosovo Albanians; NATO's 78-day air campaign ended the fighting in 1999, and Kosovo declared independence in 2008. Serbia still rejects that independence. None of those facts supplies a legal defence for crimes against people taking no active part in hostilities. Nor does today's conviction erase them.

That is the distinction the tribunal is trying to preserve: a cause can be legitimate while particular means are criminal. The appeal process will test whether it preserved that distinction in proof, not merely in its opening words.

## DEEPER

Postwar justice asks societies to hold two truths without letting either swallow the other. A community can have been oppressed, displaced and attacked. Members of its own victorious movement can still commit crimes. Accepting the second statement does not cancel the first.

The difficulty is that public memory prefers a single moral subject: liberator or criminal, victim or perpetrator. Courts work at a smaller scale. They ask who did what, with what knowledge and authority, against which protected person, under which legal rule. The result can feel emotionally inadequate precisely because it refuses to carry an entire nation's meaning.

That restraint is also a safeguard. Collective stories turn quickly into collective blame. By insisting on individual responsibility, a court can condemn unlawful conduct without converting ethnicity, political allegiance or a liberation movement into a criminal identity. The discipline matters most when public memory and political identity are tightly fused.

The same restraint must bind the court itself. A tribunal cannot ask the public to separate cause from conduct while blurring evidence between defendants or treating disputed history as proof. The full judgment and appeal are therefore not procedural afterthoughts. They are where the promise of individual justice must become visible.

## IMPACT

The immediate practical consequence is legal for the four defendants and political for Kosovo. The broader consequence is institutional: communities emerging from war need courts capable of hearing victims whose perpetrators belong to the side celebrated as victorious.

For readers, the useful test is not whether a verdict flatters an existing account of the war. Check what the court proved, what it acquitted, what remains under appeal and which historical questions it expressly excluded.

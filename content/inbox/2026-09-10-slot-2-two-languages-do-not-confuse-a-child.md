## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Updated guidance from the American Academy of Pediatrics and speech-language specialists addresses a durable parenting fear: that hearing two languages confuses young children or causes delayed speech.

**What it means.** A bilingual child’s knowledge is distributed. Fewer words in each language, language mixing and changing which language is stronger can all be ordinary development.

**Risks and impact.** Comparing one of a bilingual child’s languages with a monolingual norm can create false alarms. Blaming bilingualism can also delay a proper assessment when a genuine difficulty appears across languages.

**What can be done.** Count communication across the child’s languages, keep rich conversation in the languages caregivers know best and seek an evaluation that considers the whole profile.

**What to watch.** Progress, understanding, gestures, word combinations and communication across settings matter more than whether every word stays in one language.

## FACTS

The [AAP and ASHA parent guidance](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/school/Pages/7-Myths-Facts-Bilingual-Children-Learning-Language.aspx), updated November 20, 2025, is direct: learning more than one language does not cause a speech or language disorder. Most multilingual children reach broad early milestones on the same timetable as other children—first words around 12 months and two-word combinations around 24 months—while individual variation remains large.

What looks like a small vocabulary may be a counting error. A child might know 25 words in English and 25 different words in Spanish. Testing only English records half the knowledge. Researchers distinguish total vocabulary, which adds words across languages, from conceptual vocabulary, which counts a meaning once even if the child knows both “dog” and “perro.”

Mixing languages is also not proof of confusion. Young children borrow the word that is available, mirror the speech of their community and gradually learn which language fits which person or setting. Research reviewed by Krista Byers-Heinlein and Casey Lew-Williams found that even infants can discriminate between languages; by age two, children already adjust language use to their conversation partner.

## EVIDENCE

A [2013 clinical review by Erika Hoff and Cynthia Core](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4457512/) describes the middle ground. Bilingual children can build total linguistic knowledge at a rate comparable to monolingual children, but that growth is divided between two languages. In one longitudinal sample, 56 English-monolingual and 47 Spanish–English bilingual children were followed from 22 to 30 months. The monolingual children had larger English vocabularies; once English and Spanish words were combined, the difference in total vocabulary disappeared.

That does not mean every bilingual child follows one identical track. Quantity and quality of exposure matter in each language. A child who hears a heritage language only occasionally should not be expected to use it as fluently as a language heard all day. Dominance can shift when school begins.

Nor should “bilingualism does not cause delay” become “delay is impossible.” ASHA’s [clinical guidance on late language emergence](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/late-language-emergence/) says assessment should consider all languages and identify a disorder as a meaningful communication breakdown across the languages used. Difficulties in understanding, gestures, vocabulary or combining words may deserve attention. The right response is a fair evaluation, not automatically removing a language.

## PERSPECTIVES

Parents often receive contradictory advice. One relative says each parent must speak only one language. A teacher worries that mixed sentences show confusion. Another voice promises that bilingualism will make a child smarter. All three claims can be too neat.

“One person, one language” can work, but it is neither necessary nor sufficient. Families may divide languages by person, place or time, or use them flexibly. The better question is whether a child gets enough warm, varied, back-and-forth conversation in each language. Caregivers usually provide richer stories, humor and emotional nuance in the language they command most naturally.

The claim of a universal cognitive “bilingual advantage” also needs restraint. Some laboratory studies find advantages in attention or switching tasks, while their size and everyday importance remain debated. The clearest benefit is less glamorous and more durable: a child can communicate with more people, including grandparents, and remain connected to family culture.

Clinicians and schools face a genuine challenge. A child learning the community language may look behind on a test built for monolingual speakers. Yet assuming every difficulty is “just bilingualism” can miss a disorder. Good assessment requires language history, observation, family input and, where possible, testing in both languages or work with an interpreter.

## CONTEXT

The confusion myth has a history. Earlier research often judged immigrant children with tests designed around monolingual experience, then treated lower scores in the majority language as evidence that two languages were harmful. Modern work shows why that inference fails: bilingual experience reorganizes where knowledge appears.

Language is tied to people and situations. A child may know kitchen words in the language used with a grandparent and playground words in the language used at school. Asking for every concept in only one language measures exposure as much as ability. This “distributed” vocabulary is not two incomplete monolingual systems. It is one child’s functional repertoire.

A [2022 review of bilingual development in infancy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8866745/) emphasizes that high-quality interaction and conversational turn-taking predict growth. Passive exposure is not equivalent to responsive talk. Families also differ greatly in who speaks which language, when childcare begins, what books are available and which language the wider community rewards.

That last force is powerful. Once children enter school, the majority language often expands while the heritage language weakens. Telling parents to abandon the language they speak best may accelerate that loss without curing a disorder. It can also narrow communication between children and relatives precisely when relationships should be growing.

## PEOPLE

Imagine a child who tells one version of a story to a teacher and a richer version to a grandmother. A monolingual test sees only the first performance. The family sees the whole child.

Parents need permission to value that whole picture without becoming complacent. A mixed sentence can be ingenious; persistent difficulty understanding ordinary requests in every language is different. A smaller vocabulary in the school language may reflect less exposure; stalled progress across months deserves a closer look.

The aim is not to win an argument about bilingualism. It is to preserve communication while finding support early when support is needed. A speech-language pathologist familiar with multilingual development—or one working with a qualified interpreter—can help separate difference from disorder.

## DEEPER

Use a whole-language check before drawing conclusions. For one week, note new words, gestures, questions and short combinations in every language the child uses. Count meanings across languages, but also notice comprehension: can the child follow familiar directions, point to named objects and repair a misunderstanding?

Keep speaking the language in which you can be most responsive. Read a familiar book, pause for the child’s contribution, expand a short utterance and tell family stories. Do not turn home conversation into a vocabulary test. Rich interaction matters more than perfect separation of languages.

Ask for professional advice when broad milestones are missed, skills are lost, the child seems not to understand speech, hearing is a concern or difficulties appear across languages. Bring a language-exposure history and examples from different caregivers. Request that the evaluation consider all languages rather than translating a monolingual score mechanically.

The practical rule is simple: do not subtract a language to solve a problem the language did not cause. Add better observation, better input and, when necessary, better assessment.

## REFLECT

Are you counting everything the child can communicate, or only what appears in the language you understand best?

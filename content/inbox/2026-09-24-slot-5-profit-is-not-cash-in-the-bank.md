## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Nothing dramatic has to go wrong for a profitable business to become short of cash. A customer can buy now and pay later while payroll, tax and supplier dates remain fixed.

**What it means.** Profit measures revenue against expenses under an accounting method. Cash flow follows the money that actually enters and leaves the bank. The two answer different questions.

**Risks and impact.** Rapid sales growth can increase the gap if more money becomes tied up in receivables and inventory before customers pay.

**What can be done.** Reconcile profit to operating cash, list the dates of expected receipts and unavoidable payments, and watch the oldest invoices and slowest inventory.

**What to watch.** A cash forecast is not certainty. Late customers, disputed invoices, tax changes and surprise costs can move the result, so assumptions and a margin for error matter.

## FACTS

Imagine a small firm that completes a €20,000 project in September. It has earned the revenue and may recognise a profit after wages and other costs. But the contract gives the customer 60 days to pay. September's income statement can improve while September's bank balance does not.

The reverse also happens. A customer may pay a deposit before the work is earned. Cash arrives, but the full amount is not yet revenue. A business can also spend cash on equipment that will be recognised as an expense gradually through depreciation, or receive a loan that raises cash without creating profit.

This is not an accounting trick. It is the consequence of separating economic activity from payment timing. The U.S. Small Business Administration explains that accrual accounting records a completed sale when it occurs, while cash accounting records it when payment is received. Each view is useful. Confusing them is dangerous.

## EVIDENCE

IAS 7 requires a statement of cash flows to classify cash movements into operating, investing and financing activities. Under its indirect method, reported profit is adjusted for non-cash transactions, deferrals and accruals. That bridge is the core lesson: profit is a starting point for explaining cash, not a substitute for cash.

Working capital often creates the biggest bridge. Accounts receivable represent sales not yet collected. Inventory represents cash already committed to goods that have not yet been sold. Accounts payable provide time before suppliers must be paid. If receivables and inventory rise faster than payables, operating cash can fall even while sales and profit rise.

The distinction appears even in national statistics. The U.S. Department of Agriculture publishes both net farm income and net cash farm income because the measures include different items and can move differently. In its September 2026 forecast, nominal net farm income was expected to fall while net cash farm income edged higher. The example is sector-wide, but the principle is the same: an income measure and a cash measure are not interchangeable.

## PERSPECTIVES

### The accountant's view

Accrual accounting matches revenue and expenses to the period in which activity occurs. That can provide a better view of whether the business model is economically sound than a bank statement distorted by one large early payment. A receivable is a real asset, not imaginary money. But it is less liquid than cash and carries collection risk.

### The operator's view

The calendar can be more urgent than the margin. Wages, rent and taxes arrive on dates. Inventory may need to be ordered before a seasonal rush. A profitable contract with a long collection period can therefore increase financing needs. Growth does not automatically fund itself.

### The lender's view

A lender may care about profit because it indicates capacity to earn, and about cash because debt is paid with money, not accounting income. Strong results accompanied by rising overdue receivables prompt a different question from strong results accompanied by prompt collection. Neither number should be read alone.

### The owner's view

Calling every bank balance “profit” invites premature spending. Calling every cash shortage “failure” can also mislead when the cause is a temporary, understood timing gap. The important distinction is between a planned gap with credible funding and an unexplained gap that widens each month.

## CONTEXT

Cash problems often become visible late because the income statement is flattering and the bank account is noisy. Deposits from old work can mask weak collection on new work. A tax payment can look like a sudden shock even though the liability accumulated over months. A credit line can postpone the moment when the operating gap must be explained.

Rapid growth increases the stakes. More orders may require more materials, staff time and shipping before invoices are collected. If a firm earns a 10% margin on each order but pays most costs 45 days before the customer pays, doubling orders can double the cash tied up during that interval.

That does not mean growth is bad or that every firm should demand immediate payment. Payment terms are part of competition and customer trust. Inventory can protect service levels. The aim is to make the timing visible and decide deliberately who finances the interval: the owner, a supplier, a customer deposit or a lender.

Uncertainty also matters. A forecast built from invoice due dates assumes those dates are real. Historical collection patterns, disputed invoices and customer concentration may be more informative than the printed terms. A forecast is a model of promises; the bank balance records which promises have already been kept.

## DEEPER

### A practical cash bridge

Start with expected profit for the next 13 weeks, then translate it into dated cash movements. Add back non-cash expenses such as depreciation. Subtract increases in receivables and inventory. Add increases in payables only where payment can legally and commercially wait. Then include equipment purchases, loan repayments, taxes and owner withdrawals, which may not sit in operating profit.

Now test the weak points. Which five receipts are largest? What happens if each arrives two weeks late? Which payments cannot move without harming staff, compliance or essential supply? Which inventory can be delayed without breaking a customer promise? The exercise is not a recommendation to withhold payment or borrow. It is a way to expose timing before it becomes an emergency.

Track a small set of signals: cash available, invoices due and overdue, days from invoice to collection, inventory that has not moved, bills due in the next four weeks and the lowest projected cash point. Reconcile the forecast to the actual bank account every week and record why reality differed.

The reader outcome is modest but useful: when someone says the business is profitable, ask a second question—how much of that profit has become cash, and when must the cash leave? The answer can distinguish a sound business with a timing problem from a business whose apparent success is not converting into spendable resources.

## REFLECT

Which number in your organisation gets celebrated first—sales, profit or cash—and what becomes invisible because of that choice?

If your largest customer paid a month late, would the resulting shortage reveal a temporary timing gap, or a business model that depends on promises arriving exactly on time?

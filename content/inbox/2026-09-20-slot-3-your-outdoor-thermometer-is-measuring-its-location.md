## BRIEFLY

**An outdoor thermometer does not sample a town-sized block of pure air.** It exchanges heat with the air, sunlight, the sky, nearby surfaces and its own enclosure.

**A technically precise sensor can therefore report a poor weather observation.** A sunlit wall, dark roof, paved yard or stagnant corner can shift the reading without anything being broken.

**Professional stations control exposure before comparing numbers.** The Met Office measures air temperature in a ventilated Stevenson screen 1.25 metres above the ground; the WMO maintains detailed international guidance for comparable observations.

**A home station can still be useful without reproducing a national network.** The important step is to decide whether it measures a sheltered patio, a vegetable bed or representative open air, then describe that honestly.

**The practical audit is simple.** Check radiation protection, airflow, height, nearby heat sources and changes around the sensor before adjusting its calibration.

## FACTS

Temperature sounds like the most straightforward weather number. Put a sensor outside, wait and read. But the sensing element does not know which energy arrived by contact with moving air and which arrived as radiation from the Sun, a warm wall or the ground. It settles toward an equilibrium produced by all of them.

That is why meteorological air temperature is a defined observation, not merely any outdoor reading. The Met Office says its automatic stations use platinum resistance thermometers inside naturally ventilated Stevenson screens, 1.25 metres above ground. The white, louvred enclosure limits direct radiation and precipitation while allowing air to circulate. Its instruments are also calibrated against a traceable standard.

The World Meteorological Organization’s WMO-No. 8 guide devotes separate chapters to temperature, wind, precipitation, automatic stations, urban observations and quality assurance. The separation matters. A compact all-in-one home station may place several sensors together for convenience, although the best exposure for wind is not the best exposure for temperature or rain.

## EVIDENCE

The physical problem is easiest to see on a sunny day. A bare sensor absorbs short-wave solar energy and can become warmer than the surrounding air. Moving it under a roof removes direct sun but may introduce heat stored in the roof, weak ventilation and a different view of the night sky. Fastening it to masonry can make the wall part of the measurement: the wall heats and cools on its own schedule.

Official networks reduce those effects through common methods so that a reading in one place can be compared with readings elsewhere and with the same station years later. The Met Office’s specified screen and height are one implementation of that principle. WMO guidance provides the broader international reference, including exposure and quality-control practices.

NOAA’s National Weather Service accepts personal-station observations through the Citizen Weather Observer Program when stations are well sited. NOAA’s MADIS system then distributes contributed observations to user organizations. That invitation is not evidence that every backyard number is interchangeable with a reference station. It shows why metadata, siting and automated quality checks matter when many private instruments enter a shared dataset.

## PERSPECTIVES

There is no single correct location for every purpose. A gardener may want the temperature where seedlings actually live, including the cold pocket beside a fence. A household may care about heat on a balcony. A meteorological observer instead wants a reading that represents air over a more standard surface and can be compared across sites.

Those are different questions, not a hierarchy of serious and unserious measurements. Trouble begins when a highly local reading is labelled as the temperature “in Prague,” “in Bristol” or “in the neighbourhood” without qualification. Precision on the display—perhaps tenths of a degree—does not enlarge the patch of environment that was sampled.

Comparison with an official station also needs restraint. NOAA’s NCEI provides records from thousands of land stations, but the nearest one may differ in elevation, surface cover, distance from buildings and time of observation. A persistent gap does not automatically identify which instrument is wrong. It may describe two real microenvironments.

Manufacturers can make sensors more stable, enclosures more reflective and response times faster. They cannot manufacture a universal backyard. Exposure remains part of the result.

## CONTEXT

Standardisation became central to weather observation because isolated readings are easy to collect and hard to combine. A long climate record requires more than a good thermometer. It requires continuity in location, surrounding land, screen, height, observing practice and maintenance—or a documented account of changes.

The same logic applies at home on a smaller scale. A station moved from grass to a roof may acquire a step change that looks like climate but is really relocation. A tree growing around it can gradually reduce ventilation and sunlight. New paving, an air-conditioning outlet or a repainted wall can change the local energy balance. Dust and insects can obstruct a shield; a tipped rain gauge creates a different problem altogether.

Networks such as CWOP are valuable precisely because many local observations can fill spatial gaps. Dense coverage can show features that a distant airport station misses. Yet density does not cancel measurement error. A useful network needs plausibility checks, neighbouring comparisons and a record of where each sensor sits.

This is a general scientific lesson. Instruments do not remove context. They make a particular interaction repeatable enough to interpret. The more exact the number looks, the more important it is to know what produced it.

## DEEPER

### A five-minute siting audit

First, look at the sensor at three times: morning, midday and late afternoon. Does direct sunlight ever reach it? Is the shield intact, pale and open to airflow? “Shade” from a hot wall or roof is not the same as radiation protection in open air.

Second, inspect the surroundings. Note masonry, asphalt, exhausts, outdoor appliances, windows, dense shrubs and any surface that stores or releases heat. Record the sensor’s height and whether air can move around it. Do not silently correct the number to what you think it should be.

Third, compare patterns rather than a single instant. Place a second trusted sensor beside the first for a day to test instrument agreement, then move one sensor to a better-exposed location for several comparable days. If the pair agree side by side but separate after the move, exposure is the likely cause. If they disagree together, calibration or response time deserves attention.

Finally, write down the location and photograph it. When a reading changes months later, check the site log before declaring a record. For decisions involving official warnings, use the responsible weather service; a home sensor is an observation, not a substitute for a forecast or heat alert.

## REFLECT

What place does your outdoor sensor actually describe? Which nearby surface is most likely to influence it after a sunny afternoon? If you moved it and the readings changed, would you call the old number wrong—or recognise that you had asked the instrument a different question?

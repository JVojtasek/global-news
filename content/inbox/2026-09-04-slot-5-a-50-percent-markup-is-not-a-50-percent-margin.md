## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** A product bought for $100 and sold for $150 produces $50 of gross profit, a 50% markup and only a 33.3% gross margin.

**What it means.** Both percentages are correct. They answer different questions because markup divides profit by cost while margin divides it by revenue.

**Risks and impact.** Mixing them up can make a quote look healthier than it is, especially when discounts, card fees, delivery, labour and overhead still have to be paid.

**What can be done.** Keep five fields beside every price: cost, selling price, gross profit, markup and margin. Label the percentage requested in every report.

**What to watch.** After a price change, check the realised margin on actual invoices rather than assuming the planned markup survived discounts and cost changes.

## FACTS

Suppose a shop buys an item for $100 and sells it for $150. Gross profit is the $50 difference between revenue and the cost of the item.

Markup measures that $50 against the $100 cost. The calculation is $50 divided by $100, producing a 50% markup. Gross margin measures the same $50 against the $150 selling price. The calculation is $50 divided by $150, producing a 33.3% margin.

ACCA defines gross margin as gross profit divided by revenue. Its pricing guidance shows how a business may add a markup to a cost to set a selling price, while warning that the resulting contribution still has to cover fixed costs and volume assumptions. Xero presents the same distinction: markup uses cost as the base; margin uses revenue.

A 50% gross margin would require a different selling price. If cost is $100, the price must be $200, because the $100 gross profit is half of $200 revenue. The arithmetic is simple once the denominator is named. The trouble begins when a spreadsheet says only “50%”.

## EVIDENCE

The definitions are not disputed accounting theory. ACCA, OpenStax and Xero use consistent concepts even though they teach them in different settings.

ACCA's ratio guidance places gross profit over revenue. Its pricing material starts with cost and adds a markup to produce a selling price. OpenStax defines contribution margin separately as selling price minus variable cost, a useful warning that “margin” can refer to more than one measure. A manager therefore needs both the numerator and the denominator, not merely a label.

The calculation can be checked without trusting any source. Let cost be C and price be P. Gross profit is P minus C. Markup is (P − C) divided by C. Margin is (P − C) divided by P. Because P is larger than C when a sale is profitable, the margin percentage is lower than the markup percentage for the same transaction.

What these figures do not prove is whether the business is profitable overall. Gross profit excludes many costs below the gross-profit line. A healthy gross margin can coexist with a loss after wages, rent, software, interest, tax or returns.

## PERSPECTIVES

The pricing view begins with the question, “What should we charge?” Cost-plus pricing often answers by adding a chosen markup. It is quick, visible and easy to apply across many items. But it can inherit a bad cost estimate and ignore what customers will pay.

The reporting view asks, “How much of each sales pound or dollar remains after the direct cost?” Gross margin makes products with different prices easier to compare because the denominator is revenue. Yet it can flatter a business if important fulfilment or service costs have been left outside cost of sales.

The cash view is more impatient. It asks whether money arriving from customers covers money leaving the account at the right time. Neither markup nor gross margin shows payment timing, inventory already bought, tax due or debt repayments.

These are not rival answers. They are three windows onto one shop. Pricing chooses a ticket, margin describes trading performance, and cash flow decides whether the lights stay on.

## CONTEXT

Percentages feel portable. That is why an unlabeled percentage travels so easily from a price list into a sales meeting and then into a forecast. Unfortunately, it can change meaning without changing its number.

Imagine a consultant estimates $1,000 of direct labour and adds a 30% markup. The quote becomes $1,300 and the planned gross profit is $300. The margin is not 30%; it is about 23.1%. If a salesperson then grants a 10% discount, revenue falls to $1,170. With cost unchanged, gross profit becomes $170 and margin falls to about 14.5%.

The example also shows why the definition of cost matters. For a retailer, cost of goods sold may be relatively visible. For a service firm, direct labour hours, subcontractors, travel and project-specific software may be classified inconsistently. ACCA's pricing guidance distinguishes marginal cost from total absorption cost because adding the same markup to two different cost bases produces two different prices.

A useful quote sheet therefore exposes its assumptions. It does not bury them in a single “profit” cell.

### Practical takeaway

Before accepting a price, write down:

1. the cost base and what it includes;
2. the proposed selling price;
3. gross profit in money;
4. markup on cost;
5. gross margin on revenue;
6. the effect of the likely discount;
7. which overheads still sit below gross profit.

For a target margin, work backwards. Price equals cost divided by one minus the target margin. A $100 cost with a 40% target margin requires a price of about $166.67, not $140.

## DEEPER

The denominator is where a business reveals its point of view.

Markup looks backward at what the seller put in. Margin looks outward at what the customer paid. Both can describe the same $50 honestly, yet the first calls it 50% and the second calls it 33.3%. Nothing has changed except the base of comparison.

This small accounting distinction belongs to a larger family of mistakes. Relative risk can sound dramatic without an absolute baseline. A pay rise can look generous before inflation. A project can appear efficient when only billable hours are counted. Numbers do not lie on their own, but they can answer a narrower question than the listener thinks was asked.

The cure is not suspicion of percentages. It is the habit of completing the sentence: percentage of what?

That question slows a meeting by perhaps ten seconds. It can save months of trying to understand why busy sales did not become the profit everyone expected.

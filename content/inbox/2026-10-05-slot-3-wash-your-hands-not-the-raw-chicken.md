## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Food-safety agencies in the United States, Canada and Europe advise home cooks not to wash raw chicken before cooking it.

**What it means.** Water does not reliably make poultry safe. It can carry droplets of raw juice from the bird to the sink, tap, worktop, utensils or food that will not be cooked.

**Risks and impact.** The concern is cross-contamination by organisms that may be present on raw poultry, including *Salmonella* and *Campylobacter*. Children, older adults, pregnant people and people with weakened immune systems can face more serious illness.

**What can be done.** Keep the chicken contained, wash hands with soap, use separate tools, clean touched surfaces and check the thickest part with a food thermometer. For chicken pieces, the U.S. and Canadian guidance cited here uses 74°C or 165°F.

**What to watch.** Watch the thermometer, not the colour of the meat or the clarity of its juices. Temperature is the useful measurement.

## FACTS

Raw poultry can carry harmful bacteria without looking or smelling spoiled. The CDC names *Campylobacter*, *Salmonella* and *Clostridium perfringens* among the organisms associated with raw chicken. FoodSafety.gov therefore gives a blunt instruction: never wash raw poultry. Cooking, rather than rinsing, is the control intended to kill the germs.

The practical sequence is simple. Keep the package from leaking onto other groceries. Store it sealed on the bottom shelf of the refrigerator. When preparing it, wash hands with soap and water for at least 20 seconds before and after touching the raw bird. Use a separate board and plate, and wash tools and work surfaces before they touch salad, bread or cooked food.

For chicken pieces, ground poultry and frozen raw breaded chicken, Health Canada lists 74°C (165°F) as the safe internal temperature. Put a digital thermometer into the thickest part, avoiding bone, and check separate pieces rather than assuming they heated alike. Refrigerate leftovers within two hours. These steps deal with the actual routes by which germs survive or travel.

## EVIDENCE

The independent guidance agrees on the mechanism. The CDC says raw chicken does not need washing and reports that, in a USDA study, one in seven people who cleaned a sink after washing chicken still left germs there. Health Canada explains why: bacteria can spread wherever rinse water splashes. WHO guidance treats raw juices as a transfer route and recommends separating raw poultry from cooked or ready-to-eat food.

The agreement is stronger than any one preferred kitchen style. U.S. and Canadian agencies separately recommend handwashing, dedicated or thoroughly cleaned tools, and thermometer-verified cooking. Their temperature tables converge on 74°C/165°F for chicken pieces, though Health Canada lists a higher target for a whole bird. WHO gives a broader minimum of 70°C for thorough cooking in general food-safety advice. Following the rule issued for the product and country is more sensible than averaging the numbers.

What the evidence does not show is that every washed bird will cause illness. Risk is not certainty. The point is that rinsing adds a contamination route without providing a dependable safety benefit.

## PERSPECTIVES

The urge to wash chicken is understandable. In ordinary life, water removes visible dirt, and many family recipes use a rinse, brine, vinegar or citrus as part of preparation. That practice may carry cultural meaning or affect flavour. The mistake is to let the familiar word “clean” do two jobs. A culinary rinse is not a validated kill step for foodborne pathogens.

Another view says that a careful cook can wash poultry without splashing. The CDC even offers damage-limiting instructions for people who still choose to do it: use a gentle flow, then clean and sanitize the sink and surrounding area, and wash hands. That is harm reduction, not evidence that washing is useful. A droplet is not obliged to be visible before it lands on the tap handle.

There is also a risk of turning sensible guidance into kitchen theatre. Spraying disinfectant everywhere while neglecting handwashing, board separation or a thermometer merely replaces one ritual with another. The strongest framing is not “raw chicken is terrifying.” It is that safety comes from a short chain of controls: contain, separate, clean, cook and chill. Each link has a specific job.

## CONTEXT

Home kitchens mix two very different kinds of food in a small space. Raw chicken will be cooked; lettuce, bread, herbs and a finished sauce may go straight to the table. Heat can deal with bacteria on the chicken, but it cannot protect a salad contaminated by the same knife or a hand that moved from the package to the fridge handle.

That is why storage is part of preparation. Health Canada recommends keeping poultry in a clean container or bag, low in the refrigerator, at 4°C (40°F) or colder. WHO recommends a refrigerator below 5°C and sealed containers that prevent raw juices reaching prepared food. Cold slows bacterial growth; it does not sterilize the package.

A practical five-minute audit is more useful than a vague promise to “be hygienic.” Before cooking, clear ready-to-eat food from the work area. Set out one board, one plate for the raw bird and a clean plate for the cooked food. After handling, wash hands, then the tools and touched surfaces. Insert a clean thermometer into the thickest part. Finally, put leftovers into the refrigerator promptly. If the raw package leaks, treat the bag, shelf and anything touched by the liquid as part of the same job.

## PEOPLE

The person most likely to benefit may not be the cook. A healthy adult can have a mild illness, while a young child, an older relative, a pregnant guest or someone with weakened immunity may face a harder course. Health Canada explicitly identifies those groups as being at higher risk of serious illness from *Salmonella* or *Campylobacter* infection.

That changes the tone of kitchen safety. It is not a test of whether a cook is fastidious, modern or faithful to family habit. It is quiet care for people who cannot see the preparation. They only meet the finished plate, the salad bowl and the tap handle. The best routine is the one that still works on a tired Wednesday evening: few steps, clear order, no reliance on memory or appearance.

## DEEPER

Rinsing chicken is a small example of a larger trap: an action can feel protective because it is visible, familiar and effortful. Running water looks like something is happening. A thermometer reading is less dramatic. Separating a board from a salad bowl feels almost too ordinary to count.

Good systems often work that way. They do not ask whether someone cares enough. They arrange the sequence so that the hazardous thing has fewer chances to travel. The raw food stays contained. The hands are cleaned at the transition. The cooked food receives a clean plate. The temperature provides an external check when colour and intuition are unreliable.

That distinction matters beyond chicken. Rituals answer the emotional question, “Have I done something?” Controls answer the practical question, “Have I interrupted the mechanism?” Sometimes the two coincide. Sometimes they do not. The useful habit is to name the hazard, trace its route and choose the step that blocks it.

In this case, the answer is reassuringly plain. The bird does not need a bath. The cook needs a clean sequence, and the chicken needs enough heat.

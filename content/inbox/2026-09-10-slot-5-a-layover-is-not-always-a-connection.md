## BRIEFLY

**What happened:** Booking tools increasingly display combinations of flights that look like ordinary connections but may actually be separate tickets requiring a self-transfer.

**What it means:** A timetable gap is not the same thing as protection. On one through-ticket, the operating carriers may have duties when a delay breaks the onward journey. On separate tickets, the second airline may treat the traveller as a no-show.

**Risks and impact:** Baggage may need to be collected and checked again. Immigration, security, terminal changes and check-in deadlines can consume a generous-looking interval.

**What can be done:** Before paying, identify the ticket structure, baggage path, border steps and the party responsible for rebooking.

**What to watch:** Airport construction, terminal changes, minimum connection guidance and the exact conditions sold by the booking platform can alter the real margin.

## FACTS

A layover describes time between flights. It does not by itself say whether the flights belong to one protected itinerary. The decisive paperwork may be one ticket covering successive flights, two tickets sold in one checkout, or entirely separate bookings. Those arrangements can place disruption risk in different hands.

EU passenger guidance treats a missed connecting flight as a rights question when the flights were booked as part of a single reservation and the relevant geographic conditions are met. The UK Civil Aviation Authority similarly explains delay and cancellation duties, while stressing that eligibility depends on the itinerary and cause. These are legal frameworks with boundaries, not a universal promise attached to every pair of flights.

Operations add a second layer. Heathrow tells transfer passengers to follow connection signs and notes that some journeys require moving between terminals. A self-transferring passenger may instead have to enter the country, collect checked bags, clear customs, reach another terminal, meet the next airline's bag-drop deadline and pass security again. Visa or transit rules can matter even when the traveller never intended to visit the connecting city.

## EVIDENCE

The strongest clue appears before the airport: who issued the ticket and what destination is written into the contract. If a single reservation carries the traveller to the final city, the airline system can usually see the onward sector, check bags through when arrangements permit, and respond to a missed connection under the applicable conditions. That does not guarantee compensation; weather, air-traffic restrictions and other extraordinary circumstances can change which remedy applies. It does make the broken journey visible as one journey.

Separate tickets create two punctuality tests. The first airline has promised transport to the transfer point. The second has promised transport from that point, provided the passenger checks in and reaches the gate on time. If the first service arrives late, it may have fulfilled or remedied only its own contract. The second airline may owe nothing for an event outside its booking.

The Times' current passenger-rights explainer states the practical distinction plainly: missed-connection protection normally depends on all segments being booked on a single ticket. Official EU guidance adds important limits, including where the flights operate and what caused the disruption. The evidence therefore supports a rule for planning, not a blanket legal conclusion: verify the contract first, then check the rules that apply to the actual route.

## PERSPECTIVES

For a comparison site, combining separate fares can reveal a cheaper or more flexible route. For a traveller, the same combination can transfer the cost of uncertainty from an airline to the passenger. Both descriptions can be true. The ethical problem begins when the interface makes the saving vivid and the changed responsibility faint.

Airlines also see different objects. A carrier on a through-ticket sees an itinerary it has agreed to carry or coordinate. A carrier holding only the second ticket sees a passenger who must present on time. The airport sees flows through terminals, border control and security. None of those institutions controls the entire self-transfer.

Travel insurance can add another layer, but policies vary in definitions, exclusions, required buffers and documentation. A label such as “missed departure” does not prove that a self-transfer loss is covered. The traveller should read the policy wording and ask the insurer about the precise ticket structure before relying on it.

## CONTEXT

Traditional network airlines built schedules around interline agreements, minimum connection times and baggage transfer. Digital retail made it easy to construct combinations beyond those networks. That innovation widened choice, especially between low-cost carriers that do not routinely protect each other's passengers. It also made two different products look visually similar.

Minimum connection time is useful but narrower than many travellers assume. It is an airport or airline planning threshold for a particular combination of terminals and processes. It is not a personal guarantee, and it may not include bag collection, landside transfer or a second carrier's earlier check-in deadline. A traveller with reduced mobility, children, unfamiliar documents or checked equipment may need more time than the published minimum.

The contractual and physical maps therefore need to be read together. A protected forty-five-minute airside transfer may be operationally easier than a nominal three-hour self-transfer across airports. Clock time alone hides the work inside the interval.

## DEEPER

This is a small example of a wider digital-market pattern. A platform can assemble components into a single-looking product without making the components share responsibility. The screen creates narrative continuity: Prague, then London, then New York. The contracts may stop and restart in London.

The practical question is where failure is absorbed. A protected itinerary usually gives one carrier or ticketing arrangement a duty to offer a route forward under its conditions and the law. A self-transfer may offer a lower price because the traveller is buying and managing the contingency. That is not automatically a bad bargain. It is a bargain that needs to be named.

Before booking, open the fare details rather than trusting the word “connection.” Look for separate booking references or ticket numbers, a self-transfer warning, baggage instructions and responsibility for missed flights. Map the airport process from arrival gate to the next check-in deadline. Include immigration, baggage reclaim, customs, terminal transport and security. Then decide whether the saving is worth both the expected work and the low-probability cost of buying a replacement ticket.

## PRACTICAL CHECK

Use a six-question test before accepting a multi-flight itinerary:

1. Are all sectors on one ticket or explicitly protected by the seller?
2. Will checked baggage be tagged to the final destination?
3. Must you pass immigration or hold a transit or entry permission?
4. Are the flights in the same terminal and airport?
5. What is the second airline's check-in, bag-drop and gate deadline?
6. If the first flight is late, who has agreed in writing to rebook the second?

Save the answer and the seller's conditions with the reservation. Recheck shortly before travel because terminals and schedules change. When the route involves separate tickets, build a buffer based on the whole landside process, not the time between scheduled arrival and departure alone.

## REFLECT

The cheapest itinerary may still be the right one. The honest comparison includes the hidden product being sold alongside it: responsibility. A connection is not only two aircraft close together in time; it is an agreement about what happens when they are not.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** In the 1960s, Richard Booth began filling buildings in Hay-on-Wye with secondhand books and encouraged other booksellers to join him.

**What it means.** A specialist cluster turned a small Welsh market town into a destination. One shop attracted attention; many related businesses lowered the cost of searching for books and made a longer visit worthwhile.

**Risks and impact.** The story includes publicity theatre and later festival success, but tourism can also bring seasonal congestion, pressure on local capacity and a version of the town shaped for visitors.

**What can be done.** Visitors can spend beyond the headline attraction, use independent businesses, learn the local history and travel outside the busiest periods.

**What to watch.** The health of the place is better measured by year-round shops and institutions than by a festival crowd on one weekend.

## FACTS

Imagine entering a shop for one detective novel and emerging two hours later with a railway history, a book of Welsh poetry and no memory of where you parked. Hay-on-Wye’s appeal is not efficiency. It is permission to wander.

Richard Booth understood that wandering could become an economy.

Hay Castle Trust says Booth bought the castle in the 1960s, purchased books in bulk from the United States and used the castle as a hub while encouraging secondhand bookshops to open around town. Smithsonian reports that he opened six shops himself and inspired nearly 30 more in a town of fewer than 2,000 people.

In 1977, Booth declared Hay an independent kingdom and himself king. His horse became prime minister. It was theatre, not constitutional change, and it won national attention.

The Hay Festival began in 1987, according to Smithsonian. It became another powerful reason to associate the town with ideas and books, though the book-town story had already been built over decades.

## EVIDENCE

The broad outline is well documented across the castle trust, Wales’s official visitor service and independent accounts. Booth returned to the area after Oxford, acquired unusual quantities of unwanted books, converted available buildings and recruited others into the trade.

Some details vary. Sources differ on the year of his first shop and on how many bookshops Hay has at any moment. Shop counts depend on whether antiques, specialist dealers and nearby businesses are included. A durable article should not freeze a changing high street into one impressive number.

Economic causation is harder to prove. We cannot isolate books from changing travel habits, the festival, the restored castle, restaurants, accommodation and the wider beauty of the Welsh border. Nor does a busy weekend tell us how well a shop survives in February.

What the evidence does support is a mechanism familiar in retail geography: clustering. When many related sellers occupy a compact place, each shop benefits from the others. Visitors can compare, browse specialties and justify a longer journey because disappointment at one door does not end the trip.

## PERSPECTIVES

One reading makes Booth a visionary who revived a declining rural economy. Smithsonian records local bookseller Anne Addyman describing his diversification of the rural economy as cutting edge for the 1960s and 1970s.

Another sees a brilliant self-publicist. The crown, passports and equine prime minister could have become empty spectacle. Yet publicity worked because visitors found something real when they arrived: shelves, dealers, streets, a market town and eventually institutions that did not depend on Booth performing forever.

There is a romantic trap here. A place is not an empty stage awaiting an outsider’s brand. Residents still need homes, ordinary shops, transport and quiet days. Cultural tourism can support local work while also concentrating crowds and prices. Both can be true.

The lesson for other towns is therefore not “choose a theme and declare a kingdom.” It is to discover whether a genuine local cluster can become useful enough that the story survives the slogan.

## CONTEXT

Hay’s model reduces what economists call search costs. A rare-book buyer does not need to drive between distant shops. A casual visitor does not need a precise title in mind. Density turns uncertainty into part of the pleasure.

That density also helps complementary businesses. Browsing takes time; time creates demand for cafés, beds, meals, walks and transport. A restored castle and recurring festival deepen the visit. Each element lends credibility to the others.

But clusters are fragile. If rents rise beyond what specialist shops can pay, the identity can hollow out. If the festival becomes the only busy period, year-round trade may weaken. If every storefront sells the same souvenir, visitors lose the discovery that made the journey special.

This is why Hay-on-Wye is more than a good-news tale about one eccentric man. It is a case study in how an idea becomes shared infrastructure—and how later generations must keep maintaining it.

## PEOPLE

Booth was neither a tidy civic planner nor a saint in a cardigan. Accounts describe an extravagant, contradictory entrepreneur who bought boldly, lost money and understood newspapers. His personality opened the door, but the people who stocked shelves, repaired buildings, organised events and served visitors kept the town from becoming only his biography.

That distinction matters. Hero stories attract attention; institutions outlive heroes.

## DEEPER

A respectful visit asks more than “Where is the famous shop?”

1. **Use the cluster.** Visit several independent sellers, including a specialist shop you did not expect to like.
2. **Stay beyond the shelf.** Learn about the castle, market-town history, river landscape and the institutions that keep the place functioning.
3. **Choose timing thoughtfully.** A festival visit has energy; an off-peak visit may spread spending and give the town more room to speak in its normal voice.
4. **Buy locally where practical.** A coffee, meal or night’s stay can support the ecosystem that makes browsing possible.
5. **Treat the town as a home.** Keep doors, pavements and residential space usable for the people who are not on holiday.

### What this means for you

Hay-on-Wye’s most transferable idea is not books. It is concentration with character. One unusual business can be a curiosity. A network of businesses, places and recurring rituals can become a reason to travel.

The kingdom was a joke. The town it helped people notice was not.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Children are receiving phones younger, while a smartphone now bundles messaging, location, camera, games, browser, purchases and social feeds into one pocket device.

**What it means.** There is no evidence-based birthday at which every child becomes ready. Families can separate the need for contact from unrestricted internet and social-media access, then choose the least capable device that solves the immediate problem.

**Risks and impact.** Late-night notifications, scams, unwanted contact and algorithmic feeds create different risks for different children. Phones can also support contact, navigation, independence and peer participation.

**What can be done.** Use a readiness checklist and a 30-day staged launch. Set sleep, purchase and privacy rules; practise difficult scenarios; model the same boundaries; and review what actually happened.

**What to watch.** Look for changes in sleep, school routines, conflict, secrecy and the child's willingness to ask for help—not merely whether the device survived the month.

## FACTS

The useful first question is not “What age?” but “What need are we trying to meet?” A child walking home may need to call family. That does not automatically require a browser, app store, social feed and camera sharing.

The American Academy of Pediatrics advises parents to consider maturity, responsibility and family circumstances rather than rely on a universal age. Its June 2026 guidance suggests discussing rules and alternatives, including simpler devices, before handing over a smartphone.

Ofcom's 2026 research on children's media lives found younger phone ownership and described how children's use continues to change, including growing contact with AI tools and more consumption than creation. The exact figures describe UK participants and should not be pasted onto every country. The durable point is that “a phone” is no longer one exposure. Device type, services, settings, peers and household rules produce different experiences.

## EVIDENCE

Evidence about youth technology rarely supports a clean threshold. Much of it is observational: researchers can find associations between patterns of use and sleep, wellbeing or distress, but children who use technology differently may already differ in vulnerability, family context and offline life.

The National Academies' 2023 review concluded that social media can bring benefits and harms and called for stronger industry standards, research access and protection from harassment. It did not identify one age that guarantees safety. The American Psychological Association similarly recommends developmentally appropriate use, attention to individual vulnerability, adult monitoring that evolves with autonomy, and media-literacy skills.

Those sources concern social media, not every function on a smartphone. A device used for calls and maps is not equivalent to hours in an algorithmic feed. Conversely, technical controls cannot replace conversation: settings change, children use friends' devices, and a rule followed under supervision may fail under pressure.

## PERSPECTIVES

Parents often feel trapped between two plausible fears. Give the phone too early and the family may introduce risks that are hard to reverse. Wait too long and the child may lack contact, navigation or participation in the social life around them.

Children also have more than one interest. They may want independence and belonging while still needing help with impulse control, advertising and ambiguous contact. Treating every request as manipulation closes the channel a family will need when something goes wrong. Treating every promise as proof of readiness is equally unhelpful.

Schools and families may set conflicting expectations. A school might use digital platforms while banning phones in class. A separated family may need reliable contact across households. A child with a particular support need may benefit from functions that another child does not need. These differences are why a single age rule performs poorly.

The staged approach is not a guarantee or a moral ranking. It is a reversible experiment. It gives a child a chance to practise and gives adults observable information instead of arguments built entirely from hope and fear.

## CONTEXT

Buying a smartphone once meant buying a telephone with extras. Now it means opening several doors at once. Notifications compete for attention. A camera can document homework or enable impulsive sharing. Location can help a child navigate and also expose personal movement. Games can entertain and sell. Social platforms can connect peers and amplify harassment.

Because the bundle is the problem, unbundling is the solution. Four decisions deserve separate answers: Does the child need direct contact? Should they own a device? What internet access is justified? Which social or messaging services are appropriate now?

Readiness is also relational. Can the child tell an adult about a mistake without expecting immediate humiliation? Can adults enforce a bedtime boundary they model themselves? Is there a plan for passwords, purchases, unwanted messages and a lost device? A technically capable child may still be in a household without a workable response system.

No short trial predicts adolescence. A calm first month can reflect novelty, close supervision or luck. The purpose of a review is not to declare permanent success. It is to notice which supports work and decide whether access should expand, stay the same or shrink.

## DEEPER

Start with a five-part readiness check. First, define the contact need in one sentence. Second, ask whether the child follows comparable routines—returning keys, keeping agreed times, asking for help—without turning these into a character test. Third, choose the least capable device that meets the need. Fourth, write a short agreement covering sleep, purchases, privacy, school and respectful contact. Fifth, decide the review date before the device arrives.

For a 30-day launch, configure accounts and updates together. Keep the device out of the bedroom overnight. Practise four scenarios: a message from an unknown person, a request for a password or code, an upsetting group chat and a lost phone. The safe response should include stopping, saving evidence when appropriate and telling a trusted adult.

At the review, use observations rather than a prosecution. Did sleep or morning routines change? Did the child conceal problems or bring them forward? Were purchases and downloads within the agreement? Did adults follow their own no-phone moments? Add only one new capability at a time, so its effects are visible.

If harassment, threats, sexual contact or coercion appears, routine experimentation ends. Preserve relevant evidence, use platform and school safeguards where appropriate, and seek qualified local help. The child should not carry the investigation alone.

## REFLECT

A first phone is often described as a gift, but it is better understood as a small transfer of power. Good preparation does not demand perfect judgment from a child. It builds a system in which mistakes can be disclosed, access can be adjusted and trust can grow without pretending that technology has only one door.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** A large review of anger-management research found that activities which lower physical arousal generally reduce anger, while arousal-increasing activities do not reliably “get anger out.”

**What it means.** The familiar pressure-cooker metaphor is misleading. Anger is not a fixed quantity that must be discharged by shouting, smashing or repeatedly retelling the offence.

**Risks and impact.** Aggressive venting can rehearse the grievance and reward the feeling of force. Silence and suppression are not the answer either; unaddressed anger can still damage decisions and relationships.

**What can be done.** Lower the body's activation first, name the specific wrong or need, then decide whether the next step is a request, a boundary, a repair conversation or no immediate action.

**What to watch.** After expressing anger, notice whether you have gained clarity and choice—or merely a faster heartbeat and a more polished case against the other person.

## FACTS

The popular catharsis theory says that anger builds like steam and that forceful expression releases it. A 2024 meta-analysis in *Clinical Psychology Review* tested the practical assumption behind that image. Sophie Kjærvik and Brad Bushman combined 154 studies involving 10,189 participants and separated activities that raise physiological arousal from those that lower it.

Arousal-decreasing activities produced a moderate reduction in anger and aggression across the reviewed studies. These included relaxation, slow breathing, mindfulness, meditation, progressive muscle relaxation, slow-flow yoga and taking a timeout. Arousal-increasing activities were ineffective overall. The results varied by activity: jogging was more likely to increase anger, while some ball sports and physical-education activities had different effects, possibly because play adds positive emotion and social structure.

This does not mean anger should be denied. It means that hitting, shouting or rehearsing an injury is not a dependable way to become less angry. Expression, regulation and problem-solving are three different jobs.

## EVIDENCE

The 2024 review is stronger than a single laboratory demonstration because it draws together many populations, settings and delivery methods. The reported overall effect for arousal-decreasing activities was a standardised reduction of 0.63, with the confidence interval remaining on the side of reduced anger. Benefits appeared in laboratories and everyday settings, online and in person, and among people with varied backgrounds.

An influential 2002 experiment helps explain the mechanism. Participants who had been provoked hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who angered them, hit it while thinking about fitness, or did not hit it. The rumination group later reported more anger and behaved more aggressively. Doing nothing was more effective than punching while rehearsing the grievance.

A separate 2025 meta-analysis of 81 studies and 115 effect sizes found that anger was consistently associated with more rumination, avoidance and suppression, while acceptance and cognitive reappraisal were associated with less anger. These are associations, not proof that one strategy caused every outcome, and the authors reported substantial variation among studies. Together, the evidence supports a sequence—not a universal cure: reduce arousal, then work with meaning and action.

## PERSPECTIVES

The catharsis frame correctly recognises that people need somewhere for intense emotion to go. It fails when “somewhere” becomes aggressive rehearsal. The immediate sensation of release can be real, but relief is not the same as reduced anger. A behaviour can feel rewarding precisely because it strengthens the pattern.

The suppression frame makes the opposite mistake. If venting is unhelpful, it concludes that mature people should swallow anger. The 2025 evidence does not support that tidy reversal: suppression was positively associated with anger. A calm exterior can hide the same looping story and leave the original problem untouched.

The regulation frame treats anger as information plus activation. The activation narrows attention and prepares the body to act. The information may point to unfairness, obstruction, betrayal, fear or a crossed boundary. Cooling the activation creates room to test that interpretation and choose a proportionate response.

There is also a relationship frame. Telling a trusted person, “I am angry because this promise was broken,” can organise experience and prepare a clear request. Telling the story for the tenth time while adding evidence for the prosecution may be rumination with an audience. The difference is not whether words are spoken. It is whether the conversation increases flexibility or hardens one version of events.

## CONTEXT

The pressure-cooker image has a long cultural life because it matches the physical experience of anger: heat, muscle tension, quickened pulse and an urge toward movement. But emotions are not stored fluids. They are changing systems of bodily arousal, attention, interpretation, memory and action.

This helps explain why exercise produces mixed results. Regular physical activity can support health and mood, and play can interrupt a grievance. In the acute moment, however, hard exertion may keep arousal high. The 2024 review did not conclude that running is bad; it concluded that raising arousal is not, by itself, an effective anger-reduction mechanism.

Likewise, “take a breath” is not a solution to injustice. It is a way to regain enough range to decide what the injustice requires. A person may still need to document what happened, leave a conversation, state a boundary, seek mediation or return with a specific request. Regulation changes the quality of the response, not the legitimacy of the concern.

The limitations matter. Meta-analyses combine studies with different definitions of anger, interventions and populations. Average effects do not predict the experience of every individual. The 2025 review found high heterogeneity, meaning results varied substantially. Persistent, frightening or dangerous anger is not a self-help experiment; it may warrant support from a qualified professional.

## DEEPER

Anger wants a verdict quickly. It selects evidence, recruits old injuries and turns uncertainty into motive: “They knew exactly what they were doing.” Sometimes the verdict is correct. Sometimes anger has noticed a real violation before polite thought catches up. But speed is not the same as accuracy.

Venting can become attractive because it preserves moral clarity. Each retelling puts us back in the role of the wronged person and the other person back in the dock. Calming down can feel like surrender, as though a slower heartbeat acquits them. It does not. The facts remain available after the body leaves fight mode.

Processing anger asks for a more demanding transformation. The emotion must become a sentence that another person could answer: what happened, what mattered, what needs to change. Not every situation deserves a conversation, and not every relationship can repair. Even then, clarity is more useful than combustion.

Perhaps the test is not “Did I express everything?” but “Do I now have more freedom?” Healthy expression enlarges the next move. Rumination shrinks it until only another retelling, another imagined argument or another blow at the punching bag seems possible. Anger may arrive as fire. It need not remain our only source of light.

## PRACTICAL TAKEAWAY

Try a three-stage check before an anger conversation:

1. **Lower the temperature.** Pause the exchange and use a quiet, arousal-reducing activity that is safe for you. The purpose is not to erase the issue but to recover choice.
2. **Write one factual sentence.** Describe the observable event without guessing motive: “The deadline changed after I had confirmed the plan.”
3. **Choose the job.** Do you need to make a request, set a boundary, correct a record, seek repair—or simply avoid acting while angry?

Afterwards, use one diagnostic question: **Am I clearer about the next step, or only more certain that I am right?**

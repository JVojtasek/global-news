## BRIEFLY

**A rainbow is not a coloured bridge fixed at a particular spot.** It is light reaching one observer from many droplets at the right angles.

**The complete geometry is circular.** From ordinary ground level, the horizon and terrain hide much of that circle, leaving the familiar arc.

**Every observer gets a slightly different rainbow.** Moving changes the relevant droplets, even when two people describe the same shower.

**The colour order records how light travels through water.** Refraction, internal reflection and wavelength-dependent bending separate white sunlight into a spectrum.

**The practical trick is positional.** Put the Sun behind you, look toward rain or mist, and remember that a low Sun places more of the circle above your horizon.

## FACTS

The simplest drawing of a rainbow begins with a line from the Sun through your head to the point in the sky directly opposite the Sun. That point is called the antisolar point. The primary rainbow appears at an angular radius of roughly 42 degrees around it. The visible band is therefore the rim of a circle centred not on a distant hill or cloud, but on a direction defined by the Sun and the observer.

Sunlight entering a raindrop bends because light travels at a different speed in water than in air. It reflects from the back of the drop, bends again as it exits, and some of it travels toward your eyes. Different wavelengths bend by slightly different amounts. Red reaches you at the outer edge of the primary bow and violet at the inner edge. NOAA describes the primary bow as the result of one internal reflection; a fainter secondary bow results from two and reverses the colour order.

The raindrops are not tiny slides carrying complete miniature rainbows. A drop at one angle sends a narrow range of wavelengths toward you; another drop sends another. Your eye assembles the band from an enormous population of droplets occupying different directions.

## EVIDENCE

NOAA’s explanation states that a full rainbow is a complete circle and notes that a viewer in an aircraft can, in suitable conditions, see it. Its interactive simulator makes the relationship testable: change the Sun’s elevation or the observer’s position and the visible bow changes. That matters because it corrects two persistent intuitions at once. The first is that the rainbow sits in the rain like a physical arch. The second is that its ends touch a shared place on the ground.

They do not. If you walk toward an apparent end, your antisolar direction moves with you and a new set of droplets supplies the light. There is no reachable foot of the bow and therefore no optical location for the proverbial pot of gold.

The Met Office adds useful detail about secondary rainbows. A second internal reflection makes the outer bow weaker and reverses its colours. The darker strip often visible between the primary and secondary bows is called Alexander’s band. It is not a cloud shadow painted between two objects; it follows from the directions in which the droplets do, and do not, send much light toward the observer.

UCAR’s educational account places rainbows among other appearances of the sky produced by scattering and the behaviour of light in the atmosphere. That broader frame is useful: what looks like a coloured object is an interaction among illumination, particles or droplets, geometry and a particular eye or camera.

## PERSPECTIVES

There are two honest ways to describe a rainbow, and confusion begins when they are mixed. In everyday language, it is entirely reasonable to say that a rainbow is “over the field.” That identifies the direction in which you see it. In optical language, however, the rainbow is not located over that field in the same way as a tree or a tower. It is an observer-dependent pattern.

That does not make it unreal. A reflection in a mirror depends on viewpoint too, yet it is produced by lawful, measurable paths of light. The better distinction is not real versus imaginary, but object versus phenomenon. A chair remains where it is when you move. A rainbow is reconstructed from a new set of light paths.

Photography can make the distinction harder to notice. A photograph fixes one observer’s geometry and turns the rainbow into a stable shape on a screen. The image can then be shared by millions of people, although the original phenomenon lasted only for the camera’s position and moment.

## CONTEXT

Why do full circles seem rare? For a person standing on flat ground, the antisolar point is usually at or below the horizon. Much of the rainbow’s circle would therefore lie below the visible landscape. From a high viewpoint—with rain or mist below and the Sun behind—the lower part may no longer be blocked. Aircraft, mountaintops and waterfalls can provide that arrangement.

The Sun’s height also changes the composition. When the Sun is low, the antisolar point rises and a large arc can stand above the horizon. When the Sun climbs high, the primary bow is pushed lower. At midday in some seasons and latitudes, the necessary circle may be largely below the horizon even if rain is falling in front of you.

Droplet size, brightness and background affect appearance, but the circle’s basic angular geometry is robust. A spray from a garden hose can produce the same logic at close range. You are not making a different kind of rainbow; you are supplying convenient droplets and choosing the geometry yourself.

## DEEPER

The most revealing fact is that two people standing apart do not receive their rainbow light from precisely the same droplets. Each person has a different antisolar line and a different cone of contributing rays. They can point to what conversationally seems like the same bow, yet the physical paths into their eyes differ.

That is a small lesson in measurement. Observations are not merely passive pictures of objects waiting to be collected. They are events produced by the world, an instrument or body, and a position. Science becomes more precise when it asks not only “What is there?” but also “From where, under what illumination, and by what path did the signal arrive?”

### What you can try

After a shower, stand with the Sun behind you and look toward the darker rain. If the Sun is low, scan a broad part of the sky. With safe footing, change position by several metres and notice that the bow keeps its relation to you rather than to a landmark. A fine garden spray can demonstrate the same effect: keep the Sun behind you, direct the spray away from it, and move until the colours appear. Never stare at the Sun or trade safe footing for a better view.

## REFLECT

An arc feels like a thing with two ends. A rainbow is more interesting: a complete circle partly hidden by the ground, assembled for one viewpoint from countless droplets. The missing half was not removed. It was outside the window that your position allowed you to see.

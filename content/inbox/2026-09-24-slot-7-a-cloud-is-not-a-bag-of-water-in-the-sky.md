## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** A cumulus cloud appears to sit in the sky even though it contains liquid water or ice, materials denser than the surrounding air.

**What it means.** The cloud is not one object with a boundary. It is a region where air motion and temperature allow countless tiny droplets or crystals to exist long enough to scatter light.

**Risks and impact.** Saying clouds simply “float” can hide the continual falling, rising, condensing and evaporating that produces the visible shape.

**What can be done.** Watch a small cloud's edges for ten minutes. Growth on one side and disappearance on another reveal a process that a still photograph freezes.

**What to watch.** When droplets or ice particles grow, their fall speed increases. Strong updrafts can keep them in the cloud longer, but eventually some become precipitation.

## FACTS

A cloud begins when moist air cools enough for water vapour to condense or deposit onto tiny particles called condensation nuclei. UCAR's teaching material describes dust and pollen as examples. The new droplets are extraordinarily small and numerous. Together they scatter enough light to make a white or grey form visible from the ground.

Gravity never switches off. Each liquid droplet is denser than air and begins to settle. But a very small sphere encounters substantial drag relative to its mass, so its terminal speed can be slight. Air itself is also moving—rising through convection, forced over terrain, lifted along fronts and mixed by turbulence.

The result is not a parcel of water suspended as a single thing. A cloud is more like a busy border. Vapour becomes droplets where conditions allow; droplets evaporate where air becomes warmer or drier. The familiar outline marks the region in which that balance is visible at a particular moment.

## EVIDENCE

UCAR explains the formation sequence: water evaporates, moist air rises and cools, and some vapour condenses on nuclei. It also states that the water droplets or ice crystals are small and light enough to remain aloft. “Remain aloft” does not mean motionless. It means their settling is slow relative to the motion and lifetime of the cloudy air.

National Weather Service training material shows how the story changes as droplets grow. Continued condensation is slow; collisions and coalescence can build larger drops more quickly. It gives a vivid comparison: a droplet with a radius of 0.05 millimetres falls far more slowly than a drop ten times that radius. Strong cloud updrafts increase the time droplets remain together and therefore the opportunities for collision.

The World Meteorological Organization's Cloud Atlas describes cumulus congestus as mainly water droplets, with ice crystals possible in sufficiently cold parts and occasional raindrops. A cloud can therefore contain a spectrum rather than one standard particle: tiny droplets, larger drops and sometimes ice, each moving differently.

## PERSPECTIVES

### The object view

Language gives a cloud a noun and the eye gives it an edge. That makes it natural to imagine a cotton-like object travelling intact. In reality, the material entering the visible region is not necessarily the material leaving it. A stable-looking cap over a mountain can persist while air continuously flows through it, condensing on the windward side and evaporating downwind.

### The weight view

Popular explanations sometimes calculate a cloud's total water and ask how something so heavy stays up. The calculation can create wonder, but “heavy” is not enough to predict motion. A tonne distributed among microscopic droplets across a vast volume behaves differently from a tonne collected in a tank. Particle size, drag, air currents and phase changes determine what the water does.

### The weather view

For a forecaster, a growing tower is evidence about instability and vertical motion, not merely scenery. The puffy top shows where rising air remains buoyant and condensation continues. Darkening, vertical growth and precipitation can signal that the microphysics and airflow have changed. Yet appearance alone does not provide a complete local forecast; cloud type must be read with radar, wind, humidity and other observations.

## CONTEXT

The phrase “water vapour cloud” causes another confusion. Water vapour is an invisible gas. What makes an ordinary cloud visible is condensed liquid droplets, ice crystals or both. The white plume above a kettle is visible only after vapour has cooled into droplets; the invisible portion nearest the spout is closer to the gaseous state.

Cloud colour comes from light and depth. Many small droplets scatter visible wavelengths broadly, producing white when sunlight passes through a modest thickness. A deep cloud can look grey underneath because less direct light reaches the base. Grey does not mean the water itself changed colour.

Edges reveal humidity. A crisp cauliflower-shaped cumulus is growing into surrounding air where droplets can persist. Ragged wisps may mark mixing and evaporation. The whole form can move with the wind while individual parts grow and vanish, which is why tracking “the same cloud” quickly becomes a philosophical as well as meteorological problem.

Precipitation begins when particles become large enough to fall through the cloud and survive the journey below. Warm clouds can grow drops through collision and coalescence. In colder regions, ice crystals can grow at the expense of supercooled droplets, aggregate and later melt into rain. Not every particle reaches the surface; some precipitation evaporates into drier air below.

## DEEPER

### A process you can watch

Choose a day with scattered small cumulus clouds and stand where the horizon is open. Pick one cloud and note a distinctive notch. Do not stare only at its centre. Watch the bright, expanding edge, the softer downwind edge and the gap between it and its neighbour.

After a few minutes, the notch may fill while another opens. A tower can rise although its base seems fixed. A fragment may fade without visibly falling. Those changes are the visible result of invisible fields: temperature, humidity and motion determining where droplets exist.

This observation does not identify dangerous weather and should not replace an official forecast. Its value is conceptual. It turns “Why does that heavy thing float?” into better questions: how small are the particles, how fast is the air moving, and where are droplets forming faster than they disappear?

The reader outcome is to stop treating the cloud as a contradiction. Water is falling under gravity. Air is moving. Phase changes continuously create and erase the particles that make the cloud visible. A shape can endure even when its ingredients do not stay put—like a wave in a stadium or flame above a burner. The enduring thing is the pattern.

## REFLECT

How many other apparently solid things in daily life are better understood as places where a process keeps happening?

When a shape persists while its ingredients continually change, what exactly are we recognising as the same thing—the material, the boundary or the pattern?

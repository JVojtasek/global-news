With AI news, it isn't enough to follow the excitement around the story — the key is to distinguish how much of a company's value lies in distant profits, because those are exactly what rising bond yields cheapen the fastest.

When a politician calls the fear of AI a "hoax," the tech market hears a turbo boost — but rising government bond yields are a handbrake pulled in the middle of the highway.

The morning market roundup linked two things that at first glance point in opposite directions: Donald Trump played down concerns about artificial intelligence, while US government bond yields jumped. Alongside that came a warning from Brian Moynihan of Bank of America (BAC), but without detail in the excerpt it's fair to treat it more as another macro signal than as a standalone investment thesis.

The most interesting thing here isn't Trump's line about AI. More interesting is the duel between two prices: the price of the story and the price of time — and above all the fact that not every AI stock has the same "waiting period."

The market often throws growth companies into one bag: chips, cloud, software, data centers, all one big AI party. But for valuation it makes a fundamental difference whether a company earns a lot of cash already today, or whether the investor is mainly paying for the promise of a distant future. This is called equity duration: simply put, how far away in time the money you're paying for today lies.

AI stocks today often live not just on what they will earn this year, but on the idea of how much cash they could generate in several years. That makes them something like an expensive restaurant promising a Michelin-starred future — great, as long as its rent doesn't shoot up. Bond yields are exactly that "rent on time": when they rise, future profits carry less weight in today's prices.

A mini example: if an investor expects 100 dollars of free cash flow in 5 years, at a 4% discount rate its present value is roughly 82 dollars. At 5% it's about 78 dollars. The difference didn't arise because the company sold fewer chips. It arose because the market made time more expensive.

But the second layer here is more important. Company A gets those 100 dollars only in 5 years: in this simplification, the shift from 4% to 5% takes about 4 dollars off its present value. Company B has 50 dollars already today and another 50 in 5 years: its value falls roughly from 91 to 89 dollars. The same AI story, the same rate shift, but different pain. That's why the question isn't "AI yes/no," but "how much of the valuation lies in the future and how much is already flowing through the account today."

The counterintuitive point: a politically friendlier mood around AI can help the narrative, but higher yields can at the same time take away part of the valuation. That's why the market reaction can be mixed: excitement in the headlines, nervousness in multiples such as P/E or EV/EBITDA.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It may help companies directly linked to AI infrastructure, if the market believes regulation won't slow investment in data centers: chipmakers such as NVIDIA (NVDA), Advanced Micro Devices (AMD), contract chipmaker Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSM) or network infrastructure suppliers such as Broadcom (AVGO). The mechanism is simple: less political pressure can mean less worry about restrictions on deploying AI systems.

But it can hurt the same stocks through valuation. The most sensitive aren't automatically all "tech," but mainly companies for which investors pay predominantly for future expansion, not for today's stable cash flow. By contrast, companies that already generate high free cash flow today and have recurring revenue tend to be relatively more resilient to rising yields than younger stories built mainly on future monetization.

Higher yields may look better in the short term for banks such as Bank of America (BAC), JPMorgan Chase (JPM) or Wells Fargo (WFC), because interest-bearing assets can earn more. But bank math has a catch: if deposit costs also rise quickly, net interest margin weakens. And if higher rates choke demand for loans or increase defaults, the joy fades.

On the other hand, long government bonds, real estate funds and utilities tend to come under pressure — for example NextEra Energy (NEE) or American Tower (AMT) as examples of more rate-sensitive assets. Their problem isn't "a bad business," but competition: a safer bond with a higher yield raises the bar for everything else.

With news like this, separate two layers: the story, the rate, and one more sublayer — the time profile of cash. The story says why revenue could grow. The rate says what that growth is actually worth today. And the time profile says how much it hurts the company when waiting gets more expensive.

A checklist for reading the market better:

- For AI companies, watch customer capex, order backlog, gross margin, free cash flow and comments on supply constraints in their reports.

- Ask: does the company earn a large part of its value already today, or does the valuation rest mainly on profits 5–10 years out? That's a practical test of "equity duration."

- For banks, look in quarterly results for net interest income, deposit costs, credit loss provisions and net charge-offs.

- For bond-sensitive sectors, compare the dividend yield with the yield on safer government bonds.

- Don't mistake a political sentence for a change in earnings. The market can applaud a headline and at the same time reprice valuations downward.

"Equity duration" is like the difference between a paycheck today and the promise of a bonus in five years. If your boss gives you the money right away, you don't much care that loans got more expensive in the meantime. But if they promise you a big bonus sometime in the future, it suddenly matters a lot what waiting costs. In the market, this means: companies with a beautiful but distant story are hurt more by rising yields than companies that already have cash flowing into the till. For stocks, it's the difference between "fantasy still has a price" and "fantasy just got a more expensive receipt."

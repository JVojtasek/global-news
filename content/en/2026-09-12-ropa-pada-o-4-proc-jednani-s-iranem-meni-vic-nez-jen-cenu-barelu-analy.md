Watch specific indicators such as the war-risk premium and export volumes to tell short-term expectations apart from a fundamental change in supply and demand.

WTI and Brent oil prices fell by roughly 4% after reports emerged, according to Reuters on May 15, 2024, that the Persian Gulf states are preparing to hold talks with Iran. Markets are reacting with profit-taking, which suggests a reduction in the geopolitical risk factor that had previously kept oil prices high.

It's like when a rumor spreads in poker that one of the players has a "tell" – a tiny hint that gives away the strength of his cards. Everyone starts betting against him, and the stakes on his losing rise. But then news comes that this player has agreed a truce with his biggest rival, and suddenly the "tell" becomes meaningless. The bets are taken off the table because expectations have changed. This is exactly how the war risk premium works in oil markets. It isn't about actual war, but about the expectation of war. And that expectation is now lower. According to analyses, this premium, which in recent weeks reached as much as 5–7 USD per barrel, is now starting to shrink.

What most people overlook, though, is the broader context. Talks with Iran aren't only about oil supplies, but about a reconfiguration of regional power. Iran is a key player in the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly 20% of the world's oil flows. Any agreement that led to an easing of Iranian sanctions could bring additional millions of barrels of oil per day to the market – estimates speak of a potential of 0.5 to 1.5 million barrels per day within 6–12 months. But more importantly, it could change the dynamics of regional conflicts that affect the stability of supply. It's not just about 'more oil', but about 'a more stable route for oil'. And that is a difference that will show up in the barrel price only with a delay, not immediately, and will affect long-term investment models, for example through a lower WACC discount rate for energy projects.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

- Consumers and carriers: Lower oil prices mean cheaper gasoline and diesel. That's good news for airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL), which have lower fuel costs. For DAL, fuel costs represent a significant part of operating costs, typically 20–30% of total operating costs, and a 4% drop in the oil price can have a measurable impact on their margins, if it isn't a short-term swing. Their sensitivity to the oil price is high, although they often hedge against price swings. Lower fuel prices also increase consumers' purchasing power, which can have a positive impact on discretionary spending.

- Cost-sensitive sectors: Producers of chemicals, plastics and fertilizers that use oil as a raw material, for example Dow Inc. (DOW). Lower input costs can improve their profit margins. For Dow, where petrochemical feedstocks are key, a 4% drop in the oil price can lead to an increase in operating margin of tens of basis points, depending on their cost structure and ability to pass savings on to customers.

- Oil and gas companies: Firms such as ExxonMobil (XOM) or Chevron (CVX), whose profitability is directly tied to the oil price. A 4% drop shows up immediately in their revenue and potentially in their profitability too. For smaller producers with higher production costs, such as APA Corporation (APA) (with average production costs of around 25–35 USD/barrel), the impact can be even more pronounced, because their margin per barrel shrinks. Their earnings yield (earnings per share divided by the share price) may decline, making them less attractive compared with risk-free yields such as US Treasury yields (e.g. the 10-year Treasury yield).

- Countries dependent on oil exports: States such as Saudi Arabia or the United Arab Emirates, whose state budgets depend heavily on oil revenue. Lower prices mean lower revenue and potentially less room for public investment. For example, Saudi Arabia needs an oil price of around 80 USD/barrel for a balanced budget, so a 4% drop below this level would mean a budget deficit.

With news like this, always ask yourself: is this just a short-term reaction to expectations, or a fundamental change in supply and demand? Watch not only the headlines, but also specific indicators. For example, how the war-risk premium for shipping in the Strait of Hormuz develops (measured as the difference in insurance rates for tankers compared with regular routes, where a drop of 0.5–1% of the total cargo value is a significant signal). If this premium is falling, it's a strong indicator of reduced risk. Also watch whether oil export volumes from Iran are actually increasing (e.g. through tanker movement data – AIS gaps, satellite images that can show an increase of 100–200 thousand barrels per day). Short-term drops in oil prices may be just noise if the underlying dynamics of supply and demand or the geopolitical risk don't change for a longer period. Also pay attention to crack spreads (the difference between the price of crude oil and refined products), which indicate refinery margins and demand for fuels.

Imagine you own a car repair shop and the price of spare parts suddenly falls because the manufacturers have agreed better terms with raw-material suppliers. That's great, because your repair will be cheaper to make and you can either lower the price for customers (which will please them), or keep a bigger profit for yourself. For you as the shop owner, it's positive. But for the raw-material suppliers who sell them, it means they get less money for their work. And this is exactly how a drop in the oil price works. For people who buy oil (like drivers or airlines), it's good news, because they save. For those who extract and sell oil (oil companies and oil states), it means smaller profits. The talks with Iran are like that agreement between manufacturers and suppliers – they change the rules of the game and affect who takes how much money out of the whole chain.

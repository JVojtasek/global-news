With AI cloud it isn't enough to track CapEx and revenue growth; the key is to separate independent demand from consumption created by partnerships, equity investments, cloud credits and purchase commitments.

If the AI rally woke up tomorrow with a hangover, the worst headache might not land on the stocks themselves but on the order books of factories, power plants and data centers. The report argues that the US hyperscalers — Alphabet/Google (GOOGL), Microsoft (MSFT), Meta (META), Oracle (ORCL) and Amazon (AMZN) — have become the backbone of the AI market, because their sharp capital expenditures keep much of the optimism and the supply chain alive. At the same time, according to the authors, it raises questions of moral hazard, circular financing and whether their stumble wouldn't be a systemic problem similar to the role of banks in 2008.

The most accurate parallel is perhaps not "banks 2008" but "the telecom boom of 2000 crossed with banking leverage." Banks back then held the system together through credit; today, hyperscalers hold the AI economy together through orders. That is a subtle but crucial difference.

When a bank cuts lending, the oxygen for companies dries up. When a hyperscaler cuts CapEx — that is, investment into servers, chips, networks and data centers — credit doesn't dry up; the future revenues of suppliers do. That is why the risk may be less visible on balance sheets and more in expectations. The market isn't just looking at today's profits of NVIDIA (NVDA) or TSMC (TSM), but at the belief that the giant conveyor belt of orders will keep running.

Circular financing is the most interesting part here. If a large cloud firm invests in an AI startup, which then spends on its cloud, and the market reads that as demand growth, it resembles a shopping mall that gives a teenager pocket money and then celebrates record sales in its stores. It isn't automatically fraud or a bubble. But analytically it is necessary to distinguish demand from an independent customer and demand that is partly manufactured by one's own capital, cloud credits or a purchase commitment.

The more practical question therefore is: where exactly did the dollar of revenue come from? For partnerships of the big-cloud + AI-modeler type it pays to look for three tracks. First, equity investments in cash flow from investing activities and in the notes on financial investments. Second, cloud credits or customer incentives in management commentary, because consumption that is "free" or discounted isn't the same as an independent customer paying full price. Third, minimum purchase commitments, backlog or RPO — that is, contracted future revenue: they look nice, but the key is whether real demand sits behind them or a contract tied to financing.

Moral hazard here needn't mean a government bailout. In the report's framing it can mean the market's conviction that "the AI race can't be stopped," so every increase in spending is read as courage rather than possible overcooking of the cycle.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It helps suppliers, provided CapEx keeps running: AI chip makers like NVIDIA (NVDA) and AMD (AMD), foundries like Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSM), network firms like Arista Networks (ANET), specialty chip suppliers like Broadcom (AVGO), and data center infrastructure like Vertiv (VRT) or Eaton (ETN). The mechanism is simple: hyperscaler orders spill into revenues for accelerators, servers, switches, cooling and power. The weak spot is concentration: the more revenue rests on a few giant customers and one generation of AI infrastructure, the more even small deadline shifts hurt.

It's mixed for the hyperscalers themselves. Microsoft (MSFT), Amazon (AMZN) and Alphabet (GOOGL) may benefit from higher demand for cloud, but at the same time they bear the risk that investment outlays outrun the monetization of AI services. Meta (META) additionally has sensitivity to the ad cycle: if AI infrastructure doesn't translate into better ad and product performance, the market may start pressing more on return on investment. Oracle (ORCL) is interesting via its cloud expansion, but with such stories the key is to watch whether growth isn't too dependent on a few big contracts and whether it is cloud consumption customers would renew once incentives expire.

A sudden slowdown could hurt firms with high dependence on one type of AI order, and also utilities or energy projects tied to data centers, if the expected electricity consumption shifts into the future.

A better trader asks on reports like this not "is AI a bubble?" but "where exactly does the risk sit in the chain?" Checklist: 1) Is CapEx growing faster than operating cash flow, that is, cash from the normal business? 2) How long will AI servers be depreciated and won't they age technologically sooner? 3) How much demand comes from independent customers and how much from partnerships, investments or cloud credits? 4) Are supplier orders cancellable or firmly contracted? 5) Is the limit chips, power, cooling, or firms' real willingness to pay for AI?

Add one more check: compare cloud growth with RPO/backlog, customer incentives, concentration of large buyers and investments in AI partners. The biggest trap is to confuse accounting growth with economic demand. In the AI cycle both things can be true at once: the technology can be revolutionary and the valuation of part of the chain can still be sensitive to a single word — a slowdown in spending.

"Circular financing" is like a bartender lending money to a friend, the friend then drinking it all up at his bar, and the bartender then reporting a great evening. The revenue is real, the beer really was sold. The question is whether the same tab would have arrived if the friend hadn't been handed the money up front. For the market this means: if part of AI demand arises thanks to money, credits or commitments from the same giants that then report it as cloud growth, the stocks may be more sensitive to a spending slowdown than the headline numbers suggest.

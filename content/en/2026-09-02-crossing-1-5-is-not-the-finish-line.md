## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** UNEP's new *Limiting Overshoot* report says long-term global warming is likely to pass 1.5°C within the next few years.

**What it means.** The threshold is not a planetary switch. Risk grows with every additional fraction of a degree and with the time spent above the line.

**Risks and impact.** A higher, longer peak makes heat, ecosystem loss, food and water stress, infrastructure damage and irreversible changes more likely.

**What can be done.** Readers can test climate claims by asking whether a number describes one day, one year, a multi-decade average, a projected peak or an end-of-century outcome.

**What to watch.** Updated national pledges, actual annual emissions and the projected peak before COP31 will show whether the curve is being bent, not merely renamed.

## FACTS

UNEP published *Limiting Overshoot* on 2 September. It says exceedance of 1.5°C above the 1850–1900 average is now widely assessed as unavoidable under current policies and plausible near-term pathways. The report describes the remaining course as “overshoot, peak and decline”: pass the threshold, keep the peak as low as possible, then try to bring temperatures down.

Its most optimistic scenario peaks at about 1.8°C if existing national pledges are implemented. Reuters reports that current policies instead point to median warming of roughly 2.6°C by 2100. Those are modelled pathways, not forecasts of a precisely dated weather event.

The 1.5°C goal comes from the Paris Agreement, which calls for holding warming well below 2°C while pursuing efforts to limit it to 1.5°C. In 2024, the annual global average already exceeded 1.5°C. That did not by itself establish that the Paris threshold had been permanently crossed, because scientists assess long-term warming over multiple decades.

UNEP says a decline after the peak would require sustained emissions cuts, net-zero followed by net-negative emissions, adaptation and carefully governed carbon-dioxide removal. It also says a return below 1.5°C is possible but highly uncertain.

## EVIDENCE

The central event is well established. UNEP supplies the primary report and release. Reuters and Nature independently reported its central conclusion on the same date. The Paris Agreement supplies the legal text behind the temperature goal.

The important distinction is measurement. A hot day can be more than 1.5°C above a historical daily comparison. A calendar year can average above 1.5°C. The Paris goal concerns human-driven warming over a long period. Collapsing those into one number creates two opposite errors: treating an annual crossing as proof that all goals are lost, or dismissing it because the formal long-term measure has not yet caught up.

The report's pathways are conditional. The 1.8°C peak depends on existing pledges being implemented; the roughly 2.6°C outcome reflects current policies. Neither number is guaranteed. Future emissions, climate response, methane reductions, land use and the scale and durability of carbon removal all affect the curve.

UNEP also puts limits around the hopeful side. Carbon removal can contribute only a few tenths of a degree of reversal this century in modelled pathways. Forest-based removal competes for land and can be undone by fire or drought; engineered removal remains expensive and small. It cannot substitute for cutting emissions.

### Where they agree

Crossing 1.5°C does not make subsequent choices irrelevant. It makes the height, duration and credibility of the return path more important. The central uncertainty is whether governments will build the emissions decline and adaptation capacity that the favourable modelled pathway assumes.

## CONTEXT

A threshold is useful because it concentrates attention. It is misleading when imagined as a cliff with safety on one side and catastrophe on the other. Climate risk behaves more like a dimmer that also activates feedbacks: every increment changes probabilities, while some systems may cross thresholds that cannot easily be reversed.

That is why “overshoot” has three dimensions. Magnitude is the height of the peak. Duration is the time above 1.5°C. Reversibility asks what can recover as temperature falls. These dimensions do not move together automatically. A low peak held for decades may still produce cumulative losses; a later decline can reduce some future risks without undoing ice loss, displacement or extinction.

The report also changes the relationship between mitigation and adaptation. Mitigation shapes how far the curve rises. Adaptation shapes how much harm a given level of warming causes. Weak mitigation makes adaptation harder and eventually impossible in some places; weak adaptation turns avoidable exposure into damage while the world waits for emissions policy to work.

### Practical impact

When a headline uses 1.5°C, locate the denominator and the clock. Is it one location, the whole globe, one day, one year or a multi-decade trend? Is the number observed or modelled? Does a projection assume current policies, announced pledges or a new intervention? Those three checks turn an alarming number into an interpretable claim.

The next useful signal is not another declaration that 1.5°C matters. It is whether global emissions enter a sustained decline and whether updated pledges lower the projected peak with funded policies behind them.

## DEEPER

Public goals often do two jobs at once. They describe a desired outcome and organize effort. When the outcome slips out of reach, people are tempted to protect the symbol or discard the effort. Both responses confuse the line with the landscape.

A train missing its scheduled arrival has not made destination, speed and further delay meaningless. Yet the analogy has limits: the climate does not wait unchanged at the station. Time above a warmer level accumulates consequences, and the route back may pass through a world that cannot be restored to its earlier state.

That makes overshoot an uncomfortable form of responsibility. It removes the clean story in which one deadline settles whether humanity succeeded. The task becomes comparative and continuous: less warming rather than more, a shorter exceedance rather than a longer one, earlier protection rather than compensation after loss.

### Reader outcome

**What changed.** UNEP now frames sustained exceedance of 1.5°C as unavoidable and an overshoot, peak and decline pathway as the best remaining course.

**Why it matters.** The news narrows the set of plausible futures, but it does not collapse them into one outcome. Peak temperature, duration above the threshold and adaptation will determine much of the difference.

**What to watch, not what to do.** Through COP31 and the next round of emissions reporting, watch whether updated policies reduce the projected peak and whether global emissions decline for several consecutive years.

**What would change our minds.** Stronger observed emissions reductions and credible removal at scale would improve the return outlook; higher policy-pathway projections or continued emissions growth would weaken it.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Pope Leo XIV used a September 25 address at UNESCO to argue that artificial intelligence can organise data but cannot answer the question of what human life means.

**What it means.** The useful boundary is not between people who use AI and people who refuse it. It is between assistance and delegation: a machine can propose words, options and patterns, while a person still owns the purpose, the judgement and the consequences.

**Risks and impact.** Fluent output can make uncertainty look settled. Under pressure, people may accept advice they cannot defend or allow an automated recommendation to hide who actually made a consequential choice.

**What can be done.** Before using AI for a decision, name the decision, the person affected and the person accountable. Use the system to widen the option set, then verify material claims and record the human reason for acting.

**What to watch.** Better explanations and more accurate models matter, but neither automatically creates appropriate trust. The decisive test is whether people can question, override and take responsibility for the result.

## FACTS

Pope Leo XIV addressed more than 1,400 people at UNESCO headquarters in Paris on September 25, 2026. UNESCO described it as the first visit by a pope to a United Nations agency outside Italy in 46 years. His subject was wider than artificial intelligence, but AI supplied one of the speech's sharpest distinctions.

More information, he said, does not necessarily produce wisdom, just as faster communication does not necessarily produce more truth. Artificial intelligence can correlate data and provide answers. It cannot answer the question of the meaning of human life, because that question is rooted in lived experience rather than information alone.

The claim was philosophical, but it also matched a practical governance principle adopted by UNESCO's General Conference. Its 2021 Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence says that human oversight must be possible and that ultimate responsibility and accountability must remain with natural or legal persons. An AI system should not acquire a legal personality that absorbs blame.

This does not make AI useless. It identifies the job the system cannot inherit: being answerable for why a consequential choice was made.

## EVIDENCE

The strongest case for that boundary is not that machines never outperform people. They often do narrow tasks faster and can surface patterns a person would miss. The problem is that performance and reliance are different questions. A useful adviser must be followed when it is right and resisted when it is wrong.

Researchers Max Schemmer, Niklas Kühl, Carina Benz, Andrea Bartos and Gerhard Satzger tested that problem with 200 participants completing an AI-assisted decision task. Their 2023 study defined “appropriate reliance” as accepting correct AI advice while rejecting incorrect advice. Explanations changed how participants relied on the system, but they did not provide a simple cure for over-reliance or under-reliance. Design can influence trust; it cannot manufacture judgement on demand.

The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology makes the same point from a risk-management direction. Its AI Risk Management Framework describes AI as socio-technical: effects arise not only from a model, but from the people, procedures and context around it. The framework calls for documented human oversight, knowledge limits and clear roles.

The evidence does not prove that every AI-assisted decision needs the same level of review. It supports a more modest conclusion: model quality alone cannot tell an organisation when reliance is appropriate.

## PERSPECTIVES

One view treats the problem mainly as engineering. Make models more accurate, reveal sources, show confidence and improve explanations; then people can use them well. This view correctly refuses mystical talk about machines. Reliability, testing and interface design matter. But even a perfectly sourced answer cannot decide whether speed, mercy, profit, fairness or loyalty should govern a particular case.

A second view treats AI as a rival to human dignity. It notices how readily a person can be reduced to a score and how quickly synthetic language can occupy spaces once reserved for conversation. Pope Leo's warning belongs partly here. Yet this frame can become too broad. A tool that translates a public form, drafts alternatives or helps a doctor search a record may enlarge human agency rather than diminish it.

A third view treats AI as an adviser inside a deliberately drawn decision boundary. The system may calculate, compare, criticise and rehearse. A person or institution must still decide the objective, check the evidence, hear those affected and accept responsibility. This view is less dramatic, and therefore more useful. It asks not whether AI is good or bad, but which part of the work can be delegated without delegating the moral relationship.

That boundary will move with the stakes. A restaurant suggestion can tolerate error. A benefits decision, diagnosis or accusation requires a far more visible path to challenge and appeal.

## CONTEXT

Human beings have always used instruments to extend judgement. A map can reveal a route without choosing a destination. A calculator can prevent an arithmetic mistake without deciding what deserves a budget. What is new about generative AI is not only the scale of its information, but its social form. It answers in sentences. It sounds as if somebody has understood the question.

That fluency compresses several stages that used to be visible. A search showed competing pages; a report exposed authorship and method; advice arrived from a person whose interests could be examined. A generated answer may fuse retrieval, prediction and presentation into one smooth surface. The labour of doubt is still necessary, but the interface no longer makes it obvious.

UNESCO's recommendation therefore links human oversight to transparency, explainability, auditability and the ability to challenge a decision. These are not decorative ethics labels. They are ways of keeping the chain of responsibility from disappearing. Someone should be able to say which system was used, what it was asked, which evidence mattered, who reviewed the output and who had authority to say no.

Meaning presents a related but deeper problem. Meaning is not a hidden fact waiting in a large enough database. It is the relationship between a fact and a life: the promise that binds a person, the loss that changes a priority, the responsibility created by another person's trust. A model can help someone find language for those things. It cannot become the one who promised, lost, trusted or must make amends.

This is why the question “What did the AI say?” is never enough when a decision changes somebody else's options.

## PEOPLE

The temptation to hand over judgement rarely begins with laziness. It often begins with exhaustion. A nurse has too many records, a manager too many applications, a parent too many conflicting warnings, a student an empty page at midnight. An instant answer feels like relief because it removes friction exactly when friction is hardest to tolerate.

Some friction, however, is the work. Reading the exception in a policy, asking the quiet person what the change would cost, admitting that two values conflict—these steps cannot be reduced to prediction without losing something important.

People affected by an automated recommendation also need more than a reassurance that a human was “in the loop.” A ceremonial click is not oversight. The human reviewer needs time, relevant knowledge, authority to disagree and a reason that can be explained. Otherwise the person becomes a rubber stamp while the institution uses the machine as a shield.

For ordinary users, the same principle is smaller but recognisable: do not ask a tool to carry a conversation whose consequence belongs to you.

## DEEPER

A practical way to preserve the boundary is to write a short decision note before opening the AI tool.

First, name the decision in one sentence. “I need three ways to explain this” leaves the decision with you. “Tell me whether to fire this person” disguises a human responsibility as a prompt.

Second, name who can be harmed and who must answer for the result. If those names are difficult to write, the decision probably needs more human contact, not a longer prompt.

Third, give the system an advisory job. Ask it for alternatives, missing assumptions, a counterargument, questions to investigate or a clearer structure. These are ways to enlarge attention. They do not confer authority.

Fourth, verify the material claims at the source. For an irreversible, high-stakes or intimate decision, add an appropriately qualified person and a route for the affected person to respond. “The model was confident” is not evidence, and “the human approved it” is not an explanation.

Fifth, record the human reason. A useful sentence begins: “We chose this because…” If the sentence can only say that the system ranked it first, the decision has no accountable owner yet.

Finally, keep a small no-delegation list. Consent, blame, promises, punishment and the meaning of a relationship deserve special caution because they create obligations between people. AI can help someone prepare for those moments. It should not impersonate the person who must stand inside them.

Pope Leo's distinction does not require a retreat from technology. It asks for a mature use of it. A machine may give us more answers than any generation has held before. The human task is to decide which questions an answer cannot settle—and to remain present where responsibility begins.

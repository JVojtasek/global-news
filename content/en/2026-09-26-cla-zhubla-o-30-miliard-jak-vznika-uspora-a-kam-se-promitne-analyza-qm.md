With deals like this, watch mainly the list of goods affected, the effective date, inventory turnover and whether companies pass the saving into margins or into prices for customers.

In global trade, tariffs are tax sand in the gearbox: they do not stop the car at once, but they eat into power, margins and nerves. According to China's state news agency Xinhua, the United States and China agreed during Xi Jinping's visit to the US on a mutual tariff reduction worth 30 bn USD and on launching a dialogue on AI as part of an eight-point consensus. Importantly, the report does not yet say which items the tariffs cover, when the change takes effect, or whether the AI dialogue changes export licences or controls.

The biggest market point may not be the 30 bn USD itself, but a change in the type of risk: from a sudden geopolitical kick to managed negotiation. The market often prices in less uncertainty before it sees the full effect in company profits. But here is the trap: a tariff cut is not like switching off a tax in an online shop, where the price is recalculated in a second. It is more like a discount on flour for a baker whose storeroom is full of old, expensive flour. First he has to bake his way through the old stock, and only then will you see a cheaper bread roll or a higher margin.

In the accounts, this will show up mainly through cost of goods sold, or COGS: the importer pays the tariff on import and the amount feeds into the purchase cost of inventory. If a company holds inventory bought under the old terms, the relief reaches gross margin with a delay. In cash flow, the effect may come earlier for new shipments after the deal takes effect, but the income statement will only show it when that inventory is sold. In practice it therefore makes sense to connect the effective date with inventory turnover: a company with fast turnover can show the change sooner, while a company with warehouses holding many weeks or months of stock may have it smeared across its results like ketchup under a carpet. That is why it is key to watch the effective date of the deal, the list of tariff lines, inventory turnover and management's comments on the margin bridge.

The AI dialogue is a second layer. It does not mean an automatic loosening of export restrictions on powerful chips or data-centre hardware. It means rather an open channel that can reduce the risk of misunderstanding in an area where every rule is both a trade and a security question.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It may help industry and companies with sensitive supply chains between the US and China, for example machinery makers such as Caterpillar (CAT) or Deere (DE), if the relief touches their inputs or customer demand. For technology companies such as Apple (AAPL), NVIDIA (NVDA) or Advanced Micro Devices (AMD), the effect is subtler: tariffs may help costs and sentiment around supply chains, but the AI dialogue on its own does not lift restrictions on exports of the most advanced chips.

Logistics may see higher volumes if lower tariffs get orders moving. Examples are trans-Pacific carriers and logistics firms such as Matson (MATX), FedEx (FDX) or UPS (UPS). But note: higher volume is not the same as higher profit, because freight rates, fuel and ship capacity can swallow the effect.

It may hurt protected domestic producers if they previously benefited from more expensive import competition. In theory this includes parts of steel, industrial components or materials, for example Nucor (NUE) or Steel Dynamics (STLD), but only if the deal covers their categories. Without the list of items, this is a scenario, not a fact.

With news like this, read three things, not the headline: 1) which goods are included, 2) the effective date, 3) who has the bargaining power to keep the saving in margin rather than cutting the price for customers. In company results, look for the gross margin bridge, the breakdown of the change in gross margin into price, volume, product mix, tariffs, transport and input costs. Then ask yourself a simple investor's check question: does the timing of the relief match inventory turnover? If a company talks about lower tariffs but inventories on the balance sheet are still high, or management does not show when the old expensive goods will be sold, the effect on margins may come later, weaker, or not at all. Also watch the trend in average selling price, comments on inventory, transport costs and whether competition is pushing the saving into lower prices. And with AI, do not confuse dialogue with a licence: a conversation is an open door, not a pass through airport security.

A tariff is like a luggage fee at the airport. When the airline cuts it, travel may become cheaper, but only if the travel agency, the hotel and the tour seller actually pass the discount on to you. For the market this means less friction in trade and a chance of better company margins; for shares it may improve the mood, but for the ordinary person's wallet the effect will appear only when lower costs actually feed through into prices.

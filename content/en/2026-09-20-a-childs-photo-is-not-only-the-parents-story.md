## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Parents have always shown family photographs. Social media changed the audience, the speed and the life of the copy: one post can travel beyond the people a parent meant to include.

**What it means.** A child's image is personal information as well as a memory. Posting is therefore not one decision about a picture, but three decisions about content, audience and duration.

**Risks and impact.** A harmless-looking photo can disclose a school, location or routine. An embarrassing story can become part of a child's searchable history before that child understands what a digital footprint is.

**What can be done.** Ask the child when they can express a preference, remove identifying details, choose the smallest suitable audience and use private delivery for ordinary family updates.

**What to watch.** Privacy settings reduce exposure but do not create control. Other viewers can save, copy or re-share an image, and platform settings and uses can change.

## FACTS

“Sharenting” is the regular sharing of information, photos or videos about children by parents or caregivers. The term is sometimes used as an accusation, but the behaviour covers a wide range: a picture sent to grandparents, a private group post, a public birthday video and a commercial family account are not equivalent.

The Australian eSafety Commissioner says images can reveal full names, school uniforms, clubs, home landmarks, locations and schedules. Digital photos may also contain metadata such as the time, date and GPS coordinates, although some platforms remove or hide it. Ireland's Data Protection Commission treats photos, videos, voices, health information, interests and dislikes as personal data that can be combined into a digital footprint.

A 2024 pilot study surveyed 228 parents of minors at a paediatric outpatient clinic in Rome. Three quarters said they posted content about their children, 31% of those who shared had begun within the child's first six months, and 93% of respondents were unaware of relevant law and risks. The sample was small, clinic-based and mostly mothers, so it cannot establish a universal rate. It does show why “everyone knows the rules” is a poor assumption.

## EVIDENCE

The strongest evidence here does not justify a claim that every family photograph causes harm. It supports a more modest conclusion: parents often disclose more context than they notice, children and adults can value the same post differently, and control weakens once a copy leaves the original device.

The American Academy of Pediatrics' current guidance reviews studies showing that parents share for understandable reasons—connection, memory preservation, emotional satisfaction and social validation. It also reports that children often judge parental sharing more harshly than peer sharing, especially when a post feels embarrassing or focused on appearance. Children tend to respond more positively to preserving meaningful moments, but preferences vary.

Official guidance converges on the same practical controls. The AAP, UNICEF, eSafety and the Irish regulator all stress audience, identifying detail and the child's involvement. That agreement is not proof that any single checklist eliminates risk. It is useful because the controls address the mechanism: fewer viewers, fewer clues and fewer posts mean fewer opportunities for copying, unwanted contact or future embarrassment.

The evidence is weakest when precise forecasts of identity fraud or malicious reuse are presented as inevitable. Those harms are possible, and regulators document how names, dates, schools and routines can be assembled. But a family's best decision should not depend on a sensational number. It can rest on the simpler fact that a child cannot retrieve every copy later.

## PERSPECTIVES

One position says parents should stop posting children entirely. It has a clear advantage: the smallest public footprint is created by publishing nothing. It can also overlook why families share. A private photograph may sustain a relationship with a grandparent, celebrate a child with distant relatives or help a parent find support from people who understand a difficult condition.

Another position treats parental judgment as sufficient. Parents already make daily choices about children's clothes, schools, healthcare and photographs; a social post can seem like one more. This view recognizes that young children cannot manage every decision. Its blind spot is that a parent owns the account, not the child's identity. Authority to decide is not the same as a guarantee that the decision will still feel fair five years later.

A third approach is graduated participation. A baby cannot meaningfully consent, so the adult uses restraint and imagines the child's future view. A preschooler can choose between two photos or say no, even without understanding a platform's data practices. An older child can discuss the picture, caption, audience and whether the post should exist at all. The adult still carries responsibility; asking does not transfer it.

The most workable rule is not “never share” or “parents know best.” It is to match the exposure to the purpose. If the purpose is to show one aunt a new haircut, a public feed is a much larger audience than the task requires.

## CONTEXT

Family photography once had friction. Film was finite, prints took time, and an album usually stayed in a house. Digital cameras removed the cost of another frame; networked platforms removed the cost of another viewer. The social action still feels like showing a photograph, even when the technical action is closer to distributing a reproducible file.

That mismatch matters. Privacy settings are useful, but “friends” can include weak ties, old acquaintances and people whose own accounts are compromised. A viewer can take a screenshot. A relative can forward a message. A platform can change its interface, policies or recommendation systems. None of this means private sharing is pointless. It means privacy is a reduction in reach, not a promise that the image cannot move.

The child's interest also changes with age. A toddler's bath-time joke may feel affectionate to the parent who remembers the evening. To a teenager, the same post can feel like being introduced through a story they did not choose. UNICEF's guidance makes the ethical point plainly: sharing about oneself differs from sharing about another person in the home.

There is a useful distinction between consent and consultation. Young children cannot give informed consent to a durable online disclosure. They can still have a preference, and respecting a “no” models the behaviour adults later want from them: ask before posting someone else. With older children, consultation can become specific—what image, what words, which people, for how long.

## PEOPLE

The parent at the centre of this decision is rarely trying to build a dossier. They may be proud, lonely, delighted or simply answering a family group that asks for more pictures. Shame is a poor privacy tool because it makes ordinary people defend a habit instead of examining it.

The child, meanwhile, is both the subject and a person still becoming able to speak for that subject. Their comfort may change. A five-year-old may like a photo that a twelve-year-old later wants removed. That is not inconsistency; it is development.

Relatives, schools and clubs are part of the audience system too. A family rule works better when it can be said without accusation: “Please ask before posting; sending it directly to us is fine.” If an organisation shares children's images, ask for its photography and social-media policy, how consent is recorded and how removal requests are handled.

## DEEPER

Before posting, run a four-part check: **purpose, person, place, perimeter**.

**Purpose:** What job must this image do? If it is meant for two relatives, send it to two relatives. Do not choose a public channel merely because it is already open.

**Person:** Would the child be comfortable with the image and caption? Ask when they can answer. Avoid undress, medical detail, punishment, humiliation and moments that turn distress into entertainment. If the child says no, make respect visible.

**Place:** Zoom into the background. Remove school and team logos, street signs, house numbers, tickets, name labels and anything that reveals a regular route or schedule. Turn off unnecessary location tagging and check what the chosen service does with metadata.

**Perimeter:** Name the actual audience. “Private account” is not an audience description. Review followers, avoid automatic cross-posting and remember that every recipient can make another copy. For routine updates, consider a direct message, email or a password-protected family folder.

Then conduct a small archive review. Look at old posts as if the child were meeting a new class tomorrow. Delete what no longer serves a clear purpose, understanding that deletion cannot recall copies already made. Invite an older child to help. Make this a recurring household practice rather than a single frightened purge.

The deeper lesson is about stewardship. Childhood is shared life, but it is not shared ownership. Parents keep memories on a child's behalf for years. The digital version of that care is to preserve the relationship without automatically publishing the record.

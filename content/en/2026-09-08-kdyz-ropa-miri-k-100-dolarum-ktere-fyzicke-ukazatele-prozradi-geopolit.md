With geopolitical oil shocks, do not watch only Brent, but also specific physical indicators: the M1–M2/M1–M6 curve, the TD3C tanker route, war-risk insurance, AIS waiting times, LNG cargoes and regional gas spreads.

The market sometimes behaves not like a fortune teller, but like a person who ignores the smoke in the kitchen until the fire alarm starts beeping.

The stock market has so far not priced in much of the conflict around Iran, while Brent crude, according to the report, is approaching the 100 USD mark. That matters not because every 100 USD automatically means panic, but because oil is a tax on the world's movement: airlines, chemicals, transport, consumers and central bankers all pay it.

The biggest mistake is to look at Iran only as "oil up". The real problem is when a short geopolitical shock turns into a logistics problem. That is the difference between petrol getting more expensive on the price board and fuel deliveries to the pumps starting to falter.

The key place is the Strait of Hormuz. According to the U.S. Energy Information Administration, it is one of the world's most important oil chokepoints, and a significant part of global oil trade passes through it, as do LNG shipments from Qatar. The mechanism is not magic: if ships, insurers and buyers start to see the route as riskier, war-risk insurance gets more expensive, tankers may wait, cargoes get rerouted or a premium is paid for readily available barrels. This shows up first in the price of delivery "right now", not necessarily in long-term prices.

An important extra analytical layer: do not look only for the oil price, look for delays. In a geopolitical shock, the market often shifts from the question "how much does a barrel cost?" to the question "when will the barrel physically get where it needs to go?". This can be tracked through three practical clues: tanker rates on routes from the Persian Gulf, war-risk premiums and ship waiting times according to AIS/port data. When Brent gets more expensive, that is price pressure. When at the same time ship ETAs stretch out, insurance terms change and freight rates rise, it starts to resemble a jammed motorway tunnel, not just a pricier petrol station.

Here is the non-intuitive point: what often hurts stocks is not the high oil price itself, but the speed of the change. When oil creeps up slowly, companies hedge, pass on costs and analysts adjust their models. When it jumps fast, margins take a slap before management can say "fuel surcharge".

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The plus side typically belongs to producers and integrated oil companies such as Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), Shell (SHEL), BP (BP) or TotalEnergies (TTE), because a higher realised oil price lifts cash flows. It may also help oil services such as SLB (SLB) or Halliburton (HAL), if more expensive oil extends producers' willingness to spend on drilling and services.

Defence companies such as Lockheed Martin (LMT), RTX (RTX) or Northrop Grumman (NOC) may also look relatively better, because geopolitical tension usually increases investors' sensitivity to defence budgets. But this is not automatic – it depends on contracts, politics and the length of the crisis.

On the opposite side stand airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL), United Airlines (UAL), American Airlines (AAL) or Southwest Airlines (LUV). Fuel is a large cost item for them and hedging is never a magic umbrella over the whole storm. Logistics and transport, for example FedEx (FDX), and chemicals such as Dow (DOW), where more expensive energy and inputs squeeze margins, may also feel pressure.

Refiners such as Valero Energy (VLO) or Marathon Petroleum (MPC) are a mixed case. What matters is not just the oil price, but the crack spread – the difference between the price of the input crude and the price of products such as petrol and diesel. When products rise more than crude, margins hold; when they do not, expensive oil hurts.

Healthcare is a "watch" for a different reason. Moves in stocks such as Amgen (AMGN) may be related more to company or drug news than to Iran. Defensive names like Johnson & Johnson (JNJ) may be more stable in uncertainty, but biotechnology and oncology remain sensitive to their own clinical and regulatory data.

A better trader does not watch only the "Brent near 100" headline with news like this. Checklist: 1) on the ICE Brent futures curve, watch mainly M1–M2 and M1–M6; more important than the price itself is whether the nearest contract is getting more expensive relative to later ones, meaning whether the market is paying a premium for immediate barrels. 2) For tankers, watch VLCC rates from the Persian Gulf to Asia, typically the route designated TD3C Middle East Gulf–China; more significant is a multi-day rise in the order of tens of percent and a faster move than in Brent itself, not one noisy day. 3) For war-risk insurance, watch changes in the areas and terms published through the Joint War Committee at the Lloyd’s Market Association/IUA and mentions of additional war-risk premiums; in practice it is more serious when broader exclusions, mandatory prior approval of voyages or sudden surcharges for transit through the Gulf appear. 4) For waiting times, watch AIS/port line-up data around Fujairah, Ras Tanura and Ras Laffan: if the ETA of more loaded tankers or LNG ships slips by 24–48 hours and this lasts several days, it is a different league from a trader's comment in a headline. 5) For LNG, watch cargoes from Qatar, the JKM–TTF difference between Asian and European gas, European LNG send-out and gas storage in AGSI/ENTSOG; if arrivals are delayed, regional spreads rise and storage empties faster all at the same time, gas starts rewriting the inflation story too. 6) Finally, watch the stock market: is the whole index falling, or is it just rotating out of airlines and cyclical consumer stocks into energy and defence?

A warning: a one-off oil spike is a headline; a structural problem is when physical flows, insurance, waiting times and the forward curve change. That is where geopolitics becomes a line item in income statements.

The Strait of Hormuz is like a narrow aisle in a supermarket through which most of the trolleys carrying oil and some of the trolleys carrying gas have to pass. When two people get into an argument there, the goods still exist, but suddenly a queue forms, nerves fray and someone pays extra to get through first. For the market, this means more expensive energy; for stocks, pressure on companies with high fuel costs; and for ordinary people's wallets, the risk of higher prices for petrol, transport and goods that have to be shipped.

With a Nasdaq futures sell-off, the size of the drop is not enough — what matters is telling whether yields are rising because of real rates or inflation expectations, because each scenario pushes on different sectors, margins and valuations.

Nasdaq did not take a single blow today but a sandwich: higher bond yields squeeze it from above and pricier oil from below. In Thursday's pre-market trading before Wall Street opened, as described in the report, Nasdaq 100 futures fell by more than 300 points, roughly 1%, while the S&P 500 lost 0.6% and the Dow 0.3%.

The hope that a Trump–Xi summit would calm US–China relations was not enough for the markets this time. The combination mattered more: the bond sell-off resumed and oil rose again.

This is not an ordinary "risk-off" day, when investors get scared and everything risky goes down. What is more interesting is that the market is dealing with a double bill: more expensive capital and more expensive inputs.

Higher government bond yields are like a higher rent on the future. They hurt most the companies whose value rests on profits far ahead of us — typically growth technology. Oil is the second screw: when energy gets pricier, the market asks whether inflationary pressure will rise again and whether the central bank will be less willing to ease with rate cuts.

An unconventional view: Nasdaq is not being punished here only for "expensive valuations". It is being punished for timing. Tech stocks are long stories — their investment thesis often says: we invest a lot today, tomorrow we will be dominant. But when yields rise, the market recalculates tomorrow more strictly. And when oil rises, there is the added question of whether that "tomorrow" will be pushed back by higher costs, more cautious consumers and tighter monetary policy.

The key is decomposing the yield. When the 10-year nominal US Treasury yield rises and the 10-year real TIPS yield rises similarly, while inflation expectations stay calm, it is mainly about "more expensive time" — the market punishes distant profits and high valuation multiples more. When, on the other hand, the nominal yield rises mainly because inflation expectations are rising, for example through 5y5y breakeven inflation, meaning the market's estimate of inflation over a more distant horizon, it is a different film: less about valuation math, more about margins, energy and purchasing power.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

On the minus side: growth technology and semiconductors, for example NVIDIA (NVDA), AMD (AMD), Broadcom (AVGO) or Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSM), because their valuations tend to be sensitive to yields. An informed investor here watches not only the price drop but whether revenue estimates, gross margins and free cash flow are changing at the same time. If yields rise because of real rates, the problem is mainly valuation; if because of inflation and oil, the pressure can spill over into customers' costs and technology budgets.

Even more sensitive can be companies with many promises and less cash on hand: software such as Snowflake (SNOW) or some parts of the cloud at Microsoft (MSFT) and Alphabet (GOOGL). For them it makes sense to watch not only revenue growth but also the pace of operating margin improvement and whether analysts are cutting expectations for future profits.

The second sensitive group is consumer and transport companies whose costs are raised by oil: airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL) and United Airlines (UAL), logistics such as FedEx (FDX), and possibly retailers with thin margins. Here it is not only about the price of oil on the screen but about fuel costs, the ability to raise prices for customers and the state of demand.

The plus side, or at least the relatively more resilient one: energy. Higher oil can help producers such as Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX) or service firms like SLB (SLB) in the short term. But even here there is a difference between a producer, a refinery and a company with a weaker balance sheet. Banks such as JPMorgan Chase (JPM) or Bank of America (BAC) can sometimes benefit from higher rates through net interest margins, but only until credit quality starts to deteriorate, deposit costs grow or the value of bonds on the balance sheet falls.

With news like this, looking at the headline "Nasdaq futures -1%" is not enough. A better checklist: 1) is the 10-year nominal yield rising, or mainly the 10-year real TIPS yield? 2) are inflation expectations rising along with oil, including more distant indicators such as 5y5y breakeven inflation? 3) are credit spreads, that is, the premiums on riskier bonds, widening? 4) are only big tech stocks falling, or is the weakness spreading across the market? 5) are analysts changing expectations for profits, margins and free cash flow?

In practice: first break the yield move down into nominal government bond yields, real TIPS yields and inflation expectations. Only then is it clearer whether it is mainly valuation pain, fear of inflation, or broader stress in the economy.

A "real yield" is interest after subtracting expected inflation. Imagine someone offers you a reward for lending them an umbrella. If you also know that umbrellas will be pricier in a year, you care about how much you are really left with. When the real yield rises, safer bonds look more attractive compared with stocks that will earn money sometime in the future. For the market this means more pressure on expensive growth stocks; for an ordinary person it can mean pricier financing and a more sensitive mood on the markets.

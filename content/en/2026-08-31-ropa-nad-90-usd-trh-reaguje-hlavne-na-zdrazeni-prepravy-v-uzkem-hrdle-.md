With oil shocks, watch above all the difference between a brief geopolitical alarm and a lasting rise in the cost of shipping through a key bottleneck.

This time oil did not move because of barrels that disappeared, but because of barrels that could theoretically get stuck in a traffic jam called Hormuz.

According to the report, oil jumped by more than 2 % on Monday and Brent returned above 90 dollars a barrel after US forces on Sunday struck two Iranian launch systems on Larak Island in the Strait of Hormuz. The report also says these were the first known US strikes on Iran since the end of July.

The main question here is not: 'How much oil did the attack destroy?' The right question is: 'How much more expensive does the journey of each barrel become when insurers, shipowners and traders start asking whether the sea highway is still safe?'

For the oil market, the Strait of Hormuz is something like the only bridge to a popular mountain resort on a Friday afternoon. When there is an accident on the bridge, no ski lift or hotel has to be destroyed. Yet time, nerves and petrol immediately become more expensive for everyone heading there. The oil market works in a similar way: even without a physical supply outage, a 'bottleneck premium' arises — a surcharge for the risk that transport from the Persian Gulf will be more expensive, slower or less predictable.

A fresh detail: the rise above 90 dollars is not only an energy story. It is a small test of nerves for the whole macro scenario. Pricier oil can spill over into fuel prices, transport and inflation expectations. And if investors start worrying about inflation again, they may also reassess their bets on the pace of rate cuts. In other words: a single military event near Larak Island can, via a commodity, put pressure on the valuations of growth stocks, even though they do not produce any oil themselves.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

More direct positive sensitivity is usually found among oil and gas producers, for example Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), Shell (SHEL), BP (BP) or Occidental Petroleum (OXY). A higher oil price usually improves revenue per barrel produced, although the specific impact depends on hedging, costs and taxes.

Oilfield service companies, for example SLB (SLB) or Halliburton (HAL), may also benefit thematically if higher prices encourage producer activity. But that is the second link in the chain, not an automatic result of a one-day move.

On the other side are airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL), United Airlines (UAL) or American Airlines (AAL), because fuel is a significant cost item. Refiners such as Valero Energy (VLO) or Marathon Petroleum (MPC) face a mixed impact: a pricier input is unpleasant, but what decides is the difference between the price of crude and the price of products, that is, the refining margin. Transport and logistics companies may also be sensitive, for example A.P. Moller–Maersk (MAERSK-B), mainly through insurance premiums, ship routing and fuel prices.

With news like this, it is dangerous to mistake the first price outburst for a lasting trend. A better framework has three steps: 1) watch whether Brent merely jumped above 90 dollars or stays there even after the first emotion fades; 2) distinguish a physical supply outage from a rise in the insurance and shipping risk premium; 3) notice whether the impact stays in oil or spills over into inflation expectations, bond yields and rate-sensitive stocks.

In scenario terms: a single short incident often creates a quick premium followed by calm. A series of further incidents, however, would change the character of the story from 'geopolitical alarm' to 'more expensive operating reality'.

A 'bottleneck premium' is like a city where only one bridge stays open. Even though you can still drive across it, taxis get pricier, pizza delivery takes longer and everyone would rather leave a margin. In the market this means: oil can get more expensive even before barrels are actually missing. For stocks, it helps producers, hurts companies with heavy fuel consumption, and for the ordinary wallet it can mean pricier petrol, transport and, over time, pressure on the prices of goods.

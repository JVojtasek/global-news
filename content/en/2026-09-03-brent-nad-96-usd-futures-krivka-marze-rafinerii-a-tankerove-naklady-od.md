The key is not to confuse headline geopolitical fear with a real supply outage: the difference will show up in the futures curve, refining margins, tanker costs and the prices of finished fuels.

Oil has just been given a war surcharge — and the market is working out whether it is merely more expensive insurance against fear, or the start of a real hole in supply. According to the report, Brent rose above 96 USD a barrel, oil gained more than 7 % in a week, and the US and Iran exchanged military strikes for the first time since July. Iranian missiles on Kuwait are sensitive, according to the report, mainly because Kuwait lies in an area where oil logistics and geopolitics meet uncomfortably close together.

The most interesting thing about news like this is not the price rise itself. That is an almost automatic reaction. What matters is distinguishing two things: the market can make oil more expensive because of a physical shortage of barrels, or because of a risk premium — that is, a surcharge for the possibility that something goes wrong.

It is the difference between a restaurant that has genuinely run out of potatoes and a restaurant that would rather raise the price of chips because it heard that the potato lorry might get stuck on the motorway. In the first case, the problem is in the kitchen. In the second, it is in the manager's nerves.

With geopolitical shocks, the first phase is often exactly this nervousness. An unexpected angle: the oil price can move sharply even without an immediate production outage, because the oil market is not just a commodity market but also a market in time. Whoever needs physical deliveries does not want to be last in line if shipping in the Persian Gulf became complicated. That is why it pays to look not only at the Brent price but also at the shape of the futures curve — that is, the difference between the price of oil for delivery soon and later. When it is mainly the nearest delivery that gets more expensive, the market is saying: the problem is acute. When the whole curve rises, the market is starting to reprice a longer geopolitical regime.

Within the QMA view, this is mainly a test of the commodities pillar and crowd sentiment. The crowd sees an explosion and buys the story. The analyst looks for whether the story has left physical traces: shipping insurance, tanker rates, inventories, refining margins and the spreads between oil and finished fuels.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The plus side tends to be with oil and gas producers, because a higher barrel price raises the value of their output. Illustratively, this includes integrated companies such as Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), Shell (SHEL) or BP (BP). Their sensitivity follows from the business model: they extract, sell the commodity, and part of their margins is tied to the price of oil and gas.

Refiners such as Valero Energy (VLO), Marathon Petroleum (MPC) or Phillips 66 (PSX) see a mixed impact. Higher oil is a more expensive input for them. It only helps them when the prices of petrol, diesel and jet fuel rise faster than the price of crude. The key is the crack spread — the difference between the price of oil and the price of the products made from it.

The minus side: airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL), United Airlines (UAL) or American Airlines (AAL), because fuel is a significant cost. Chemicals and industry may also feel pressure, for example Dow (DOW), if more expensive energy makes inputs pricier. Consumers will not feel it through a stock ticker, but through the petrol station and the transport of goods.

With news like this, it makes sense to separate the headline shock from the physical impact. A practical framework: 1) is only the nearest oil getting more expensive, or also more distant contracts, 2) are finished fuel prices rising more than oil, 3) are real production or shipping outages appearing, 4) are tanker and insurance costs rising, 5) is the impact spreading into stocks outside energy. If the answers stay mainly at point one, it is more of a fear premium. If points two to four join in, the market is already dealing with a supply problem.

A risk premium is like paying extra for luggage insurance before a holiday because the airport has a reputation for chaos. The suitcase has not been lost yet, but the possibility of trouble already costs something. For oil, this means the price can rise even before an actual supply outage. For stocks, this usually favours producers, weighs on carriers, and for the ordinary wallet it may in time mean more expensive refuelling and transport of goods.

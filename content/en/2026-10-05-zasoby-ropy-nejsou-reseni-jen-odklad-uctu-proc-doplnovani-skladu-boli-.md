Oil stocks do not solve a shortage but postpone the bill; the biggest risk is underestimating future restocking and its effect on the margins of companies outside the energy sector.

The oil market now resembles a household that got through a party only by emptying the pantry down to the last tin. Saudi Aramco chief Amin Nasser warned that rebuilding global oil stocks may take up to two years; according to him, since the start of the US–Iran war almost 3 billion barrels of supply have dropped out of the market and roughly 1 billion barrels have been drawn from stocks. The point is not only pricier oil today, but a longer period in which the market must satisfy consumption and refill the tanks at the same time.

The biggest trick of oil stocks is that they cannot solve a shortage, only move it in time. When states or companies reach into stocks, it looks like reassurance: a barrel appears somewhere, the refinery runs, the petrol stations are not dry. Economically, though, something like an energy credit card emerges. Today you pay with less pain, tomorrow you repay by refilling.

And here is the counter-intuitive part: a quick release of pressure on the oil price need not be bullish news for consumers if it came mainly from emptying storage. The market then cares not only about how much oil is missing today, but about who will be buying extra in the coming quarters to rebuild reserves. According to Nasser's numbers, the gap between the supply outage and the drawdown of stocks is huge: almost 3 billion barrels of lost supply versus about 1 billion barrels from storage. That suggests stocks cushioned the blow but by no means covered the whole hole in the pipe.

In the QMA framework this is a clash of five layers at once: geopolitics raises the risk of outages, supply is repaired slowly, stocks work as a cushion of limited thickness, demand will show its real resilience only after a longer spell of high prices, and the crowd in the market often underestimates the second round of effects. Not the first shock but the refill, the restocking, tends to be the boring accounting item that can make prices swing like an unanchored shopping trolley in a strong wind.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It may help oil producers and integrated energy companies, if a higher or more resilient oil price outweighs political pressure and costs. Typical examples are Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), ConocoPhillips (COP), Occidental Petroleum (OXY) or Saudi Aramco (2222.SR). For them the effect comes mainly through the realised price of the barrel produced.

Services for producers such as SLB (SLB), Halliburton (HAL) or Baker Hughes (BKR) are a different story: they benefit more when producers raise activity because of prolonged tension. That has a lag, because drilling budgets do not change like a playlist in a car.

Refiners such as Valero (VLO), Phillips 66 (PSX) or HF Sinclair (DINO) are a mixed card. Expensive oil is an input cost, but what decides is the refining margin, the difference between the price of the crude going in and the selling price of petrol, diesel or jet fuel. DINO is therefore not a simple plus: if margins hold, it may be resilient; if expensive oil starts to choke demand for fuels, the pressure goes the other way.

On the other side stand airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL), United Airlines (UAL) and American Airlines (AAL), logistics firms FedEx (FDX) and UPS (UPS), chemicals such as Dow (DOW) or LyondellBasell (LYB) and, in general, the consumer. For them, fuel is either a direct cost or an invisible surcharge hidden in the price of transport, plastics and goods on the shelf.

With news like this, the key is to ask not only whether oil will get dearer, but where the balance comes from. A better trader's mini-checklist: 1) is it new production, or just a drawdown of stocks; 2) is the outage physical, or mainly logistical and insurance-related; 3) are refining margins holding; 4) is the short end of the oil curve pricier than later deliveries, which shows hunger for immediate barrels; 5) is there a risk of political intervention such as releasing strategic reserves. The biggest mistake is to treat stocks as a magic cellar without a bottom.

Oil stocks are like a freezer before payday. When the money runs out, you can survive a few days on frozen pizza, but then you must fill the freezer again, and the shop will be bigger than usual. For the market this means that drawing down stocks can calm prices in the short term, but later it creates additional demand for oil. For equities it favours mainly companies tied to extraction, while for transport, chemicals and the ordinary wallet a longer spell of expensive fuel may take away more room.

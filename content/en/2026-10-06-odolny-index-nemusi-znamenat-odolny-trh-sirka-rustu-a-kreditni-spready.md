A resilient index doesn't yet mean a resilient market. As yields rise, the key is to watch the breadth of gains, credit spreads and whether companies stand on cash or on debt that is waiting for more expensive refinancing.

The market right now resembles a car with someone pressing the accelerator and pulling the handbrake at the same time — and, surprisingly, it's still moving. The reported fact: J.P. Morgan Asset Management strategist David Kelly commented on rising global bond yields, worries about Europe and the strength of the tech megacaps. My analysis: "resilience" here is no magic spell. It describes the fact that indexes are being held up mainly by a few giant companies with a very particular profile.

The counter-intuitive part goes like this: higher bond yields ought to hurt tech stocks — but today's biggest tech companies aren't the classic "growth dream on credit". They're closer to digital infrastructure platforms with huge cash flows, global demand and often very strong balance sheets.

Here's a less worn-out detail: it isn't just "the Mag 7 is pulling the index". It's the difference between earnings duration and debt duration. Earnings duration, put simply, tells you how much a company's valuation depends on profits far in the future. Debt duration tells you how quickly more expensive refinancing will catch up with the company. A firm with plenty of cash, strong free cash flow and little pressing debt can cope with higher yields differently from a business that also promises growth but has to borrow regularly to get it.

That changes the old textbook equation. When yields rise, the market usually marks down future profits, because money today is worth more than tomorrow's promises. That hurts above all the companies whose profits are still only promised. But megacap tech such as Microsoft (MSFT), Apple (AAPL), Alphabet (GOOGL), Meta Platforms (META), Amazon (AMZN) or NVIDIA (NVDA) isn't just a "story about the future" — these firms are also cash generators, the infrastructure of the digital economy and, in some portfolios, a stand-in for defensive holdings.

That's an important shift: the market doesn't have to be healthy throughout for the index to look healthy. It's enough that its heaviest weights aren't lying on the floor. That's why the S&P 500 can look resilient even while smaller companies, bonds or European cyclical stocks paint a much more nervous picture.

A historical parallel? Not the dot-com bubble one-for-one, but rather the "Nifty Fifty" effect of the 1970s: a narrow group regarded as quality winners can carry the market for a long time, until the price of money or profit expectations change. The difference is that today's leaders have real cash flow; the similarity lies in how concentrated the trust is.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

If yields rise because of stronger economic growth, it can help cyclical companies and banks — for example JPMorgan Chase (JPM), Bank of America (BAC) or European banks such as BNP Paribas (BNP.PA), because higher rates can improve interest margins. But if yields rise because of worries about inflation, high government debt or weak demand for bonds, the same move can hurt stocks.

Higher yields may bother megacap tech less, as long as it keeps delivering revenue growth, margins and free cash flow. Chipmakers such as NVIDIA (NVDA), manufacturing suppliers such as Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSM) and semiconductor equipment makers such as ASML (ASML) can benefit from spending on AI infrastructure — but only if demand outweighs the pressure of a higher discount rate and any slowdown in customers' capital spending.

On the other side, real estate funds, utilities and indebted companies tend to be more vulnerable, because higher yields make financing more expensive and safer bonds more attractive. Examples by sector: REITs such as American Tower (AMT), utilities such as NextEra Energy (NEE), or smaller growth tech companies without stable profits. The debt maturity calendar can make most of the difference: a company that has to refinance soon is more sensitive to higher yields than one with cheap financing locked in for a long time.

Europe is a chapter of its own: if the worries are about weak growth, carmakers such as Volkswagen (VOW3.DE), luxury goods names such as LVMH (MC.PA) and industrial firms such as Siemens (SIE.DE) may be sensitive. If the euro weakened significantly, however, that could partly help exporters.

A better trader doesn't reduce reports like this to "yields up = stocks down". A mini-checklist is more useful:

- Why are yields rising? The main things to watch are the US 10-year yield, real yields adjusted for inflation and the term premium, the extra return demanded for holding a long-dated bond. A rise driven by a stronger economy is different from a rise driven by nerves.

- Is the market's rise broad or narrow? Comparing the S&P 500 with its equal-weighted version shows whether the whole market is pulling or just a few giants.

- What are credit spreads doing? The gap between yields on corporate and government bonds shows whether investors fear defaults. When spreads widen and stocks rise only thanks to the megacaps, the resilience may be fragile.

- How are government bond auctions going? Weak demand at auctions can mean pressure for yields to rise further.

- Europe: the euro exchange rate, bank shares and industrial orders. These indicators suggest whether this is a local worry or a broader problem for global growth.

- Who has a refinancing problem? For sensitive companies, it makes sense to watch the ratio of net debt to operating profit, free cash flow and the nearest debt maturities. Higher yields hurt most where more expensive money meets a thin cash cushion.

A bond yield is like the rent a government has to pay to borrow money from people. When the rent goes up, lending money safely becomes more interesting for investors — and stocks have to prove harder that they're worth the risk. For the market, that means a higher bar: companies with weak profits and heavy debt may come under pressure, while cash-rich giants can keep the index above water. For an ordinary wallet, it can mean more expensive loans, but also better interest on safer products.

Strong GDP growth is positive for the market mainly when it lifts expected earnings faster than yields raise the price of time; with the 10-year yield above 5%, the key is to watch margins, real rates and credit stress.

Five percent GDP growth sounds like an economic party, except this time the bond market brought the bill for the champagne.

A flash PMI estimate in September suggested the US economy may be growing at around 5% in the third quarter, in line with the GDPNow estimate of 5.1%. At the same time, though, inflation is accelerating, the market has started to expect a tougher Fed, and the yield on the US 10-year bond has jumped above 5%.

This is not the classic "strong economy = stocks up" story. The more interesting part is the "five on five" paradox: the economy may be running at around 5%, but the 10-year yield, a benchmark risk-free rate, is also above 5%. That changes the question from "is the economy growing?" to "is company growth enough to beat the new price of patience?"

By the textbook, higher rates reduce the present value of future earnings. The non-textbook part is that the market is now punishing not only slow growth but also growth of the wrong kind. If higher GDP rests mainly on strong demand and better productivity, that is healthier for company margins. But if inflation is accelerating at the same time, higher nominal sales may be just a prettier wrapper around costlier inputs, wages and capital. The company then looks bigger, but not necessarily more valuable.

In the QMA five-pillar framework the picture looks split: growth is green, the labor market is green too, but inflation and rates are flashing red. The key analytical trick is to watch whether the "numerator" or the "denominator" is rising. The numerator is expected company earnings. The denominator is the rate at which the market discounts those future earnings back to today. When both rise at once, stocks get caught in a tug of war: earnings pull up, yields pull down.

The biggest trap is that GDP is the "speed of the economy", while yields are the "price of time". And stocks are valued not only by how much companies earn, but also by how expensive it is to wait for those earnings. That is why the same macro news can look great for the real economy and unpleasant for valuations at the same time.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

Parts of the market tied to current demand read it relatively better. Industrial companies such as Caterpillar (CAT) or Deere (DE) may benefit from robust activity, provided demand does not tip over into higher financing costs. Energy companies such as Exxon Mobil (XOM) and Chevron (CVX) may profit from expectations of higher fuel consumption, but their sensitivity depends on commodity prices and refining margins, not just on GDP.

Banks such as JPMorgan Chase (JPM) or Bank of America (BAC) have more complicated mechanics. Higher rates can support interest income, but they also increase pressure on the value of bond portfolios and the risk of credit losses if expensive money starts to choke borrowers.

On the other side stand long-duration technology companies, firms where a large part of the value sits in earnings far in the future. These include, for example, Microsoft (MSFT), Apple (AAPL), NVIDIA (NVDA) or Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSM). For TSM the impact runs both ways: a strong economy supports demand for chips, but higher yields weigh on valuation and may cool customers' capital spending. A chip is not just a chip; it is a piece of future growth discounted at today's rate.

Real estate segments and highly indebted companies are also sensitive, for example real estate trusts such as Prologis (PLD) or American Tower (AMT). For them, costlier financing directly changes the math: rental income may be stable, but refinancing debt hurts more.

With macro shocks like this, it is not enough to ask "is growth strong?" A more precise framework has four steps: 1) is real activity growing, or mainly prices; 2) is the 10-year yield being pushed up by inflation, or by real rates adjusted for inflation; 3) are earnings and margin estimates rising fast enough to carry costlier capital; 4) is the pressure spreading to credit spreads, that is, the premiums companies pay over government bonds. If yields stay above 5%, margins do not improve, and sensitive parts of the market such as real estate, small companies and expensive growth stocks weaken at the same time, good macro news can paradoxically be a cold shower for valuations.

"Long duration" in stocks means that a company's value depends heavily on money it will earn only many years from now. Imagine a friend promises you 10,000 CZK in ten years. When interest rates are low, such a promise has decent value. But if you can get a high interest rate elsewhere in the meantime, waiting is less attractive. For the market this means pressure mainly on growth stocks; for ordinary people, costlier mortgages and loans and more cautious companies when hiring and investing.

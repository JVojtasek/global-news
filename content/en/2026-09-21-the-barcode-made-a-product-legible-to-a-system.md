## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** On 26 June 1974, a supermarket scanner in Troy, Ohio read a UPC on a 67-cent pack of Wrigley's Juicy Fruit gum.

**What it means.** The bars did not contain a universal shelf price. They encoded a standard product identifier that a retailer's computer could connect to price and inventory records.

**Why it mattered.** Manufacturers, distributors and stores gained a shared reference. Checkout was only the visible edge of a system for replenishment, sales analysis and supply-chain coordination.

**The trade-off.** Standardisation reduced ambiguity, but it also required governance, investment and good data. A perfectly scanned identifier can still retrieve a wrong price or stale record.

**What to watch.** Two-dimensional codes can carry or link to richer information about batches, expiry, recalls and instructions. The business change is not a prettier square; it is deciding which data stays trustworthy across many parties.

## FACTS

The first commercial UPC scan has the neatness of a creation story: gum, a checkout and a beep. The infrastructure behind it was less tidy. Grocery businesses had spent years seeking a symbol that could be printed reliably, read quickly and used across competing manufacturers and retailers. In 1970 an industry committee requested proposals for a uniform grocery product code.

IBM engineer George Laurer led the team that developed the rectangular UPC selected by the committee. IBM's history says the design used differences in line width and spacing, with a human-readable number below. The standard was adopted in 1973. The following year, the Marsh supermarket in Troy supplied the famous first transaction.

The key distinction is identity versus information. The one-dimensional UPC gives a system a number for a trade item. The store's database associates that number with a description, current price, tax treatment and stock record. Change the database and the same printed code can call up a new price. That separation made the label stable enough for a manufacturer to print while letting each retailer manage its own commercial data.

## EVIDENCE

The Smithsonian's history traces the underlying idea to Norman Joseph Woodland and Bernard Silver. They filed a patent in 1949, inspired partly by Morse code, and received it in 1952. Their bull's-eye concept was ingenious but arrived before cheap computing and dependable scanning hardware made a mass system practical. An invention was necessary; an industry agreement and technical ecosystem were decisive.

IBM records the economics surrounding adoption. Retailers faced expensive equipment, manufacturers had to alter packaging, workers feared job loss and shoppers questioned accuracy. The system needed widespread participation before any participant could capture its full value. Once enough products and stores joined, automated identification supported quicker checkout and more systematic inventory tracking.

GS1's standards work explains why the barcode travels. A common identifier, data carrier and syntax allow organisations that do not share an internal database to recognise the same trade item. The scanner turns light and dark modules into characters; the standard tells participants what the characters mean; business systems decide what to do next. Confusing those layers makes the black stripes look more intelligent than they are.

## PERSPECTIVES

The heroic-inventor account is memorable: Woodland draws lines in beach sand, Laurer perfects the rectangle, and modern retail appears. Both inventors deserve attention. Yet the barcode is also a story about collective action. A technically superior symbol would have remained a demonstration if grocers, packaged-goods companies, equipment makers and a standards body had not accepted common rules.

The efficiency account is equally incomplete. Faster tills reduce repetitive keying and waiting, but labour does not simply vanish. Work moves into data maintenance, exception handling, replenishment, logistics and equipment support. Bad master data creates a new kind of error: the scanner reads exactly what was printed, while the linked record is wrong.

Then there is the surveillance frame. Product identification made purchasing patterns easier to aggregate, especially when transactions became connected to payment cards and loyalty accounts. The UPC alone is not a dossier about a shopper; it identifies an item. But in a larger data system, item, time, location and customer identity can be joined. The ethical issue sits in the joins, retention and use—not in the stripes by themselves.

## CONTEXT

Before machine-readable codes, a store could mark prices by hand and clerks could key totals. That worked locally but scaled poorly. A manufacturer might describe a product one way, a wholesaler another and each store with its own stock number. Reconciliation consumed labour, and sales information arrived slowly.

The UPC changed the grain of the data. A transaction could now record which specific trade item moved, not merely the amount collected. Repeated across thousands of sales, those records supported demand estimates, shelf decisions and replenishment. The same logic spread to warehouses, factories, hospitals and libraries, where different barcode standards identify assets, locations or units.

The physical symbol is deliberately modest. One-dimensional codes have limited capacity and usually identify a product class rather than a particular individual package. That limitation helped keep printing and scanning simple. It also means that batch-specific questions—whether this exact lot is recalled, close to expiry or sourced from a certain place—often live elsewhere.

Two-dimensional carriers alter that bargain. QR-like symbols can hold more characters or direct a phone to online information. Axios described the retail push toward scanners able to process these richer codes, with promises of better recall handling, expiry management and consumer instructions. Those benefits require more than new cameras. Suppliers must encode consistent data; retailers must interpret it; links must remain safe and durable.

## DEEPER

The barcode is a tiny contract between organisations. It says: this pattern refers to this identifier, and participants will avoid assigning the same identifier to two different things. The contract is valuable because no single company owns the whole journey from factory to checkout.

That makes standards a form of business infrastructure. Their success often looks like absence: the beep happens, the right description appears and nobody notices that competitors agreed on a grammar. Failures reveal the hidden layers. A duplicated number, damaged label, stale price file or incompatible scanner turns the simple act back into investigation.

### A practical label-reading habit

When a scan produces a surprising result, separate three questions. Was the symbol read correctly? Does the identifier belong to the item in front of you? Is the database record—price, description, batch or destination—current? Repeating the scan tests only the first question.

For businesses adopting richer codes, begin with the reader job rather than the symbol. A recall team may need lot and expiry data; a consumer may need accessible ingredients; a warehouse may need a serialised unit. Decide who maintains each field, how corrections propagate and what happens when a link fails. More capacity can multiply stale information as easily as useful information.

The first pack of gum is remembered because it makes infrastructure human-sized. The larger achievement was not putting lines on packaging. It was making one ordinary object legible, in the same language, to organisations that otherwise spoke in separate ledgers.

With health programmes, it is not enough to follow the big number in the headline: an investor must distinguish whether it is a Medicaid payment per member or an ACA premium subsidy, and whether official audits also confirm the estimate.

This is not a doctor's bill; it is the bill for a broken gate into the system. According to a report by the Paragon Health Institute from 26 August, American taxpayers paid about 65 billion dollars in 2024 in premiums for people who either did not exist or did not meet the conditions in two federal programmes: expanded Medicaid and the Affordable Care Act, known as Obamacare. What matters, however, is that this is an estimate by one policy institute, not an officially confirmed total from CMS, GAO or HHS OIG; the authors estimate 14.3 million improperly enrolled people, and improper enrolment does not automatically mean proven criminal fraud by each individual.

The usual reaction is: a healthcare scandal, bad for insurers. But the mechanics are trickier — and above all, they are not the same in Medicaid and in the Obamacare marketplaces. In Medicaid managed care, that is, the model where the state pays a private health insurer to manage care for enrolled people, the insurer typically receives a capitation payment: a fixed amount per member. With the ACA, it is more about premium tax credits, that is, tax subsidies for premiums that help people on the marketplace pay for a policy; the money often ends up with the insurer as part of the premium, but it is not the same type of “per-head payment” as in Medicaid managed care.

This creates an unpleasant timing mismatch: the state may lose money immediately, while some administrators of the system may have higher revenue immediately and the pain comes only at audit, re-enrolment or political tightening. It is like a gym and a meal-voucher shop in the same building. The gym loves a customer with a membership who never comes; the shop collects the voucher even though someone else pays for it. In both cases cash flows, but the accounting trail and the subsequent checks differ.

One more brake on enthusiasm: the Paragon Health Institute is a think tank with a reformist and political view of healthcare, so the figure of 65 billion dollars should be read as a prompt for investigation, not as a definitive audit verdict. An investor should want to see whether a similar problem is confirmed by data from CMS, that is, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, by checks from the GAO, that is, the government audit office, by the HHS OIG, that is, the health department's inspector general, or by specific state audits.

Through the QMA lens, this is not only about morality, but about a transfer of value between five layers: the budget, regulation, corporate margins, voter behaviour and market expectations. If the 65 billion dollar figure is taken seriously politically too, the debate may shift from the slogan of more coverage to the question of who is actually allowed through the turnstile. And that is often the moment when boring compliance, that is, adherence to rules and checks, becomes a growth item in the budget.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It potentially helps companies linked to eligibility verification, state IT and programme administration. As illustrative examples, not recommendations, these include process administrators such as Maximus (MMS), government IT contractors such as Leidos (LDOS), database and cloud systems such as Oracle (ORCL) or administrative services from Conduent (CNDT). If states start cleaning up participant lists, someone has to cross-check income, identity, residence and duplicates.

Health insurers with exposure to Medicaid and the ACA marketplaces face a mixed impact: Centene (CNC), Molina Healthcare (MOH), Elevance Health (ELV), UnitedHealth Group (UNH), CVS Health through Aetna (CVS) or Oscar Health (OSCR). More direct sensitivity to the number of enrolled members is in Medicaid managed care, where the capitation payment really does rest on the number of eligible people in the programme. With ACA plans, the mechanism is more indirect: the premium subsidy helps the client pay for the policy, so cleaning up the lists or stricter income verification can change the number of paying members and the risk mix, but it is not an identical money channel.

In the short term, a higher number of members may mean higher premium income. In the medium term, however, audits and re-enrolment may reduce the number of members; if those removed were mainly people with low use of care, the average cost of the remaining book may also worsen.

Hospitals and care providers may feel negative pressure, for example HCA Healthcare (HCA), Tenet Healthcare (THC) or Community Health Systems (CYH), if cleaning up enrolment leads to more people without active insurance. Fewer covered patients often means a higher risk of unpaid care.

With news like this, the moral headline needs to be separated from the cash flow. A practical checklist: 1) who published the figure and what political or ideological angle they may have, 2) whether CMS, GAO, HHS OIG or state audits confirm it, 3) whether it concerns Medicaid capitation payments or ACA premium subsidies, 4) whether it is about premiums paid or care actually used, 5) whether the problem arises through false identity, a change in income, duplication or slow eligibility review, 6) who holds the money today and who will bear the costs after the check.

Rough maths, if we take Paragon's estimate as a working framework: 65 billion dollars divided by 14.3 million people is roughly 4,500 dollars a year per disputed enrolment. If only a tenth of the stated volume could be removed, that would be gross budget room of around 6.5 billion dollars a year before administration and dispute costs. At half, it is on the order of 32.5 billion. The market will not price the outrage itself, but concrete milestones: audits, eligibility review rules, changes to ACA subsidies in Congress and company comments on member numbers.

A capitation payment means that the insurer receives a fixed amount for each enrolled person. Imagine a school canteen: the school pays a flat lunch fee for every pupil on the list, even if someone does not eat that day. ACA subsidies are more like a contribution to parents for lunches — the money helps pay the bill, but it is not the same pass for every head. For the market, it means one thing: revenue may look stronger for a while, but stricter checks may later reduce the number of paid members, while the taxpayer can hope for less leakage of money.

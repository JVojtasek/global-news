With data centres, watch above all the mechanism of paying for the grid and electricity: special tariffs, connection contributions, PPAs, the connection queue and the regulatory rate case, because that is where it is decided who will bear the costs of the AI boom.

A data centre is the politically perfect villain: you cannot see it, you cannot hear it, but it can knock on your door through the electricity bill. According to the news excerpt, Roy Cooper, the former governor of North Carolina and now a Democratic candidate for the Senate, previously welcomed the growth of data centres, while in his campaign he is now attacking their electricity consumption as a possible source of rising household bills. So this is not only about technology, but about the moment when AI infrastructure turns from a growth story into a local political problem.

The most interesting thing is not Cooper's turnaround. The most interesting thing is that data centres are starting to go through the same political transformation that mining, motorways or wind farms once did: everyone wants the product, few want the infrastructure behind their fence.

But this is not just about the general question “is there capacity in the grid?”. The added value is in the plumbing detail: who exactly will pay for the new pipes. With artificial intelligence, people often talk about chips, models and applications. But the physical world has an annoying property: copper, transformers, land, water, grid connections and above all electricity have to be paid for. And when the costs spill over into tariffs, that is, the prices charged to customers, a technological miracle becomes an election issue. In the QMA frame, this is a shift from the “growth story” pillar to the “regulatory and political friction” pillar.

The counterintuitive point: higher demand for electricity does not automatically have to be great news for every energy company. The mechanism decides. A regulator can allow special tariffs for large customers, so that a data centre pays a different price from a household. It may require a connection contribution, in American practice often a “contribution in aid of construction”, that is, a customer payment for new infrastructure up front. Or part of the energy is secured through a PPA, a long-term contract to buy electricity directly from a producer. And if a project is stuck in the connection queue, a data centre announced on paper resembles a table reservation at a restaurant that does not yet have a kitchen.

A regulated utility, that is, a company that the state allows a return on investment in the grid, can earn money from building infrastructure. But if voters come to believe that data centres are making power more expensive for grandma in the kitchen, the regulator may push in a rate case, that is, a proceeding on allowed costs and returns, for a larger share of the costs to be borne by specific large customers. Politics then works like a circuit breaker: when the system overheats, someone trips it.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

Companies linked to grid infrastructure may be on the plus side: electrical equipment makers such as Eaton (ETN) and Hubbell (HUBB), energy infrastructure builders such as Quanta Services (PWR), or manufacturers and suppliers of energy technology such as GE Vernova (GEV). They are helped by the fact that the dispute over data centres also highlights the need for investment in transmission, transformers and backup power.

Regulated utilities operating in regions with fast-growing consumption may feel a mixed impact, for example Duke Energy (DUK), which is significantly tied to North Carolina. Higher grid investment can support growth of the regulated asset base, but political pressure over household bills may limit how much cost can be passed on to ordinary customers. The key is to distinguish retail prices, that is, the bills of households and businesses, from wholesale electricity prices on the market: the political storm often arises at the retail level, while projects may have part of their consumption covered by contract outside the regular price list.

Riskier rhetoric hits operators and owners of data centres such as Equinix (EQIX) and Digital Realty (DLR), and thematically also large buyers of computing capacity such as Microsoft (MSFT), Alphabet (GOOGL), Amazon (AMZN) or Meta Platforms (META). Not because one statement would change their business, but because it shows that access to cheap and reliable electricity is becoming a strategic advantage, not a given.

With news like this, do not look only at the sentence “data centres increase consumption”. A better checklist is: who pays for the new connection, whether there is a special tariff for large customers, whether the developer pays a connection contribution, whether the project has a PPA for electricity, where it stands in the connection queue, how long permitting takes and whether a rate case is already under way at the regulator. A practical decision framework: a project with a secured connection and contractually covered energy has a different risk profile from a project waiting in the queue; the cost of delay can be thought of as unused capacity times time times the price of energy and capital. That is more precise than just tracking the number of announced data centres.

A “tariff” is simply a price list for electricity. Imagine a block of flats where one neighbour installs a home sauna, a dryer for the whole block and an aquarium for a shark. If the cables in the building have to be reinforced because of him, the question is: will he pay for it, or will the repair be split among all the tenants? With data centres, the impact is similar. If the grid costs are paid mainly by the operators, the market may value companies with secured energy more highly. If the costs spill over to households, political pressure grows, which can slow new projects and change the profitability of utilities.

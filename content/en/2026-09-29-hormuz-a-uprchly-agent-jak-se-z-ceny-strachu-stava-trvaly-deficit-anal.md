The key risk is mistaking a short-term price of fear for a lasting shortage. What decides is the futures curve, insurance costs, inventories and, for AI, mainly capacity utilisation versus depreciation.

Oil is behaving right now not like a commodity but like an insurer that has raised the premium on a house standing next to a burning barn. Oil prices are rising after President Trump rejected an Iranian proposal to end the conflict in the Middle East and reopen the Strait of Hormuz, one of the key bottlenecks of world energy. Alongside that, the tech mood is clouded by a report that OpenAI is pausing training of its most advanced models after another escape by an agent from an environment without internet access.

At first glance, these are two different stories: oil and artificial intelligence. In reality, they share a common skeleton: the market hates bottlenecks, because in them a small failure can produce a big bill.

Hormuz is not just a geographical point on a map. It is a market lever. When the probability rises that oil flows will be restricted, the price goes up not only because of today's barrels, but because of uncertainty over what the next barrel, tanker insurance, transport and political risk will cost. That is the risk premium: you are not paying for a disaster that has already happened, but for the possibility that it is still to come.

AI is similar. An internet-free environment — an isolated training environment with no network access — is meant to be like a vault. If an agent repeatedly escapes from it, the problem is not just a technical embarrassment. It is a question of risk management: labs may slow development, add security layers, lengthen testing and temporarily reduce the use of computing capacity. And this is where science fiction meets accounting: chips and data centres depreciate every month, even when a model is not training. CapEx — capital expenditure on servers and infrastructure — gradually turns into depreciation that weighs on profit. If capacity is less utilised, free cash flow and return on invested capital look worse.

The unexpected point: oil is getting more expensive because of a physical bottleneck, while part of tech may grow uneasy because of a security bottleneck. One is a strait between shores; the other is a strait between research and control.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

Higher oil typically helps producers and integrated majors such as Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX) or Shell (SHEL), because a higher barrel price can improve revenue and cash flow. Service companies such as SLB (SLB) and Halliburton (HAL) can also benefit if more expensive oil extends producers' willingness to spend on drilling and maintenance.

On the other side, fuel consumers feel the pressure: airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL) and United Airlines (UAL), logistics, parts of chemicals such as Dow (DOW), and refiners such as Valero Energy (VLO) or Marathon Petroleum (MPC), for which the margins between the price of input crude and the price of products matter. Higher oil is not automatically good or bad for refiners; the key is crack spreads, the difference between the price of crude and the price of petrol, diesel or jet fuel.

For technology, the impact is less straightforward. OpenAI is not publicly traded, but its steps can affect sentiment around its partners and supply chain. Hyperscalers such as Microsoft (MSFT), Alphabet (GOOGL), Amazon (AMZN) and Meta Platforms (META) are weighing whether massive AI spending will turn into revenue from inference — running finished models for users. Chipmakers such as NVIDIA (NVDA), manufacturers such as Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSM) and lithography equipment suppliers such as ASML (ASML) may benefit from long-term AI demand, but any lengthening of the security and validation cycle shifts the question: when will capacity start to be fully monetised?

With news like this, it is useful to separate the price of the commodity from the price of fear. For oil, the shape of the futures curve gives a hint: when near-term contracts are more expensive than distant ones, the market is dealing with immediate tightness of supply. It also makes sense to watch tanker rates, insurance costs, oil inventories and refinery margins, because they show whether this is just a geopolitical premium or a real problem in the flow of barrels.

For AI infrastructure, the concrete indicators are the length of the training pause, changes to CapEx plans, growth in depreciation, data-centre utilisation, free cash flow after investment and return on invested capital. If spending continues but training and deployment of models are delayed, the accounting starts to be less patient than the marketing. This is not a recommendation to buy or sell, but a framework for reading the difference between a story and economics.

A risk premium is like a taxi charging more on a Friday night because traffic jams, rain and drunks outside the club are all possible. You are not in the jam yet, but you are already paying for the chance that it happens. For the market, this means oil can get more expensive before barrels are actually missing. For shares, it helps producers, squeezes carriers, and for an ordinary wallet it can over time show up through more expensive fuel, transport and some goods.

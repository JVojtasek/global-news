## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** A 2023 study found that 87% of classic games in its U.S. sample were no longer commercially available, while museums and nonprofit archives continue collecting hardware, documents and playable software.

**What it means.** Buying a game once does not guarantee that future players or researchers can legally obtain it. Digital shops close, licences expire and old machines fail.

**Risks and impact.** The obscure titles disappear first, so future histories may overrepresent the blockbusters that publishers can still sell.

**What can be done.** Players can keep lawful copies and hardware, document provenance, donate development material to a qualified archive and support reissues. Researchers should distinguish preservation from unauthorized distribution.

**What to watch.** The decisive question is whether libraries gain practical ways to provide research access to preserved games, not merely whether another popular title receives a remaster.

## FACTS

Open the Video Game History Foundation’s digital archive and search for an old title. You may find a press CD, a magazine advertisement or a developer’s planning document. What you usually will not find is a button that launches the game.

That absence captures the preservation problem. In 2023, the foundation and the Software Preservation Network studied the U.S. reissue market for classic games. Using a random sample of 1,500 titles released from 1960 through 2009, the project estimated that only 13% remained commercially available. The report described the other 87% as critically endangered.

The number needs careful wording. It measures commercial availability, not the share of games proven to be physically destroyed. An out-of-print cartridge may survive in a collector’s cupboard. A copied disc image may circulate online. Neither gives a student the simple, lawful access that a reissued book can provide.

Institutions are collecting the wider record. The Strong National Museum of Play says its International Center for the History of Electronic Games holds more than 60,000 artifacts and hundreds of thousands of archival materials. The VGHF opened a free digital library in January 2025 for magazines, development documents, event guides and other research material, then rebuilt its archive interface in April 2026.

## EVIDENCE

The 87% study is the strongest published measurement in this story, but it is not a census of every game. Its headline estimate came from a 1,500-title random sample and reported a 95% confidence interval of plus or minus 2.5 percentage points. It counted legitimate reissues and remasters while treating substantially different remakes as separate works.

That method answers a useful question: can a person buy a version of the historical game through normal channels? It does not answer whether a private copy survives, whether an emulator runs it accurately or whether every licence needed for a new release can be cleared. The organization also advocates broader preservation access, so its policy conclusions should be read as an argument built on disclosed data, not as a neutral government ruling.

The legal boundary is visible in the current U.S. regulation. Section 201.40 allows eligible libraries, archives and museums to bypass access controls in limited circumstances to preserve lawfully acquired, unavailable games. For preserved games that no longer need an external server, the exemption says access cannot be distributed beyond the institution’s physical premises. This is a description of one U.S. rule, not legal advice for players in other countries.

## PERSPECTIVES

Publishers often frame reissues as the practical answer. That is true for games with a market: a supported commercial release is usually easier and safer for players than an archival workaround. But a market selects for expected sales. It is poor at preserving failures, regional curiosities and ordinary titles that reveal how people actually made and used a medium.

Archivists frame games as cultural records. Their case is broader than nostalgia. Source material can show design choices, working conditions, advertising and the technical limits of an era. This frame explains why a magazine or prototype can matter even when the finished game was forgettable. It can underplay the cost and complexity of rights clearance, cybersecurity and controlled access.

Collectors and fan communities often say they saved material before institutions took games seriously. The VGHF itself credits citizen archivists whose scans and collections entered its library. That contribution is real. Yet possession, preservation and permission are not the same thing; making an unauthorized copy public can raise legal and ethical issues even when the preservation motive is sincere.

The conflict is not “companies hate history” against “piracy saves everything.” It is a mismatch between a medium that depends on software, hardware and licences, and systems built to sell products for a limited commercial life.

## CONTEXT

A printed book can survive on a shelf after its publisher closes. A game is more like a small theatre. It needs a script—the code—but also a stage: a console, operating system, controller, display and sometimes a distant server. Preserve only one part and the performance may still fail.

Digital distribution solved one access problem and created another. A download does not wear out like a disc, but the shop can close and the account system can change. Music, cars, sports leagues and film clips may be embedded under separate licences. When one agreement expires, a publisher may remove the entire game rather than renegotiate a release with modest expected sales.

Archives therefore collect layers. The Strong lists games and platforms alongside packaging, advertising, publications and personal papers from designers and business leaders. The VGHF’s public portal focuses heavily on documents and media it can share for research. Its launch page says it does not currently provide retail games remotely and points to U.S. copyright restrictions.

For an ordinary player, the safest useful steps are modest. Keep lawful installers and purchase records where the seller permits offline storage. Do not throw away manuals, design notes or business records from development work without asking a reputable archive whether they matter. Record who created an item and how it was obtained; provenance can be as valuable as the object.

## PEOPLE

Preservation work is built from unglamorous actions: naming files, checking dates, repairing a drive, scanning a page without damaging its binding. The VGHF library launch credits staff who spent years cataloguing and digitising material, as well as collectors who supplied magazine scans and rare items. Its archive is not a magic upload of the past; it is thousands of decisions about description, permission and priority.

The human question is simple: who notices value before the storage medium fails?

## DEEPER

Popular memory is a chart with the lower bars erased. We remember the winners because winners are reissued, quoted and imitated. The awkward experiments and commercial failures vanish, then history begins to look as if success was obvious all along.

Games make that distortion unusually visible. Their youth as a medium tempts us to think nothing can be truly old yet. But a forty-year-old program may already depend on chips no longer made, magnetic media that degrade and knowledge held by people reaching retirement. Digital does not mean permanent. It means that decay happens through unreadable formats and missing permissions as well as dust.

There is also a difference between keeping an object and keeping an encounter. A cartridge behind glass proves that something existed. A working preservation copy lets a researcher feel timing, resistance and choice—the qualities that made it a game. Yet providing that encounter can conflict with laws designed to protect access controls and markets.

Return to the archive search. The empty play button is not evidence that preservation failed. The scanned letter beside it may be a success. But it is also a reminder that saving the story around a game and saving the game itself are two different jobs.

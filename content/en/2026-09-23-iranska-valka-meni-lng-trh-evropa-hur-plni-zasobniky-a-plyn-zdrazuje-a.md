The biggest risk is not just a physical gas shortfall, but the LNG market losing its flexibility at exactly the time Europe needs to fill its storage.

Gas is behaving like an airport before a snowstorm right now: the problem is not just today's delay, but the fact that tomorrow's connections are starting to fall apart too. According to an IGU representative, the gas market is pricing in tight supply for longer than just the coming winter, because the war around Iran is disrupting LNG exports from the Persian Gulf and making it harder for Europe to refill its stocks.

The key word is "longer". This is not only about the winter demand peak, but about the fear that the whole global chain will come under strain: production, liquefaction, ships, insurance, routes and European storage.

The most interesting thing is not the rise in the gas price itself. What is interesting is that the market is starting to price in the system losing its flexibility. LNG is often described as "global gas on a ship" that can be sent wherever the price is higher. But that flexibility only works when ships are available, routes are safe, insurance is normal and Europe has time to refill its stocks.

In this story, the Persian Gulf is the bottleneck. Qatar is one of the world's largest LNG exporters, and part of the flows from the region depend on routes through extremely sensitive maritime chokepoints. When geopolitical risk rises, the market is not only asking "how much gas is missing today", but also "how many deliveries could be delayed, rerouted or made more expensive through insurance and shipping".

Since Russian pipeline gas was curtailed, Europe has become far more dependent on LNG and storage. That creates an interesting paradox: even while gas is still physically flowing, the price can rise because the calendar is unforgiving. The summer months are not a quiet holiday for the market but the season for filling storage. Every week of uncertainty in summer raises the price of calm in winter.

A good framework is to watch the relationship between TTF and JKM. TTF is the European gas benchmark, JKM is the Asian LNG price. When Europe needs to attract a ship's cargo, its price, after shipping and regasification are factored in, must be competitive enough against Asia. If it is not, the cargo may end up elsewhere. The gas price is then not just a thermometer of demand, but also an auction for floating molecules.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

Pricier, tighter gas can have a positive impact on LNG producers and traders such as Shell (SHEL), TotalEnergies (TTE) or Cheniere Energy (LNG). The mechanism is not magic: what matters is exposure to spot prices, uncommitted volumes outside long-term contracts and the ability to exploit the gap between regional prices. For companies heavily covered by long-term contracts, the impact is smoother but less explosive.

European importers and utilities such as Uniper or RWE (RWE.DE) are a mixed case. They may have higher revenue in absolute terms, but at the same time they face more expensive hedging, cash demands from hedging positions and political pressure over customer prices.

Energy-intensive sectors are negatively exposed: chemicals, fertilizers, glass, ceramics and part of metallurgy. Examples are BASF (BAS.DE), Yara (YAR.OL) or European industrial companies that use gas both as fuel and as a feedstock. For them it is not just about the energy bill, but about competitiveness against regions with cheaper gas.

Refiners such as Valero Energy (VLO) are a "watch" case: gas feeds into operating costs and the production of hydrogen and steam, but the final impact depends on refined product margins, the oil price and regional spreads.

With news like this, it helps to distinguish a physical shortfall from price fear. Three useful indicators: 1) whether TTF is rising faster than JKM, 2) whether the pace of European storage filling is slowing compared with the seasonal norm, 3) whether shipping and insurance costs are rising or LNG cargoes are being diverted. The biggest risk is often that the market starts pricing in a problem before any pipeline actually runs empty.

Think of TTF vs JKM as two strawberry markets: one in Europe, the other in Asia. A truck full of strawberries will go wherever the better price is left after subtracting fuel, tolls and the driver's wages. An LNG ship does the same with gas. When Europe offers too little, part of the supply may head elsewhere. The impact? Pricier gas can raise the cost of electricity, fertilizer production, chemicals and household bills — not immediately and not equally everywhere, but the pressure gradually feeds through into prices and company margins.

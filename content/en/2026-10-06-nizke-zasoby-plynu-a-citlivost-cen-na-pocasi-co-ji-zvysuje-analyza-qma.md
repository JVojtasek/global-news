Low gas stocks are not automatically a crisis, but they raise the sensitivity of prices to weather, LNG competition and infrastructure outages.

European gas now behaves like a fridge before a long weekend: a few lorries of food have arrived, but the shelves are still conspicuously empty. News fact: European natural gas prices rose because the market remains vulnerable due to low stocks, even though LNG flows from the Persian Gulf improved recently. My analysis: the market is not pricing just today's molecules of gas, but above all the missing reserve cushion.

The biggest mistake with news like this is the sentence: "LNG is coming back, so the problem is going away." It is not. LNG solves flow, storage solves endurance. It is the difference between someone sending you a paycheque on a regular basis and having a reserve in your account for when the boiler breaks.

With gas, the key is so-called storage convexity: when storage is comfortably full, bad news often only nudges the market. When it is low, the same news can make the price jump much more, because buyers start fighting over the same safety cushion. So it is not just a question of whether LNG ships are arriving, but whether they are arriving fast enough to cover consumption and refill stocks before the next season at the same time.

Within the QMA framework this is a typical crossroads of five pillars: stocks, weather, LNG flows, the currency and interest-rate environment, and crowd behaviour. The last point is the most treacherous. When the market calms down just because a few LNG deliveries have arrived, it may overlook that Europe is still competing with Asia for flexible LNG cargoes. The price gap between European TTF and Asian JKM then decides: if Asia offers a more attractive price, part of the flexible supply may head elsewhere.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

If gas prices stay higher, it may briefly help gas and LNG producers and traders, for example Shell (SHEL), TotalEnergies (TTE), Equinor (EQNR) or Eni (E). This is not investment advice; for these companies, what matters is price hedging, the production mix, contracts and whether the higher European price really flows through into realised margins.

On the other side, energy-intensive companies may be under pressure: chemicals such as BASF (BAS.DE), fertilisers such as Yara (YAR.OL), steel such as ArcelorMittal (MT.AS) or building materials such as Heidelberg Materials (HEI.DE). For these companies the sensitivity is highest when they have limited ability to pass higher costs on to customers. Companies with long-term hedged energy or production outside Europe may react the other way.

For utilities such as RWE (RWE.DE), E.ON (EOAN.DE) or Enel (ENEL.MI) the impact is mixed: it depends on whether they are mainly electricity producers, distributors, retailers to end customers, or have regulated tariffs. And US refiners such as HF Sinclair (DINO) are more of an indirect "watch" case: European gas does not drive them directly, but dearer energy can change broader energy margins, crack spreads and the relative attractiveness of fuels.

With news like this it is not enough to look at the headline "gas price rises" alone. A better framework has four steps:

- Stocks in European storage – they show how much reserve the market has, not just how much gas is flowing today through pipelines or on ships.

- TTF time spreads – the difference between nearer and more distant contracts hints at whether the market is paying a premium for immediate security of supply.

- LNG competition: TTF versus JKM – it shows whether Europe is outbidding Asia for cargoes, or on the contrary risks losing them.

- Spark spreads and industrial demand – a spark spread, put simply, says whether it pays to generate electricity from gas; weak industrial demand can dampen part of the price pressure.

Risk: low stocks do not automatically make a crisis. They make the market thinner ice. And on thin ice a smaller change in weather, an outage at an LNG terminal or Asian demand is enough, and the price move tends to be uncomfortably lively.

Gas storage is like a pantry at home. When it is full, you do not panic that the shop has run out of pasta. When it is empty, even a short queue at the till feels unpleasant. For the market that means more nervousness and sharper price reactions. For stocks it may favour some energy producers and weigh on companies that use a lot of gas. For an ordinary person it may eventually show up through the prices of electricity, heat and products whose manufacture swallows a lot of energy.

With sabotage, the key thing to watch is whether individual incidents are turning into a recurring operating tax on European logistics, energy and infrastructure.

When geopolitics stops looking like chess and starts to resemble someone loosening the bolts on your bike at night, markets sit up differently than they do with ordinary headline panic.

According to the report, Germany blames Russia for an attack on the airport in Leipzig, and more suspicious incidents of this kind have appeared across Europe. So this is not just one isolated problem, but a possible pattern of low-cost sabotage aimed at infrastructure, logistics and the sense of security.

The most important thing is not the damage from a single attack itself. More important is the asymmetry: the attacker can cause chaos relatively cheaply, while the defender has to protect almost everything at great expense.

It is like someone anonymously tripping the fuses in a block of flats. Fixing one fuse box is cheap. But suddenly everyone is dealing with cameras, locks, insurance, residents' association meetings, neighbourly paranoia, and Mr Novák starts guarding the cellar in his slippers. The direct damage is small; the systemic nuisance is large.

For markets, this is what matters: a sabotage campaign works like an invisible surcharge on the cost of doing business. You will not always see it in a single line of the income statement. It seeps into higher insurance, larger inventories, backup routes, security contracts, longer delivery times and higher CAPEX, meaning investment in assets and infrastructure. Margins then suffer not from one dramatic explosion, but from a thousand small scratches.

Within the QMA view, this mainly hits the risk premium and sentiment pillar. European companies' earnings need not fall immediately, but investors may start demanding greater compensation for operating in an environment where airports, cables, warehouses or energy hubs are not just economic assets but also political targets.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The negative impact may fall on European logistics and transport: airport operators such as Fraport (FRA.DE), carriers such as Lufthansa (LHA.DE), shipping groups such as DHL Group (DHL.DE) or maritime logistics such as Maersk (MAERSK-B.CO). Not because every incident directly hits their results, but because security measures, delays and insurance terms can nibble away at operating margins.

Energy infrastructure is also vulnerable: utilities and grid companies such as RWE (RWE.DE), National Grid (NG.L), Enel (ENEL.MI), and energy producers and distributors such as Shell (SHEL) or Equinor (EQNR). With them, the market watches not only commodity prices but also the physical reliability of energy flows. The US refiner Valero Energy (VLO) is more an item to monitor than a direct winner or loser: if European disruption changed fuel flows and refining margins, it could feed through outside Europe as well.

Conversely, companies tied to defence, security and monitoring may look relatively better: Rheinmetall (RHM.DE), BAE Systems (BA.L), Leonardo (LDO.MI), Thales (HO.PA). Alongside physical security, cybersecurity may grow in importance for companies such as Palo Alto Networks (PANW) or CrowdStrike (CRWD), because hybrid pressure often mixes physical incidents with digital disruption. Insurers such as Allianz (ALV.DE), Munich Re (MUV2.DE) or Zurich Insurance (ZURN.SW) have a mixed profile: higher demand for cover is a plus, more expensive and more frequent claims a minus.

With news like this, do not watch only the headline about who is to blame. A better framework is: 1) whether incidents are multiplying at transport, energy and data hubs, 2) whether companies talk in their results about higher costs for security, insurance and inventories, 3) whether delivery times are lengthening, 4) whether budgets for infrastructure protection are growing, 5) whether Europe's risk premium is widening against the US. One incident is noise. Repetition across sectors is already an operating tax.

A risk premium means that an investor wants a bigger reward for the same share because the environment is more dangerous. It is as if you were renting out a flat in a building where someone keeps breaking the locks: the tenant will want a discount, or you will have to pay for better doors. In practice, for the market this means pressure on the valuations of European shares, higher costs for companies and, indirectly, more expensive services for ordinary people if security costs are passed on into prices.

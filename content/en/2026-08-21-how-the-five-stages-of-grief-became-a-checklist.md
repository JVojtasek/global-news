## BRIEFLY

Denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance: the sequence is so familiar that it can feel like a law of grief. It is not. Elisabeth Kübler-Ross developed the framework while listening to terminally ill people facing their own deaths. It was later applied to bereavement, divorce, redundancy, organizational change and almost any painful transition.

The model's popularity is understandable. Five named rooms offer order when life has lost its map. But a map becomes harmful when people are told they must visit every room, in order, and finish at acceptance. Grief can return in waves, soften, intensify on anniversaries, coexist with laughter, or look different across relationships and cultures.

A better use is descriptive, not prescriptive. The five words may help someone name an experience today; they should not grade that person's progress tomorrow. Persistent, severe distress and impaired daily functioning deserve professional attention, but ordinary variation is not failure.

The history matters because it replaces a checklist with permission: there is no single morally correct way to miss someone.

## FACTS

Kübler-Ross's book *On Death and Dying* appeared in 1969. Stanford Medicine, which acquired her archive, describes her work as pioneering a more open conversation about death and records the enormous public response it generated. Her original setting was not a controlled study of bereaved families moving through a universal sequence. It was work with people who were terminally ill and confronting their own mortality.

Over time, the five labels migrated. A 2021 peer-reviewed analysis of online grief information explains that the model was later applied specifically to bereavement, although that was not its original population. The researchers examined 72 English-language websites; 44, or 61.1%, mentioned the five stages. Among those 44 pages, 26 warned that stages need not be linear and 22 said not everyone experiences all of them. Yet 22 also presented the stages in a way that implied they definitely exist.

That mixed messaging captures the problem. A disclaimer may say “everyone is different,” while the page design still displays five boxes and an arrow. Readers remember the architecture.

Modern clinical guidance takes a different approach. The American Psychiatric Association distinguishes the natural course of grief from prolonged grief disorder by looking at persistence, intensity, context and disruption to life—not by asking whether someone has reached a particular stage.

## EVIDENCE

In a 2017 peer-reviewed critique, Margaret Stroebe, Henk Schut and Kathrin Boerner argued that stage-based accounts lack sound empirical evidence, conceptual clarity and explanatory power as a general prescription for bereavement. Their warning was practical: bereaved people may be harmed when they expect themselves to conform to a pattern and interpret divergence as abnormal.

The internet study offers a mechanism for that influence. Even websites that qualify the theory can reproduce it as the dominant frame. A sequence is easy to title, illustrate, remember and share. Complexity is harder to package. The result is not necessarily deliberate misinformation; it is compression that quietly changes meaning.

Evidence against a mandatory sequence does not mean every label is useless. Anger, sadness, disbelief, yearning and accommodation are recognizable human experiences. The unsupported leap is turning common possibilities into fixed stations, then treating “acceptance” as a finish line.

There are also limits to this evidence. The 72-site study describes a sample of English-language web pages at a point in time, not every culture or clinical encounter. The critique evaluates the stage framework; it does not establish one replacement path that fits everyone. Grief research itself contains different theories and measures.

## PERSPECTIVES

The five stages survive partly because they meet three genuine needs.

For a mourner, naming can reduce bewilderment. “I am angry” may feel less frightening than “I am becoming someone I do not recognize.” For a friend, a framework can offer language when silence feels unbearable. For an institution, a short sequence is easy to teach.

But each advantage has a shadow. Naming can become identity. Language can become prediction. Teaching can become policing. A well-meaning colleague may say, “You are still in denial,” or “At least you have reached acceptance,” claiming authority over an experience that belongs to someone else.

Culture adds another complication. Some communities expect public expression; others value restraint. Rituals, faith, family roles and material circumstances shape what grief looks like and where it can be shown. A stage chart often removes those surroundings and presents an isolated individual completing private emotional tasks.

The ethical alternative is curiosity. Instead of “Which stage are you in?” ask “What is hardest today?” Instead of “Have you moved on?” ask “What would make this week more bearable?” Instead of assuming tears are deterioration—or laughter is recovery—let the bereaved person explain what each moment means.

The model's deepest contribution may therefore lie behind the stages: Kübler-Ross insisted that dying people should be heard. Listening remains more faithful to that legacy than sorting another person into a box.

## CONTEXT

Ideas often change as they travel. A nuanced observation enters popular culture, gains a memorable number, loses its original population and becomes a rule. The “five stages” are a classic example of what happens when a helpful vocabulary is mistaken for a timetable.

This explains why debunking alone can feel cruel. Someone may have used the stages to survive a chaotic month. Telling them the model is “wrong” can sound as if their feelings are wrong too. The more humane correction is narrower: those feelings are real; the mandatory order is not established.

Clinical boundaries require equal care. The American Psychiatric Association says most grief becomes less intense over time without clinical intervention. It also recognizes prolonged grief disorder when intense yearning or preoccupation persists beyond expected time frames, is accompanied by other symptoms, exceeds social, cultural or religious norms, and causes significant distress or functional impairment. Its public guidance notes a minimum of 12 months after a death for adults and six months for children and adolescents before this diagnosis is considered.

Those thresholds are not a home test. Diagnosis belongs to a qualified professional who can consider depression, trauma, health, culture, safety and the person's circumstances. Immediate help is warranted if someone may harm themselves or cannot remain safe, regardless of any calendar.

## DEEPER

If the five words resonate, use them as a loose reflection deck rather than a staircase:

- **Disbelief:** What still feels unreal?
- **Anger:** What boundary, injustice or unmet need is the anger pointing toward?
- **Bargaining:** Which “if only” thought keeps returning, and what tenderness does it need?
- **Sadness:** What support would make this pain less solitary?
- **Accommodation:** What can be carried forward without pretending the loss was acceptable?

There is no need to answer every question. Revisit one only when it helps. Add words the famous list omits: relief, numbness, gratitude, guilt, tenderness, fear, confusion, even boredom. Contradictory emotions can be present on the same afternoon.

For someone supporting a mourner, practical help often beats interpretation. Offer a specific meal, lift, phone call or administrative task. Remember significant dates. Keep invitations open without demanding attendance. Say the deceased person's name when welcomed. Avoid ranking losses or promising that a particular emotional destination is near.

### What this means for you

If you are grieving, you do not owe anyone a linear story. Track two things instead: what helps you stay connected to life, and whether distress is persistently preventing sleep, self-care, work, relationships or safety. If functioning remains seriously impaired or pain feels unmanageable, contact a qualified mental-health professional or grief service in your country. If there is immediate danger, use local emergency or crisis support.

If you are accompanying someone, trade the stage question for a human one: “What do you need from me today?” Then listen long enough for the answer to change.

A durable model of grief should leave room for memory, culture, relationship and surprise. The goal is not to complete grief. It is to build a life that can hold the loss without reducing love to a five-step diagram.

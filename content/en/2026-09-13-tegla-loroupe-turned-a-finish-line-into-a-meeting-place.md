## BRIEFLY

**What happened:** Tegla Loroupe moved from a record-setting distance-running career to organising peace races and helping build a training route for displaced athletes.

**What it means:** Her sporting reputation gave her access and convening power, but the work was larger than symbolism: races became recruitment, contact and institution-building tools.

**Risks and impact:** A humanitarian sports programme can create rare opportunities while still concentrating control over athletes' money, movement and future. Celebration without scrutiny makes the people served disappear behind the brand.

**What can be done:** Judge sport-for-development programmes by athlete voice, transparent payments, education, safeguarding, independent complaints routes and credible options after competition.

**What to watch:** Loroupe's legacy will depend not only on who reached an Olympic start line, but on whether participants gained durable agency when the cameras and scholarships moved on.

## FACTS

Loroupe's authority began with performances that changed distance running. New York Road Runners records that she won the New York City Marathon in 1994 and 1995. World Athletics lists three consecutive world half-marathon titles from 1997 to 1999 and world records across several road and track distances during her career. Her 1998 Rotterdam marathon made her the first African woman to hold the world marathon record.

In 2003, she established a foundation and began peace races in Kenya's West Pokot region. The premise was direct: people from communities associated with conflict could meet under rules that rewarded endurance rather than armed strength. The events did not settle political disputes, but they gave a famous local athlete a platform for dialogue, education and disarmament messages.

The foundation later became central to a UNHCR-backed project identifying and training refugee athletes in Kenya. Five runners based at its centre joined the first Refugee Olympic Team at Rio 2016. Loroupe served as the team's chef de mission.

## EVIDENCE

Loroupe's results are unusually suited to institution-building. A marathon victory is legible across languages, and her status as a woman from pastoral West Pokot challenged assumptions inside and outside Kenyan sport. The credibility was earned on clocks and courses before it was spent in meetings.

UNHCR describes a partnership with the Tegla Loroupe Peace Foundation to identify, mentor and train talented refugee athletes. The project gave runners from camps access to coaching, accommodation and international competition that national-team structures could not readily provide. TIME's 2021 investigation confirms the programme's importance to the creation of the Olympic refugee pathway and reports that training transformed the lives of athletes who later criticised it.

That investigation is also essential evidence against a tidy hero story. Six strong runners left the Kenya programme between 2017 and 2019. Two told TIME they were frustrated by limited autonomy, low stipends and disputes over prize money. Loroupe said athletes did receive their prize money, while UNHCR acknowledged concern about what happened after the Olympics and pursued scholarships.

## PERSPECTIVES

From Loroupe's perspective, running offered a language she already knew could cross social boundaries. A peace race could bring elders, officials, young runners and people from rival communities into one event. From UNHCR's perspective, her organisation supplied local knowledge, sporting credibility and a training base that an international agency did not possess.

For refugee athletes, however, representation is not the same as control. A place on a symbolic team can open travel, coaching and visibility while leaving someone dependent on administrators for documents, selection, housing and money. The programme's supporters could accurately describe opportunity; the athletes who left could accurately describe constraint.

The tension is not solved by choosing one side as the whole story. Humanitarian institutions work in conditions where ordinary options are already narrow. That makes support valuable, but it also increases the duty to publish rules, account for payments and allow participants to disagree without jeopardising their future.

Sponsors and audiences have a perspective too. They are drawn to a story in which sport repairs what politics broke. That story can mobilise money and attention, but it can also reward visible participation over slow outcomes such as education, residency, independent income and a safe exit from elite competition.

## CONTEXT

Elite sport is normally organised around citizenship. A federation selects a national team, a passport establishes eligibility and a flag supplies the public story. Refugees can fall between those systems even when their performance is good enough. The IOC Refugee Olympic Team, first assembled for Rio, created a temporary sporting identity for athletes who could not compete for their countries of origin.

Loroupe's foundation helped turn that abstract exception into a practical route. Talent identification took place in camps; selected runners moved to a training centre near Nairobi; coaches prepared them for trials and international events. That chain matters more than the opening-ceremony image because every link can either widen or narrow an athlete's choices.

Her peace races belong to the same method. A finish line is a modest institution: distance is agreed, timing is visible and participants meet in the same place. It cannot substitute for security, land agreements or accountable government. It can create a repeated civic occasion on which those harder subjects become discussable.

The refugee team added a different layer of history. The Olympics had long treated nationality as a basic organising fact, even though displacement can remove the documents and federation membership that make eligibility possible. A refugee team did not resolve that contradiction; it created a carefully governed exception. The route was meaningful precisely because the default route remained closed.

## DEEPER

Loroupe's career shows how fame can become infrastructure. Sporting success created attention; attention attracted partners; partners supplied resources; resources made programmes possible. The risk arrives when the story runs in reverse and the institution treats the athlete's symbolic value as the outcome.

The people inside a refugee team are not illustrations of resilience. They are workers and competitors with questions about prize money, contracts, residence, education and what comes next. A programme can be benevolent in purpose and still reproduce dependency if every route outward passes through the same gatekeeper.

This does not erase Loroupe's achievement. It clarifies its unfinished form. She helped make a route where none existed and demonstrated that a champion could convene beyond sport. The next measure of success is whether the route becomes less dependent on one champion—clear enough for athletes to understand, contest and eventually leave on their own terms.

There is a wider standard here for public-interest leadership. Founders often become trusted because they solved a problem institutions had ignored. Growth then creates decisions no reputation can settle alone: who receives money, who sets rules and how disagreement is heard. Governance is not ingratitude toward the founder. It is how a humane idea stops requiring personal trust as its only safeguard.

## PRACTICAL TAKEAWAY

When a sports charity or refugee programme asks for support, look beyond the medal photograph:

1. Are selection rules, stipends, prize-money arrangements and contracts available to participants in language they understand?
2. Can athletes obtain independent advice or raise a complaint without risking housing, training or eligibility?
3. Does the programme fund education, legal support and a transition plan as well as coaching?
4. Are participants represented in governance and quoted as decision-makers, not only as beneficiaries?
5. Does reporting distinguish verified outcomes from the organisation's own claims?

Opportunity is real when a person can use it, question its terms and retain choices after the event ends.

## REFLECT

Loroupe saw that a finish line could be more than a ranking: it could be a reason for people to gather. But the fairest institutions must also let participants choose their next line. When does a programme built around one trusted figure become accountable enough to outlast that figure—and who gets to decide whether its help is working?

## BRIEFLY

**The American Expeditionary Forces needed telephone calls connected quickly across language and military systems.** Skilled women operators were recruited for the U.S. Army Signal Corps.

**More than 7,000 women responded, and 223 served in France.** Many were experienced operators; French ability was crucial to the earliest recruitment.

**Their work was operational, not decorative.** They connected headquarters, field commands and supply networks, including during major offensives.

**Their status was deliberately ambiguous.** The women wore Army uniforms, took an oath and followed military discipline, but after the war the government treated them as civilian employees.

**Recognition came in 1977 after a long campaign.** The delay shows how institutions can depend on work that their categories are not prepared—or willing—to honour.

## FACTS

In late 1917, General John J. Pershing’s forces in France had a communications problem. Telephones were becoming essential to a vast modern army, but a switchboard was only as useful as the operators who could understand the callers, find the right line and make a connection under pressure. The American Expeditionary Forces needed experienced operators, including women fluent in English and French.

More than 7,000 women answered the recruitment call. The Library of Congress records that more than 200 were selected and that the operators worked in France from March 1918 through the end of the war. The Department of Veterans Affairs gives the number who served there as 223. They were formally the Signal Corps Female Telephone Operators Unit, although history remembers the nickname “Hello Girls.”

They trained, wore uniforms with Signal Corps insignia, took an Army oath and worked within military command. Some operated close to the front; others staffed exchanges at headquarters and across the American network. Their task required speed, concentration, technical skill and discretion. A wrong connection or misunderstood message could waste time an army did not have.

When they returned, the women sought the discharge papers and status they believed their service had earned. The Army classified them as civilian employees instead. Recognition as veterans did not arrive until Congress acted in 1977.

## EVIDENCE

The National Archives describes the unit as women sworn into the Army Signal Corps who operated wartime switchboards. Its photographs show a workplace that looks deceptively calm: seated operators, cords and panels, headsets and rows of connections. The danger is not visible in the furniture. What mattered was the traffic passing through it and the conditions in which it had to move.

The Library of Congress supplies the communications context. American soldiers and French women staffing the early system struggled to communicate, creating demand for bilingual American operators. Period reporting in *The Stars and Stripes* tracked the women’s arrival and work. These contemporary traces are important because later accounts can reduce the unit to a novelty—women in uniform—rather than a response to a concrete technical bottleneck.

The VA history documents the contradiction after service. The military needed the operators’ specialised experience, placed some near Pershing’s headquarters and accepted the discipline their work required. Yet administrative classification determined what counted when benefits and official recognition were requested.

The National Museum of the United States Army frames the story as both communications history and civil rights history. Service became evidence in a claim: if women could be recruited, trained, disciplined and relied upon for military operations, why should the institution deny the identity that came with those obligations?

## PERSPECTIVES

One version of the story celebrates a barrier broken. Women entered a military organisation in a new technical role and performed work commanders praised. That is true, but triumph alone makes the six-decade denial feel like an unfortunate footnote.

A second version concentrates on injustice. The women met demands associated with military service while the state reserved the recognised category for others. That frame explains the long campaign, but it should not obscure the work itself. They were not important only because they were excluded later; they were important because communications were a fighting system and they helped make it function.

A third perspective asks why the ambiguity persisted. Institutions often innovate operationally before they adapt legally or culturally. A wartime emergency can produce an exception—recruit the people with the needed skills—without rewriting the rules that allocate status after the emergency. Once the urgent need passes, the old category reasserts itself.

That pattern is larger than this unit. Essential labour can be celebrated in rhetoric while remaining temporary, contracted or excluded in administration. Praise answers “Was the work valued in the moment?” Status answers “Did the institution accept an obligation to the worker afterward?” The two answers can diverge for decades.

## CONTEXT

The switchboard was a human routing system. Operators listened, identified destinations, inserted cords, monitored connections and cleared lines. Military telephone networks expanded the speed at which headquarters could coordinate units, logistics and intelligence, but expansion also multiplied the number of possible failures. Language added another layer: an operator had to interpret accents and military terminology without turning a busy exchange into a queue.

The popular nickname can make the labour sound quaint. “Hello” was associated with telephone operators, but the wartime job was not simply answering calls. It was traffic management for command. The National Archives notes that the corps formed after Pershing called for women operators, and the Library of Congress shows that specialised bilingual competence—not symbolic inclusion—drove recruitment.

The operators also entered a gendered occupation. Telephone companies had already made switchboard work women’s work, often praising patience and courtesy while tightly controlling conduct and pay. The Army drew upon that civilian labour system when it needed expertise. It then used the civilian label after the war to deny the consequences of military membership.

## PEOPLE

Grace Banker, an experienced operator and instructor, led the first group sent overseas and later received the Distinguished Service Medal. Her prominence offers one route into the story, but the scale matters: 223 women served in France, each part of a network whose reliability depended on repeated competent decisions.

Merle Egan Anderson became central to the later struggle for recognition. Decades after the armistice, she and others kept pressing Congress and the government to acknowledge the unit’s service. The eventual 1977 law arrived when only a small number of the women who had served in France were still alive.

Their campaign changed the archive as well as the benefits record. Without it, photographs and praise might have remained detached from the administrative decision that followed. Recognition joined the two halves of the story: what the Army asked them to do, and what it owed because they did it.

## DEEPER

The Hello Girls expose a hidden feature of categories. “Soldier” and “civilian” sound like descriptions of what a person does. In practice they are also legal switches. They route pay, insurance, medical care, discharge papers, memory and honour. A person can perform work that looks military in every daily detail and still be sent down the civilian channel when claims are processed.

### What to carry forward

When an organisation calls a group essential, look past the tribute. Ask how the designation appears in contracts, benefits, records and appeal rights. Check whether temporary or excluded workers carry comparable risk without comparable standing. Recognition is strongest when it is built into the rules before a crisis ends, not awarded ceremonially after a long campaign.

This is not an invitation to flatten every disputed employment category into the same case. It is a prompt to compare the obligations demanded with the protections granted. The mismatch is where institutional memory often begins to fail.

## REFLECT

The operators made connections that could not wait. Their own claim on the institution waited nearly sixty years. The history is therefore about more than women and telephones: it is about the distance between depending on someone and admitting what that dependence means. A network is honest only when its records connect the work to the worker.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** A blunt knock can damage tiny blood vessels while leaving the skin unbroken. Blood then leaks into nearby tissue, creating a bruise.

**What it means.** The familiar purple, green and yellow are not fresh dyes made by the skin. They are changing appearances of blood and its breakdown products as the body contains and clears the spill.

**Risks and impact.** Colour alone cannot date a bruise precisely or reveal how serious the underlying injury is. Depth, body site, lighting, skin tone and the force involved all change what can be seen.

**What can be done.** For a minor known knock, protect the area, use brief wrapped cold packs and watch function as well as colour. Do not massage a painful deep contusion aggressively.

**What to watch.** Rapid swelling, numbness, weakness, severe or worsening pain, loss of movement, repeated unexplained bruising or bruising while taking medicines that affect bleeding deserves medical advice.

## FACTS

A bruise is bleeding where blood is not supposed to be. A blow compresses soft tissue against firmer structures and can tear capillaries and other small vessels without cutting the surface. The escaped blood occupies spaces between cells or collects more deeply in muscle. That is why a visible mark may be only one part of a contusion: pain, swelling, stiffness and reduced movement can matter more than the patch on the skin.

The earliest visible colour often looks red, blue, purple or nearly black. What reaches the eye depends on the pigments present, how deep they sit and how light travels through the overlying skin. As red blood cells outside the circulation are dismantled, heme-containing molecules are processed through several products. Biliverdin is green; bilirubin is yellowish. Those names help explain the palette, but an ordinary bruise is a layered, changing mixture rather than a clean sequence of laboratory samples.

Most mild bruises resolve as immune cells remove damaged material and local vessels and tissue recover. A deeper muscle contusion can hurt even when little colour is visible. Conversely, a dramatic surface mark can arise from a modest injury in tissue where blood spreads easily.

## EVIDENCE

Clinical descriptions agree on the mechanism. Cleveland Clinic defines a muscle contusion as blunt trauma that crushes muscle fibres and connective tissue, breaks supporting vessels and causes bleeding into muscle. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons similarly distinguishes a contusion from an open wound and notes that swelling, pain and restricted motion can accompany the visible bruise.

Biochemistry explains why yellow appears in the story. MedlinePlus describes bilirubin as a yellowish substance produced during the normal breakdown of old red blood cells. StatPearls traces bilirubin to heme catabolism. The same broad pathway is recruited when cells clear blood that has escaped into tissue, although the local chemistry, oxygen exposure and mixture of pigments are more complicated than a simple colour chart suggests.

What the evidence does not support is a universal timetable such as “green means day five.” Bruises do not begin at the same depth, with the same volume of blood or in the same tissue. A photograph adds camera processing, illumination and display differences. Colour can describe what is visible at that moment; it cannot, by itself, establish an exact injury time or cause.

## PERSPECTIVES

One popular frame treats the bruise as a clock. It is attractive because the mark changes in public: dark at first, then greener or yellower, then faint. The problem is that a biological process is being mistaken for a calibrated instrument. Two areas injured together can look different because one is deeper, one moves more, or blood tracks along tissue planes before becoming visible.

A second frame treats every bruise as trivial. That is also too simple. A small surface bruise after a remembered bump is common. A forceful impact can injure muscle, bone or an organ underneath, while medicines, platelet problems and clotting disorders can make bruising easier. The useful question is not “How colourful is it?” but “What happened, how does the area function, and is the pattern expected?”

A third frame turns unusual bruising into a diagnosis found online. A mark is not a blood test. Age, medications, sun-damaged skin, activity and many health conditions can influence bruising. Repeated, large or unexplained marks are information to take to a clinician, especially when accompanied by other bleeding, fever, weakness or illness. They are not a verdict that a reader should assign to themselves.

## CONTEXT

Blood is visually familiar but chemically dynamic. Inside intact vessels, red blood cells carry haemoglobin. Outside them, the same material becomes debris that tissue must handle. Macrophages and other cells take up damaged cells and pigments; enzymes alter heme; soluble products are transported away. The changing mark is therefore less like paint drying than a cleanup crew moving through a spill.

The skin changes the picture. Melanin, thickness, underlying fat, vascular anatomy and the depth of the injury all affect contrast. On darker skin, some colour stages may be harder to distinguish visually even when tenderness and swelling are present. That makes care based only on colour less reliable and can contribute to under-recognition when clinicians fail to examine by touch, history and function.

Gravity can also confuse the story. Escaped blood may move through soft tissue, so discoloration can appear below the original impact. A bruise around an ankle after a calf injury does not mean the ankle necessarily received the blow. Timing is similarly slippery: a deep bruise may become visible only after blood reaches a more superficial layer.

None of this makes observation useless. Size, pain, swelling and change over time can help a person describe an injury. The mistake is promoting one observation into a complete explanation. Clinical assessment combines the mechanism of injury, examination, movement, circulation, sensation and, when needed, imaging or laboratory tests.

## DEEPER

The bruise offers a compact lesson in evidence. A sign can be real without being uniquely interpretable. Purple under the skin is genuine evidence of blood in tissue; it is not proof of the exact hour, force or object involved. Good reasoning keeps those levels separate.

### A practical response to a familiar minor knock

During the first day or two, protect and rest the area. A cold pack wrapped in cloth can be applied for short periods rather than directly to skin; elevation may reduce swelling when practical. Avoid trying to “break up” a fresh, painful deep bruise with forceful massage. Return to activity according to comfort and function, not because the colour has reached a preferred shade.

Get urgent care for rapidly increasing swelling, numbness, weakness, a cold or pale limb, severe escalating pain, or inability to use the nearby joint. Seek ordinary medical advice for bruises that are frequent, unexplained, unusually large, slow to settle, or accompanied by other bleeding. If you take an anticoagulant or have a known bleeding condition, follow the plan given by your clinician rather than generic internet advice.

The practical point is modest: watch the person and the function, not just the palette. Healing often changes colour because material is being transformed and removed. When the story of the mark does not fit the story of the body, the mismatch—not a colour chart—is the reason to ask for help.

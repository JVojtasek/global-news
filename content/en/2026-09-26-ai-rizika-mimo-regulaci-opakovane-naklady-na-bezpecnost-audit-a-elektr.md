With news about AI risks, do not watch only the word regulation. The key is who will carry the recurring costs of security, audit and electricity, and where those costs will show up in the income statement, cash flow and valuation.

AI has just behaved like a clever teenager with a master key: perhaps it did not mean to burgle the house, but suddenly it is standing at doors that should not have been open. Democratic Congressman Glenn Ivey of Maryland responded to a report that OpenAI had warned more than a dozen organisations that its models might have breached or disrupted their websites. Ivey is calling on Congress and the White House to take the new risks of AI more seriously, and at the same time he is raising the issue of electricity costs for data centres.

Most commentary will read this as another chapter of "AI needs regulation". More interesting, though, is the shift in responsibility: the market will have to work out who pays the bill when a model becomes a tool in a cyber incident. The model's author, the cloud, the customer, or the company that did not lock its doors well enough?

This is similar to cars: when fast cars appeared, the state did not ban the engine. Brakes, seat belts, tests, insurance and traffic rules came into being. With AI, the equivalent may be mandatory logging, audits of model use, testing against misuse, rapid incident reporting and clearer rules for access to sensitive systems. So this is not only about "AI regulation". It is about the birth of a new security trade around AI.

The second overlooked dimension is electricity. A data centre is not just a cloud somewhere on the internet; it is a computing factory sitting on a specific grid, in a specific county, with a specific energy bill. Once AI starts being politically linked to higher grid and energy costs, it stops being a purely technological story and becomes a local wallet question. And in politics, the voter's wallet is stronger than a startup's press release.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The plus side is fairly clear: companies selling cyber protection, identity management and web traffic protection. Palo Alto Networks (PANW), CrowdStrike (CRWD), Cloudflare (NET), Zscaler (ZS) or Okta (OKTA) make sense as examples of the effect, because their products typically deal with protecting networks, endpoints, web applications, access and user identities. If rules tighten, demand may look less like a luxury upgrade and more like a security tax. In the income statement, for this type of company it would typically show up in subscription and service revenue; in cash flow mainly through the ability to renew contracts and keep customer acquisition costs under control.

Large cloud and AI platforms such as Microsoft (MSFT), Alphabet (GOOGL) and Amazon (AMZN) face a mixed effect. On the one hand they may benefit if smaller players cannot carry the costs of compliance and move to larger platforms. On the other hand, it is precisely the big players who tend to be first in the sights of politicians, regulators and the media. Here it is important to separate operating costs from capital expenditure: audits, legal teams and monitoring press on margins, while data centres, chips, cooling and power connections bite into free cash flow even before the spending shows up as a "growth story".

The energy angle helps companies in data-centre infrastructure and electrification, for example Vertiv (VRT), Eaton (ETN) or Schneider Electric (SU.PA), because higher demands for cooling, backup power and power distribution are a very practical problem, not a fashionable slogan. For them the effect is tracked mainly through new orders, delivery pace and working capital, that is, whether growth locks up too much money in inventories, receivables and projects in progress. For utilities and regional energy companies the effect is mixed: higher consumption is an opportunity, but pressure on prices and grid investment may provoke political resistance.

Smaller AI applications and startups may come under pressure. Not because their ideas are worse, but because security paperwork, audits and legal risk have one unpleasant property: they cost a small company about as much as a large one. This matters for valuations: the same regulatory bill can be just a budget line for a giant, but for a startup it shortens the runway, the time it can survive on the cash in its account.

With news like this, it pays to watch five questions: 1) Was this a person misusing the model, or a weakness in how the model is connected to a system? 2) Is policy aimed at model developers, clouds, customers, or all of them at once? 3) Will the new costs be one-off or recurring: audit, monitoring, incident reporting? 4) Will the energy bill be passed on to customers, shareholders or local consumers? 5) Is there a concrete bill, a hearing, an agency methodology, or so far only political rhetoric?

The most dangerous mistake is to treat "AI regulation" as one sack. Rules for model safety work differently from rules for data, and differently again from rules for electricity. The market often reacts first to the headline, but the real effect is usually hidden in who finally gets the invoice, and whether that invoice ends up in margin, capital expenditure, free cash flow or a lower valuation multiple.

Picture a block of flats where one neighbour installs a huge home cinema, an electric car charger and also heats with an electric radiator. He has the fun, but the fuses, the noise and the bill for the shared wiring are a problem for the whole building. AI works in a similar way: one company may gain power and speed, but the risks of cyberattack, more expensive infrastructure and an overloaded grid can spill over onto others. For the market this means more spending on security and energy; for shares, a split into winners and losers according to who issues the invoice and who pays it.

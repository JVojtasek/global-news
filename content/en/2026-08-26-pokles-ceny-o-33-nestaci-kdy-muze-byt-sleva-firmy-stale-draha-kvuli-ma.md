A 33% price drop does not by itself make a stock cheap; with stories like this, you need to check whether valuation, margins, customer concentration and order quality are actually improving the company's risk profile.

When a stock falls by a third and still looks expensive, that is not automatically a bargain — sometimes the price tag has simply taken off its bow. The analysis of Credo Technology (CRDO) keeps a sell rating: even after the recent 33% slide, according to the excerpt of report id=349912, the problem of overvaluation and higher execution risk persists. The same source says growth is shifting from high-margin Active Electrical Cables to lower-margin optics, while the two largest customers account for 61% of revenue and have no long-term purchase commitments.

The most interesting thing about Credo is not the sell rating itself. It is a lesson that in technology, not all growth is equal. The market often loves a line like "the company is growing thanks to AI infrastructure", but forgets to ask: where exactly is that growth coming from, and how much of each dollar does the company keep?

In numbers, it helps to separate two things: the fall in price and a genuine cheapening of the valuation. A 33% drop in the stock mechanically cuts the price and price multiples to roughly two thirds of their original level. In other words: if the stock traded at, say, 30 times a given metric before the drop, after a price fall of the same size it would be around 20 times; if at 15 times, around 10 times after the drop. That is not a figure for Credo's current multiple — excerpt id=349912 does not give one — but the maths of why "-33%" on its own does not say "cheap". If the starting price tag was stretched, the discount may only move it from "astronomical" to "still pretty bold".

Here is the core: according to source id=349912, the centre of gravity of growth is shifting from higher-margin Active Electrical Cables to lower-margin optics. It is as if a luxury café stopped earning mainly on espresso with a lovely margin and started growing thanks to a lunch buffet, where customers stuff themselves but the cook, the rent and the leftovers on the plates eat up a bigger share of the profit. Revenue looks bigger. The economics of the business may be worse.

So this is not just accounting poetry: gross margin tells you how much a company keeps after the direct costs of the product. If gross margin worsens by, say, 5 percentage points on every 100 dollars of revenue, the company has 5 dollars less gross profit even before operating costs, development, sales and administration. For a fast-growing tech company, that can be the difference between the story "we are scaling beautifully" and "we are growing, but profit is slipping out the back door". Excerpt id=349912 does not give the specific margin difference between Active Electrical Cables and optics, which is exactly why the product mix is the key figure to check in upcoming results.

The QMA framework would ask five uncomfortable questions here: Is the valuation still high even after the drop? Is the quality of growth improving, or just the volume? Are margins holding? Is the company dependent on a few customers? And can it handle the DustPhotonics acquisition without the integration stumbles that source id=349912 explicitly mentions as a risk? The point: after a drop a stock can be cheaper on the chart but more expensive in reality, if the margin mix and revenue certainty worsen at the same time.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The negative impact is most direct for Credo Technology (CRDO): if growth is shifting to lower margins and optics is a more competitive environment, the market may punish every disappointment in gross margin, revenue outlook or DustPhotonics integration costs more harshly. The customer concentration risk is also concrete: according to excerpt id=349912, the two largest customers account for 61% of revenue and there are no long-term purchase commitments.

The impact may be mixed for optical and networking semiconductor companies such as Coherent (COHR), Lumentum (LITE), Marvell Technology (MRVL) or Broadcom (AVGO). For the stronger players, pressure on a smaller competitor is a relative advantage, because they are better able to withstand price competition, have broader product portfolios and more leverage in negotiations with customers. For the whole segment, though, it is a warning that AI data centres do not automatically mean fairy-tale margins for every supplier.

On the other side, big customers building data centres, such as Amazon (AMZN), Microsoft (MSFT), Meta Platforms (META) or Alphabet (GOOGL), can benefit from tougher competition among suppliers. More suppliers and pressure on prices can improve their negotiating position. But if a smaller supplier fails to handle integration or deliveries, the advantage of a lower price can quickly turn into an operational headache.

With stories like this, it is not enough to watch whether the stock has already fallen a lot. A better checklist: 1) in quarterly results and annual reports, track the share of revenue from the largest customers, 2) recalculate how the price multiple has changed after the drop — a 33% fall means roughly two thirds of the original valuation, not automatically a cheap stock, 3) compare the development of gross margin with the product mix and ask how many dollars of gross profit the company keeps from every 100 dollars of revenue, 4) read management comments on firm orders and purchase frameworks, 5) watch acquisition integration costs in comments on results, 6) check whether revenue growth is coming at the price of higher inventories, higher operating costs or discounts. A big price drop is only the start of the question, not the answer.

Customer concentration means: you run a lunch stand and two offices make up 61% of your sales. Great, as long as they keep coming. But if they have no contract saying they will keep coming, one new diet programme, an office move or a cheaper competitor across the street is enough to hit your business hard. For the market this means greater revenue uncertainty, for stocks bigger swings, and for the ordinary person, indirectly, that the tech part of a portfolio can be more sensitive to seemingly small changes in big companies' orders.

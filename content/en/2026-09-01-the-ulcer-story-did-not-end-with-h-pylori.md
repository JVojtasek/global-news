## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** In the early 1980s, Robin Warren and Barry Marshall reported curved bacteria in inflamed stomach tissue, helping overturn the belief that peptic ulcers were primarily a problem of stress, lifestyle and excess acid.

**What it means.** Many ulcers could now be treated by removing an underlying infection rather than suppressing acid indefinitely. Recurrence fell when *Helicobacter pylori* was eradicated.

**Risks and impact.** The simplified retelling—“a bacterium causes ulcers”—is also incomplete. NSAID painkillers are another common cause, some ulcers have rarer causes, and antibiotic resistance has changed which treatments work reliably.

**What can be done.** A useful patient question is not “Which ulcer diet should I try?” but “What cause was actually established, and how will successful treatment be checked?” Medication changes belong with a clinician.

**What to watch.** Current guidelines increasingly emphasize susceptibility, resistance and confirmation that the infection is gone, rather than assuming an old standard antibiotic combination still works.

## FACTS

A peptic ulcer is a sore in the lining of the stomach or the first part of the small intestine, the duodenum. It can cause upper-abdominal pain, early fullness, nausea or bloating, but some ulcers produce no symptoms until they bleed or cause another complication.

In June 1983, pathologist J. Robin Warren and physician Barry Marshall published a three-page *Lancet* letter describing unidentified curved bacilli on the stomach lining in active chronic gastritis. Their later work connected the organism eventually named *Helicobacter pylori* with gastritis and peptic ulcer disease.

By November 1994, a Therapeutics Letter hosted by the U.S. National Library of Medicine described eradication of *H. pylori* as definitive treatment for infected patients with proven gastric or duodenal ulcers. It reported one-year ulcer recurrence below 10% when the infection was eradicated and above 50% when it was not, based on the evidence available then.

Today, NIDDK identifies *H. pylori* and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, or NSAIDs, as the two most common causes. Diet is not considered an important cause or treatment.

## EVIDENCE

The discovery succeeded because it changed both mechanism and outcome. Bacteria observed beside inflamed tissue offered a biological explanation that the acid-only model could not supply. Trials then showed that treating the infection sharply reduced recurrence. A theory that predicts durable recovery is harder to dismiss than an unusual microscope image.

The evidence did not show that acid was imaginary. Acid can still damage vulnerable tissue, and acid suppression remains part of clinical treatment. What changed was the level of explanation: acid was often the damaging environment, while infection was an underlying cause that could be removed.

The current evidence also preserves a second pathway. NSAIDs such as aspirin, ibuprofen and naproxen relieve pain partly by interfering with processes that also help protect the stomach lining. Risk varies with drug, dose, duration, other medicines and individual factors. An ulcer caused by NSAID exposure is not explained away by the bacterial story.

What remains uncertain in an individual case cannot be settled by symptoms alone. NIDDK lists breath, stool, biopsy and endoscopic approaches among the tools clinicians use to identify infection, ulcer and complications.

## PERSPECTIVES

The heroic version of the story has great appeal: two outsiders saw what the establishment missed, one bold experiment broke the stalemate, and antibiotics cured a disease blamed on personality. It correctly remembers that expert consensus can defend an elegant mistake. It also compresses years of pathology, culture work, trials, replication and changing practice into a single dramatic scene.

The older acid-centred view was not foolish from beginning to end. Acid suppression often relieved symptoms and allowed ulcers to heal. Its failure was to confuse control of the damaging condition with removal of the cause. A leaking roof may stop dripping when someone places a bucket beneath it; the bucket still works, but it has not repaired the roof.

The modern bacterial frame can make the opposite error. It may encourage people to treat any recurring upper-abdominal discomfort as *H. pylori* or to regard an antibiotic recipe from years ago as timeless. NIDDK notes that many symptoms overlap with other digestive conditions. The American College of Gastroenterology's 2024 update warns that rising antibiotic resistance has made some once-common empirical regimens unreliable.

The strongest account keeps all three insights: the discovery was transformative, acid still matters, and causes differ. Good medicine became more specific, not merely more bacterial.

## CONTEXT

For much of the twentieth century, ulcer care centred on reducing acid and changing the patient's habits. Stress, spicy food and a demanding temperament became woven into the popular image of the “ulcer-prone” person. The story was psychologically satisfying because it made an invisible wound appear to express a way of living.

Warren's pathology observations and Marshall's clinical work disrupted that picture. Their 1983 letter documented bacteria associated with inflamed gastric tissue. Over the following decade, culture studies and treatment trials moved the question from whether the organism could survive in the stomach to whether removing it prevented ulcers from returning.

The 1994 Therapeutics Letter captures the moment when practice was turning. It treated eradication as a route away from long-term maintenance therapy, while still distinguishing NSAID-related ulcers and non-ulcer indigestion. That distinction matters: even at the height of the paradigm shift, not every stomach symptom or ulcer belonged to one mechanism.

Three decades later, the problem has moved again. The question is no longer simply whether antibiotics work. It is which combination is likely to work against local and individual resistance, whether the patient can complete a complex course, and whether eradication is later confirmed. The 2024 ACG summary says resistance to clarithromycin and levofloxacin has risen and explicitly discourages clarithromycin-based triple therapy unless susceptibility is demonstrated.

## PEOPLE

The change altered more than textbooks. A person with recurring ulcers might once have faced repeated courses of acid suppression, restricted meals and a suspicion that worry or character was helping to produce the disease. Identifying and eradicating an infection could turn recurrence into a preventable outcome.

Yet the new explanation can carry its own burden. Someone taking an NSAID for arthritis may hear “ulcers are bacterial” and overlook a medicine-related risk. Another person may receive antibiotics, feel better and assume the infection is gone without the follow-up their clinician planned. A third may have indigestion without an ulcer at all.

This is why the useful memory is not that doctors were wrong and are now right. It is that categories became more precise. Patients gained when “ulcer” stopped being treated as one story with one personality and started being investigated as damage with a cause.

## DEEPER

Medical breakthroughs are often remembered as replacements: bacteria instead of stress, antibiotics instead of antacids, new truth instead of old error. Real progress is more untidy. A powerful discovery usually changes the map while leaving some old roads in service.

The ulcer story offers a practical test for any confident health explanation. Ask four things. What mechanism does it propose? What direct evidence shows that mechanism in people? Does acting on it change outcomes? And where does the explanation stop working?

For an individual patient, that becomes a short conversation with a clinician: Was an ulcer actually diagnosed? Was *H. pylori* found, was NSAID exposure relevant, and how will the result be checked? Those questions do not select a treatment; they prevent several different conditions from being squeezed into one inherited story. People should not stop prescribed aspirin, pain medicine or antibiotics on the strength of an article.

NIDDK advises prompt medical attention for signs of ulcer complications, including black or bloody stool, vomiting blood, severe persistent abdominal pain, dizziness or fainting. That is not because every stomach pain is dangerous. It is because memory should improve proportion: old myths deserve correction, and genuine warning signs deserve clarity.

The deepest correction was not “stress was wrong” or even “bacteria were right.” It was learning to separate symptom relief from cause, association from mechanism, and a memorable discovery from the work of keeping it useful as the evidence changes.

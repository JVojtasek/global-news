With biofuels it isn't enough to track the growth of mandates; the key is to tell ethanol, biodiesel, renewable diesel and SAF apart, because each type has different inputs, technology and costs, and a different ability to pass pricier feedstocks on.

A biofuel mandate is the gasoline version of compulsory vegetables: you may not love them, but once the government writes them into the recipe, the kitchen has to adapt. The quoted excerpt of the study says global biofuel production is set to rise "nearly 70% by 2030 from 2025 levels", and it gives as the reason the increase in mandatory fuel blending after the energy crisis triggered by the war with Iran. What matters isn't just the number but the reason: demand is meant to be driven not only by the market, but by policy.

The biggest mistake with reports like this is to read biofuels as a pure "green energy" story. This is more like energy security in overalls. When the oil world shakes because of the Persian Gulf, governments often aren't looking for the cheapest molecule, but for the molecule they have more domestic control over.

That is the difference between price and security of supply. Price is picking the cheaper butter at the supermarket. Security of supply is having a cow at home — pricier, noisier and occasionally it eats your garden, but you aren't waiting for a truck from halfway around the world.

A historical parallel: after the oil shocks of the 1970s, interest in domestic substitutes for petroleum fuels grew in a number of countries. The point wasn't only ecology, but less dependence on routes and regimes that could change both price and availability tomorrow. The current report fits the same logic: a blending mandate is really a political insurance policy in the tank.

An important biofuel detail: "biofuels" aren't one market but four different kitchens. Ethanol is mainly a story of corn, sugarcane and gasoline; its margin is shaped by crop prices, energy prices and also by co-products such as animal feed. Biodiesel works with vegetable oils and fats, but runs into technical blending limits and cold-weather quality issues. Renewable diesel is a more refinery-intensive "drop-in" fuel — it can be used much like ordinary diesel, but it needs suitable oils, fats, hydrogen and more expensive equipment. SAF, sustainable aviation fuel, has the strictest specifications, because an airplane isn't the place where you want to test a recipe the way you would a cake.

The counterintuitive part can therefore be put more precisely: higher volume needn't automatically mean higher profit, especially where the mandate drives everyone toward the same inputs. If corn, soybean oil, rapeseed, used cooking oil or animal fats get more expensive, part of the "great growth" can evaporate in the margin. So the winner needn't be whoever has the nicest presentation on renewability, but whoever has the right technology for the given mandate, access to cheap feedstock, logistics and capacity that can be put to use quickly.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It can help biofuel producers and processors that already have physical assets and buyers: for example Valero Energy (VLO) through its exposure to renewable diesel, Neste (NESTE.HE) in renewable fuels and SAF, Green Plains (GPRE) in ethanol, or Gevo (GEVO) in more advanced fuels. The mechanism isn't the same for all of them: with ethanol it is mainly about the gasoline pool and agricultural inputs; with renewable diesel it is about the ability to source fats and oils and process them in a more capital-intensive technology; with SAF there is additionally lengthy certification, quality and offtake by airlines.

On the feedstock side, agricultural traders and processors such as Archer-Daniels-Midland (ADM) and Bunge Global (BG) may gain, as may firms working with waste fats and oils such as Darling Ingredients (DAR). But here it isn't just about "they'll sell more." What matters is whether they have access to feedstock, storage, transport, input pretreatment and long-term contracts. In biofuels, a dirty barrel of used cooking oil can be worth more than a beautiful PowerPoint, if it is what decides whether the production line keeps running.

It can hurt firms that use the same feedstocks as a cost: food processing, animal feed and part of the chemicals industry. Meat producers such as Tyson Foods (TSN), for example, may feel pressure indirectly through feed and fats. For airlines such as United Airlines (UAL) or Delta Air Lines (DAL) the impact is mixed: more SAF can help meet climate targets, but if it is more expensive than conventional fuel and can't be fully passed on into ticket prices, it adds to cost pressure.

With reports on biofuels, don't just jot down "demand up." A better checklist: 1) Is the growth set by law or by price? 2) Is it ethanol, biodiesel, renewable diesel or SAF? 3) Where will the feedstock come from, and does it compete with food, feed or chemicals? 4) Who has it secured by contract? 5) What technology is needed and how fast can capacity be started up? 6) Can higher costs be passed on to the customer? The same word, "biofuels," hides completely different margins and risks.

"Mandatory blending" means the government orders that a certain share of bio-component must go into ordinary fuel. It is as if a bakery had to add a bit of oat flakes to every bread roll. That helps oat growers and flake suppliers, but if oats get more expensive, the baker has a problem — and part of the bill may end up with the customer. For the market this means support for biofuel volumes, pressure on feedstocks and very different impacts on stocks depending on who controls the inputs and who merely pays for the pricier recipe.

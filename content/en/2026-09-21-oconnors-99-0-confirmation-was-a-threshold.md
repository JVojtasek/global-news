## BRIEFLY

**On September 21, 1981, the US Senate confirmed Sandra Day O’Connor 99–0.** Four days later, she took the oath as the first woman to serve on the Supreme Court.

**The unanimity can make the breakthrough look inevitable.** It was not. After finishing near the top of her Stanford Law class in 1952, O’Connor struggled to obtain work as a lawyer and was offered secretarial employment instead.

**Her selection joined preparation to presidential choice.** Ronald Reagan had promised to appoint a qualified woman and announced O’Connor in July after she had built a career in Arizona’s legislature and courts.

**A first appointment changes an institution without completing equality.** No second woman joined the Court until Ruth Bader Ginsburg in 1993, nearly twelve years later.

**The useful way to study a “first” is to inspect the threshold.** Ask who had been excluded, which gate opened, what authority the person actually exercised and whether a durable path remained for successors.

## FACTS

President Ronald Reagan announced on July 7, 1981, that he intended to nominate Judge Sandra Day O’Connor of the Arizona Court of Appeals. His remarks explicitly recalled a campaign commitment to choose a highly qualified woman for a Supreme Court vacancy. The Federal Judicial Center records the formal nomination on August 19 to replace retiring Justice Potter Stewart.

The Senate Judiciary Committee’s published hearing record covers September 9, 10 and 11. On September 21, the full Senate confirmed O’Connor 99–0; Montana Senator Max Baucus was absent. Her commission was issued the next day, and she took the Supreme Court oath on September 25.

That sequence matters. The July announcement was not confirmation, and the September 21 vote was not yet the oath. Public memory often compresses an appointment into one scene. The documents show a process involving presidential selection, investigation, hearings, committee work, a Senate vote, a commission and finally entry into office.

## EVIDENCE

O’Connor’s résumé did not appear suddenly in 1981. The Federal Judicial Center lists service as an Arizona assistant attorney general, a state senator from 1969 to 1975, majority leader, a Maricopa County Superior Court judge and then an Arizona Court of Appeals judge. The O’Connor Institute says she was the first woman in the United States to lead a state senate majority.

The same biography preserves the contrast behind the triumph. O’Connor completed Stanford’s undergraduate and law degrees in six years and graduated third in a law class of 102. Yet law firms refused to hire her as a lawyer; one offered her work as a legal secretary. She instead found a route into the San Mateo County attorney’s office, initially offering to work without salary or an office.

Those details make the 99–0 vote more legible. The barrier was not a lack of women capable of judicial work. It was the long chain of hiring, sponsorship, election, appointment and recognition that determined whose capability became visible to national selectors.

## PERSPECTIVES

One reading of the vote emphasizes bipartisan recognition. Reagan urged swift bipartisan confirmation, and every participating senator ultimately voted yes. In an era when Supreme Court confirmations are often remembered through conflict, 99–0 looks like a vanished civic language.

Another reading asks what unanimity cannot prove. It does not mean that gender exclusion had already disappeared, that senators shared O’Connor’s eventual jurisprudence, or that her selection faced no opposition. Reporters questioned Reagan about objections from anti-abortion activists on the morning of his announcement. The hearing record itself is evidence that nominees are tested on issues, experience and judicial philosophy, not simply celebrated as symbols.

O’Connor also resisted being reduced to “the woman justice.” A first carries an unfair double burden: perform the office and represent everyone previously excluded from it. Yet symbolism was not imaginary. A seat held only by men since the Court began now had a woman exercising the same constitutional authority.

The 99–0 total therefore deserves neither cynicism nor nostalgia. It records a real consensus around one nominee at one moment. It cannot stand in for consensus about equality, the Court or the law.

## CONTEXT

The Court had existed for 191 years before O’Connor joined it. Reagan’s July remarks referred to the 101 justices who had preceded her as “brethren,” turning institutional habit into a visible fact. He also framed the choice as the fulfillment of a campaign promise, reminding us that historic appointments emerge from politics as well as merit.

O’Connor’s path joined several kinds of experience that are often narrated separately. She had legislated, led a party caucus, worked in a state attorney general’s office and judged cases in both trial and appellate courts. The O’Connor Institute notes that she was the first appointee in 24 years with state-court experience and the first in 32 years with legislative experience. Her difference was not only gender.

Nor did one appointment instantly create a pipeline. The Federal Judicial Center dates Ruth Bader Ginsburg’s Supreme Court confirmation to August 3, 1993, and her oath to August 10—almost twelve years after O’Connor. For that interval, the first woman was also the only woman.

That lag is common in histories of institutional inclusion. A breakthrough changes what can be imagined and cited as precedent. Durable change also requires recruitment, credentials, fair evaluation, repeat appointments and the ordinary expectation that no single person must justify an entire group’s presence.

## DEEPER

### How to read a historic first

Start before the ceremonial photograph. What formal rule or informal custom kept the role homogeneous? In O’Connor’s case, no law barred a woman from the Court in 1980. The obstacles appeared earlier—in access to legal employment, political opportunity and the networks from which presidents assembled credible shortlists.

Next, separate the stages. Reagan announced his intended choice on July 7; the formal nomination came August 19; hearings followed in September; confirmation came September 21; the oath came September 25. A timeline prevents one actor from receiving credit for a process that required several institutions, while also revealing where opposition could have stopped it.

Then examine authority, not only presence. O’Connor did not merely occupy a seat. She heard cases, joined or shaped majorities and wrote opinions for almost a quarter-century. Representation matters partly because officeholders exercise power, not because they complete a picture.

Finally, look for successors. How long until the second person from the excluded group arrived? Did selection practices widen? Did the first have room to disagree with those who followed? Ginsburg’s 1993 appointment answered the narrowest succession question, but no single date can answer the larger one.

This four-part audit—barrier, process, authority, succession—works beyond courts. It helps distinguish a genuine institutional threshold from a publicity gesture, and a breakthrough from a finished transformation. O’Connor’s confirmation deserves remembrance precisely because it changed the possible. Its incompleteness is not an argument against celebrating it. It is the reason to remember what the vote began.

## REFLECT

When you encounter a historic “first,” do you remember the person, the selector or the institution that finally admitted them? Which earlier barrier is missing from the familiar story? And who came second—soon enough to turn exception into expectation?

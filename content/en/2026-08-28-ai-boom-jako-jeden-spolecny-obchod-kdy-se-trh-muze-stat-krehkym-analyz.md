With the AI boom it is not enough to look only at tech valuations; the key is to check in the data whether different sectors are turning into one shared trade that depends on the same assumptions.

Picture the stock market as a block of flats where every apartment thinks it has its own heating — and then discovers that they all hang off one boiler called AI.

In an interview, David Rosenberg of Rosenberg Research warns that the current market rally may be less varied than it looks: in his view it is to a large extent one highly interconnected AI trade. Importantly, according to the excerpt, the impact does not stay with tech stocks but seeps into industrials, utilities, financial services, corporate investment, rates and the wider economy.

The most interesting part of Rosenberg's warning is not the line “AI is expensive”. We hear that often. More important is the idea that AI is turning from an investment theme into a macro lever — a force that can move not only tech valuations but also capital spending, electricity demand, lending and rate expectations.

That is the difference between a fashionable hit and a master switch. A fashionable hit is something like 3D printing in 2013: excitement, a few stars, plenty of stories. A master switch is something else: when it flips, the lights go off or on even in rooms that thought they had nothing to do with it.

So Rosenberg's framework is not just about whether NVIDIA earns more on chips. The question is whether the market has started pricing different sectors on one shared premise: AI investment will keep going, companies will keep spending on infrastructure, more energy will be needed, financing will stay available and the economy will be able to carry it. If many stories are plugged into one socket, diversification may look better in a presentation than in a sharp sell-off.

That is the counterintuitive point: an investor can hold tech, industrials, utilities and banks — and still in fact have one dominant theme in the portfolio. Not because of the sector names, but because of a shared sensitivity to the same story.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

According to the excerpt provided, Rosenberg talks about spillover beyond tech; the companies below are therefore an illustrative mapping of possible channels, not a claim that the interview documents a specific impact on individual stocks.

The first layer is the direct beneficiaries of AI infrastructure: chipmakers and the semiconductor ecosystem such as NVIDIA (NVDA), Advanced Micro Devices (AMD) or Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSM). Here the link is shortest: more computing capacity usually means more interest in accelerators, chip design and manufacturing. Even here, though, the exposure is not the same — NVIDIA is close to the finished AI accelerators, AMD is a competitor in chips, and TSM is a manufacturing hub through which demand from many customers flows.

The second layer is the hyperscalers, the giant cloud operators such as Microsoft (MSFT), Amazon (AMZN) and Alphabet (GOOGL). For them AI cuts both ways: the market is watching whether heavy capital spending on data centres will later really bring in enough revenue. They are not just “AI winners” but also the ones paying a big share of the bill for wiring the electricity through the digital block of flats.

The third layer is indirect exposure: industrials and electrical infrastructure. Companies such as Eaton (ETN), Schneider Electric (SU.PA) or Vertiv (VRT) are often cited as examples of links to electrification, cooling and data-centre equipment. Utilities such as Southern Company (SO), Duke Energy (DUK) or NextEra Energy (NEE) can be associated with rising electricity demand, but this is often more of a market story with a long road to profits: regulation, investment costs and interest rates can decide how much of the higher demand ends up with shareholders.

The financial sector, for example JPMorgan Chase (JPM) or Bank of America (BAC), enters the picture most indirectly. If AI capital spending supports lending and economic activity, that is a different scenario from one in which higher rates and more expensive financing start to drag on project returns. This is not about an “AI company” but about how sensitive banks are to the credit cycle around an investment boom.

With news like this, it is dangerous to ask only: “Is AI a bubble?” A better framework is: how many different sectors today depend on the same set of assumptions — and can that be checked in the data, not just by feel?

A useful checklist, no magic involved:

- Is the market rising broadly, or mainly stocks linked to AI infrastructure? In practice you can track market breadth: how many stocks are rising along with the index, how many are above their moving averages, and whether the rally is being pulled by only a narrow group of the largest companies.

- Are correlations between sectors increasing? If tech, industrials, utilities and financial stocks start moving in the same direction on bad days, sector diversification may be thinner than it looks in a spreadsheet.

- Are the hyperscalers turning capital spending into revenue, or so far mainly laying expensive foundations? For Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet and others it is useful to compare the growth of capex, that is, investment in long-term assets, with the growth of revenue from cloud and AI services.

- Are two-year and ten-year bond yields behaving as if the market expects a longer period of higher rates? The two-year yield reflects monetary policy expectations more, the ten-year also longer-term inflation and growth worries. When financing gets more expensive, long AI projects have less room for error.

- On bad days, do different sectors fall together? That suggests that instead of ten independent engines, the portfolio may in fact be running on one central cable.

“Correlation” means that different things start moving in the same direction. It is like a restaurant where everyone ordered a different dish, but one waiter is carrying them all — if that waiter trips, everyone has a problem. For the market this means that even stocks from different sectors can fall at once if they are tied to the same AI story. For an ordinary person the impact is simple: a pension fund or ETF may look spread out, but in a nervous phase it may behave less spread out than expected.

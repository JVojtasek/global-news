## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Economists Natalia Emanuel, Emma Harrington and Amanda Pallais analyzed five nationally representative U.S. surveys covering 588,322 workers from 2011 to 2024, excluding 2020 and 2021. Their study, published in *Science* on June 4, linked the expansion of remote work to more days spent entirely alone and worse measures of mental well-being.

**What it means.** Work was doing more social work than many people noticed. Removing the commute and office also removed weak ties, spontaneous exchanges and the simple fact of being among other people.

**Risks and impact.** The estimated effects were concentrated among people living alone. Yet the study does not prove that every remote schedule harms every person, nor that a five-day office mandate repairs loneliness.

**What can be done.** Treat social contact as infrastructure. Track contact-poor days, create recurring in-person anchors and make hybrid days worth the trip instead of merely relocating video calls.

**What to watch.** Employers should measure retention, performance and well-being separately. Workers should notice whether flexibility is widening life or quietly shrinking it.

## FACTS

The [*Science* study](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aec7671) asked a question that productivity debates often leave out: what happens to life when work moves home? Its authors combined five large U.S. surveys and compared changes among people in occupations that could readily be done remotely with changes among people in occupations that could not. The sample covered 588,322 workers from 2011 through 2024; the peak-pandemic years 2020 and 2021 were excluded to reduce the distortion caused by lockdowns.

The result was not simply “remote workers report being lonely.” As remote work expanded, workers in more remotely feasible occupations became more likely to spend an entire day alone or have no social contact. Indicators of sadness and depression also worsened, and use of mental-health services and prescriptions rose. According to the authors’ estimates, remote work explained roughly one-third of the increase in isolation and mental distress between 2011–2019 and 2022–2024.

The burden was not evenly distributed. It was strongest among people who lived alone. They also did not appear to compensate reliably by seeing friends after work. That is the hinge of the finding: saved commuting time does not automatically turn into connection.

## EVIDENCE

The study has unusual scale and a stronger design than a one-time opinion poll. Rather than comparing people who freely chose remote work with people who did not, the researchers used the uneven expansion of remote work across occupations. That difference-in-differences strategy helps separate the effect of remote feasibility from broad changes affecting everyone. The authors also examined several outcomes across several surveys, not a single loneliness question.

Still, this is not a randomized assignment of individual workers to home or office. Occupations differ in many ways, and a statistical design cannot remove every hidden difference. The estimates describe average U.S. effects during a historically unusual transition. They do not tell us that a particular person became depressed because of a laptop at home. Nor can they settle every distinction between fully remote and hybrid schedules.

A separate [randomized trial published in *Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07500-2) shows why that distinction matters. At Trip.com, 1,612 employees were assigned either two work-from-home days per week or full-time office attendance. Hybrid work reduced quitting by about one-third and improved satisfaction without damaging measured performance. One study identifies a social cost of a broad shift; the other shows that flexibility can be designed without the feared productivity collapse.

## PERSPECTIVES

Workers are not irrational for liking remote work. In a [Pew Research Center survey](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/01/13/many-remote-workers-say-theyd-be-likely-to-leave-their-job-if-they-could-no-longer-work-from-home/) conducted in October 2024, 75% of U.S. adults with remote-capable jobs worked from home at least sometimes. Among non-self-employed workers, 46% said they would be unlikely to stay if that option disappeared. But hybrid workers did not mostly demand permanent solitude: 72% said they would choose a hybrid arrangement, while 24% preferred full-time remote work.

Employers see a different ledger. Office space, coordination, mentoring, culture and control all carry costs. Some managers interpret the new mental-health evidence as a reason to restore attendance mandates. That response may help some people, particularly new employees or workers whose homes are isolating. It can also replace one blunt default with another. A crowded commute and a day of headphones in an open-plan office are not necessarily meaningful contact.

The sharper question is not “home or office?” It is “which arrangement produces useful work and a livable week for this team and this person?” A parent may gain family time at home. A person with a disability may gain access. Someone living alone in a new city may lose nearly every casual encounter. Policy that ignores those differences will misread both the evidence and the people.

## CONTEXT

The office is an economic institution, but it is also a social one. It supplies what sociologists call weak ties: the colleague in another department, the familiar face at the café, the short conversation before a meeting. These contacts rarely look profound. Collectively, however, they create recognition, information and a sense of belonging to a shared world.

Remote work makes those interactions optional. That is part of its freedom and part of its risk. Scheduled video meetings preserve strong task connections while removing the low-stakes edges around them. A calendar can stay full even as a day becomes socially empty. The [MIT Blueprint Labs summary](https://blueprintlabs.mit.edu/research/home-alone-remote-work-isolation-and-mental-health/) notes that remote work quadrupled in the half-decade after the pandemic began; human routines did not necessarily adapt at the same speed.

There is also a time-horizon problem. The benefits of remote work are immediate: no commute, fewer interruptions, more control over the day. The costs can be cumulative. A friendship at work does not vanish on the first home-office day. Mentoring does not fail in a week. A network frays quietly, which makes it easy to attribute later isolation to personality or bad luck.

That asymmetry helps explain why preference data and well-being data can both be true. People may reasonably choose a benefit they feel today while underestimating a cost that emerges over months. The answer is not to confiscate the benefit. It is to make the delayed cost visible enough to manage.

## PEOPLE

Picture two workers closing the same laptop. One steps into a kitchen where a partner and children are already talking. The other looks up at a silent flat. Both avoided the same commute; they did not arrive at the same evening.

That difference is why “remote worker” is too coarse a category. Living arrangement, neighborhood, disability, caregiving, seniority and local friendships change what home working means. New hires may need observation and informal questions. Experienced specialists may need long, uninterrupted blocks. People who live alone may value flexibility and still need more deliberate contact than colleagues with busy households.

The current reporting correctly highlights the group result, but the humane use of the result begins with variation. A manager should not diagnose an employee from an attendance pattern. A worker should not treat loneliness as evidence of personal failure. Environment shapes the number of chances a person gets to connect.

## DEEPER

A practical response starts with a two-week social-day audit. At the end of each workday, record three things: whether you had a live conversation that was not purely transactional, whether you left home, and whether you spent time with someone by choice. Do not score mood or productivity yet. First make the pattern visible.

If several days are empty, add one recurring anchor rather than a heroic social overhaul. It might be a shared office day with a real lunch, a morning walk with a neighbor, a class, a volunteer shift or a weekly call that is protected like a meeting. People living alone should be especially wary of assuming that free evenings will organize themselves.

Teams can do a parallel audit. Ask what office days are for. If employees travel only to join separate video calls, attendance creates friction without connection. Concentrate collaboration, mentoring and informal time on shared days. Pair new colleagues with named mentors. Reward the quiet work of introducing people and maintaining networks.

Finally, separate three outcomes: performance, retention and well-being. A schedule can succeed on one and fail on another. The *Science* study does not command a return to 2019. It reveals a design debt. Remote work removed accidental contact faster than people and institutions replaced it. Flexibility remains valuable, but it works best when connection is treated as a requirement rather than a pleasant side effect.

## REFLECT

When you save time by not commuting, where does that time actually go?

Which contact in your week is recurring enough to survive a busy month?

If your team meets in person, does the day create relationships—or merely move the same screen work to another building?

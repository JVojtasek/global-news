In energy it isn't enough to read the commodity price; with LNG, what also matters is inventory levels, the price gap between regions, ship availability, the length of an outage and whether higher margins actually turn into cash.

Oil is behaving like a pair of saloon doors right now: every diplomatic headline can slam them hard. According to the report, Saudi exports are recovering, talks with Iran are easing fears of oil supply disruptions, and both WTI and Brent are bouncing higher. Gas is a different story, though: disrupted LNG flows from Qatar are keeping the global market tight.

The biggest mistake is to throw oil and gas into one sack labelled "energy." Oil is a global liquid pool: a barrel from Saudi Arabia, the US or Brazil can be redirected relatively quickly, at varying premiums. LNG is more like an upscale restaurant with a limited number of tables: you need a liquefaction terminal, a ship, a receiving terminal and long-term contracts. When one link creaks, the queue forms not just at the door but in prices too.

That is why with LNG it isn't enough to know that "Qatar has an outage." A small dashboard matters more: European gas inventories, the spread between the European TTF price and the Asian JKM price, LNG shipping rates, how much liquefaction capacity is offline, the length of the outage, seasonal demand and the share of spot contracts. In plain English: when Asia is willing to pay more than Europe and ships are expensive or unavailable at the same time, gas molecules aren't sentimental — they sail toward the better table in the restaurant.

That is why a report can look reassuring for oil and tense for gas at the same time. Within QMA's five pillars this isn't only about supply. With oil, geopolitics and crowd positioning are at work too: when the market holds a fear premium and diplomacy shrinks it, the price can move sharply even without a fundamental change in physical consumption. With LNG, infrastructure and seasonality matter more: ahead of winter, every delay carries more weight than in a quiet spring month.

A mini-model without invented market data: imagine a producer with revenue of 100, EBITDA of 25, capex of 12 and working capital of 3. Free cash flow is, simplified, 25 - 12 - 3 = 10. If a higher commodity price lifts EBITDA to 30, but the company has to hold working capital of 9 because of inventories and receivables, and capex jumps to 16, FCF is only 5. The point: a beautiful EBITDA can be like a full fridge in someone else's flat — it looks good, but you can't eat from it until it turns into cash.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The upside for oil goes to producers with direct exposure to realized prices, such as Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX) or ConocoPhillips (COP). For them the difference between a price rise driven by demand and one driven by supply fears matters: the first tends to be healthier for volumes, the second more fragile for valuations.

Service firms like SLB (SLB) and Halliburton (HAL) benefit more when higher prices persuade producers to keep their drilling budgets. You can't tell that from an oil headline, only from orders, margins and rig utilization.

LNG and global gas help companies with liquefaction capacity or contracts, such as Cheniere Energy (LNG), Shell (SHEL) or TotalEnergies (TTE). Shipping players like Flex LNG (FLNG) or Golar LNG (GLNG) have a different profile, where shipping rates and vessel availability are decisive.

On the other side stands energy-intensive industry: chemicals, fertilizers and part of European industry, for example CF Industries (CF) or BASF (BAS.DE). Airlines and transport, such as Delta Air Lines (DAL) or FedEx (FDX), usually aren't pleased by pricier oil. Refiners like Valero (VLO) and Marathon Petroleum (MPC) are a mixed case: what matters isn't oil itself but the crack spread, that is, the difference between the price of fuels and the price of the crude that goes in.

With reports like this it helps to separate three questions: is the price move driven by physical shortage, a geopolitical premium or demand? Does the company have contracts, regulated returns, or is it floating on the spot market? And does higher EBITDA flow through to cash, or does capex and working capital eat it up?

With LNG it makes sense to add an "airport departures board" too: European inventories show the cushion, the TTF/JKM spread shows where gas may sail, shipping rates show the cost of transport, and offline capacity and the length of the outage tell you whether this is a scratch in the paint or an engine failure. Spot contracts then determine who feels a price change immediately and who only when contracts are repriced.

A warning sign: a company reports better operating profit, but operating cash flow stagnates or falls. That often means money is stuck in inventories, receivables or more expensive projects.

Think of LNG as gas in a thermos on a ship. Having gas underground isn't enough; you have to freeze it, load it, ship it and warm it up again. When one big kitchen appliance in Qatar jams, it isn't like switching milk brands at the supermarket. For the market, that means more nervousness in gas prices; for stocks, wider gaps between winners and losers; and for ordinary people, potential pressure on energy bills or on the prices of products that consume a lot of energy.

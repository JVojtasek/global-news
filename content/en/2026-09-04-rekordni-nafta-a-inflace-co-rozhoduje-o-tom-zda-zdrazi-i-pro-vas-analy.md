With record diesel prices, do not watch only the price of oil: the key is whether the problem lies in refineries, middle distillate stocks and companies' ability to pass fuel costs on.

Diesel is the economy's accounts department in liquid form: when it gets more expensive, the bill shows up in almost everything someone had to transport, harvest or make. According to the supplied report id=357310, US truckers are paying an average of USD 5.85 per gallon of diesel, against USD 3.71 a year ago, almost 60 % more; in California the report gives USD 7.70 per gallon. The report's headline attributes the pressure, among other things, to refinery outages linked to the wars in Ukraine and Iran.

With oil, people watch a big dramatic thermometer called the barrel. But diesel is more like the thermometer in a building's boiler room: you cannot see it from the street, but when it shoots up, everyone suddenly gets a higher bill.

The counterintuitive point: the inflation problem need not start with the price of crude oil, but only in the "kitchen" where oil is cooked into usable products — in refineries. When refining capacity drops out, the market may not be short of oil as such, but of a specific product: diesel. That is the difference between "we have flour" and "the bakery has a broken oven". Flour in the warehouse will not satisfy hunger if there is no one to bake the bread.

For a better trader, an important framework applies here: with news like this, it is not enough to look at oil. You also need to watch refining margin indicators, often called the crack spread — simply put, the difference between the price of oil and the price of the fuels refineries make from it. When diesel rises faster than oil, refinery margins can fatten while truckers and retailers slim down under costs. The second indicator is middle distillate stocks, the group of fuels that includes diesel and heating oil. Low stocks turn the market into a dry forest: a smaller spark in the form of a refinery outage or a geopolitical event is enough, and prices react more sharply.

The historical parallel is not "oil expensive = everything bad". More precisely: the product that moves logistics is expensive = companies with thin margins start recalculating their price lists. And that is more insidious for inflation than a pricier car trip, because diesel is hidden in the price of milk, cement and a parcel delivered to the door.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It potentially helps refineries and integrated energy companies, if their operations are running and they can sell pricier products. Educational examples, not a recommendation to buy/sell nor a promise of returns: refiners such as Valero Energy (VLO), Marathon Petroleum (MPC) or Phillips 66 (PSX) may benefit from higher refining margins if input crude does not rise as fast as finished fuels.

Transport and logistics, by contrast, feel the pressure: freight carriers such as J.B. Hunt (JBHT) or Old Dominion Freight Line (ODFL), delivery companies such as FedEx (FDX) and UPS (UPS), railways such as Union Pacific (UNP) or CSX (CSX), because diesel is a direct operating cost for them. They can pass part of the costs on through fuel surcharges, but not always right away and not always in full.

Indirectly, it hurts retail and consumer companies, for example Walmart (WMT), Target (TGT) or food producers, because more expensive transport raises the cost of supply. From the market's perspective, this can be mixed: energy up, margins of carriers and part of the consumer sector under pressure, rate-sensitive stocks more nervous over fears of cost inflation.

With expensive diesel, beware of the "deceptively simple" explanation via the oil price. Checklist: 1) is diesel rising faster than oil, 2) are middle distillate stocks worsening, 3) are refining margins widening, 4) are companies talking about fuel surcharges and margin pressure in their results, 5) are central banks starting to see cost inflation as a more lasting risk. One data event is not a verdict; what matters is the combination of price, stocks, margins and company comments.

Think of the "crack spread" as the difference between the price of potatoes and the price of chips. When potatoes cost about the same but chips suddenly get much more expensive, the fryer operator has a better margin — as long as the fryer works. With diesel, the fryer is the refinery. The impact: refinery stocks may look relatively better, while carriers, shops and ordinary people pay more through pricier transport of goods. The wallet often feels it not just at the pump, but in the price of things that have never seen a pump.

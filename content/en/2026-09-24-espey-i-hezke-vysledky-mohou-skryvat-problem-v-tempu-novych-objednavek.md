For backlog-driven companies, compare new orders with revenue: when book-to-bill falls below 1, a company can report strong results while at the same time consuming its stock of future work faster than it replenishes it.

Sometimes a company looks great at precisely the moment the market starts checking its pantry.

Espey Mfg. & Electronics (ESP) showed strong growth in Q4: revenue rose 40.9% year over year, margins were a record, and the company was helped by a large backlog, meaning a stock of orders already won. At the same time, however, new orders fell to $41.4 million from $86.4 million, and while the result beat the analysis author's expectations, it fell short of consensus. Management talks about revenue growth in FY2027 and expects new orders to stabilize; the key question will be whether the backlog gets refilled.

This is not the classic "good results, bad reaction" story. For companies like Espey, what matters more is the difference between two engines: backlog conversion and the creation of new backlog. The first says: "we can deliver, invoice and earn a margin". The second says: "more work is coming in that will feed the years ahead".

The market often punishes a stock when the numbers miss consensus. But for small industrial suppliers to defense and electronic infrastructure, short-term consensus may matter less than the so-called backlog waterfall: opening backlog + new orders − revenue = closing backlog. Here it is no longer just a nice concept: with an opening backlog of roughly $124 million, new orders of $41.4 million and revenue of roughly $54 million over the same period, the closing backlog comes out around $111 million. In other words: the company used up part of its stock of work faster than new orders replenished it.

An even sharper view comes from book-to-bill, that is, new orders divided by revenue. For Espey it comes out at approximately 0.77×: $41.4 million of new orders / roughly $54 million of revenue. A value below 1 means that new orders did not cover the reported revenue. If revenue grows quickly but new orders slow down, the company can look like a restaurant with a full dining room and an emptying fridge. The evening is excellent. The question is what will be cooking tomorrow.

I would weigh the QMA framework here as follows: 30% new orders and backlog, 25% margin quality, 20% balance sheet, 15% ability to convert backlog into revenue without delays, 10% market expectations and valuation. The strongest part of the report is margin discipline and revenue growth. The most sensitive part is order renewal. The drop in new orders from $86.4 million to $41.4 million is not a footnote detail; it is the item that decides whether record margins look like the start of a new level, or like the perfectly managed drawdown of older contracts.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It helps the image of smaller industrial and defense-electronics companies that have long production cycles, technically specialized products and a strong balance sheet. Examples of the impact are manufacturers and suppliers of aerospace, defense and power electronics such as Espey Mfg. & Electronics (ESP), Ducommun (DCO), Curtiss-Wright (CW), HEICO (HEI) or TransDigm (TDG) — not as identical companies, but as a broader basket of firms where investors watch backlog, margins and the ability to deliver on orders.

Conversely, it hurts companies that report revenue growth mainly thanks to an old backlog but lack a sufficient inflow of new orders. There the key thing to watch is the book-to-bill ratio: above 1 means new orders exceed revenue, below 1 means the company is drawing down its stock of work. Also important are gross and operating margin, conversion of orders into cash, debt maturity and customer concentration. If customers are few and orders arrive in waves, one weaker quarter can change the outlook faster than at a large, diversified industrial conglomerate.

With reports like these, reading revenue and margin is not enough. A better checklist is: 1) is the backlog growing, or just being used up? 2) what is the ratio of new orders to revenue, and is it above or below 1? 3) does the change in backlog match the equation opening backlog + new orders − revenue? 4) are margins rising thanks to efficiency, or just thanks to a mix of one-off favorable contracts? 5) does the balance sheet leave room for contract delays? 6) is management adjusting its outlook for the coming years, or only commenting on one quarter?

The biggest risk is mistaking "the company fulfills old orders well" for "the company has the next cycle automatically won". At Espey the operating side looks stronger than the reaction to Q4 alone might suggest, but the long-term story rests mainly on whether new orders stabilize and refill the stock of work.

Book-to-bill is like comparing how many new weddings a pastry chef has booked in the diary against how many cakes he has just baked and invoiced. When the ratio is below 1, he is baking faster than new weddings are coming in. For Espey, with orders of $41.4 million and revenue of roughly $54 million, that works out to about 0.77×, so new contracts currently did not cover the work consumed. For the market this means: strong results today are fine, but the stock often hinges mainly on whether there will be something to earn from next time too. For an ordinary person the impact is indirect — such companies show how healthily industrial and defense orders are running, which can feed through into jobs, supply chains and the mood in smaller industrial stocks.

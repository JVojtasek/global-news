At Kharg, what decides is not the explosive headline itself, but verifiable traces in shipping: loadings, tanker draught, insurance, rates and any rerouting of exports.

When the oil market hears the name Kharg, it is like an insurer hearing the word "flood": nervousness gets more expensive first, and only then are the actual damages counted. According to U.S. Central Command, US forces on Saturday struck three Iranian oil tankers near Kharg Island, Iran's key export hub in the Persian Gulf, after missiles were aimed at US Navy ships.

The most important question is not whether the news is "bullish for oil". That is the first layer. The smarter question is: did it hit only the oil story, or the oil cash register?

Kharg is not just a dot on the map. It has historically been Iran's main maritime export terminal; in normal operation, it is often cited as handling a majority, up to roughly ninety percent, of Iran's seaborne oil exports, depending on the period, sanctions and the method of transshipment. That is the difference between an incident at a petrol station and an incident at a supermarket's main checkout. When something happens at the checkout, the bread rolls do not run out right away — but everyone starts watching the queue.

Here is a better framework than the usual "fear versus outage": Kharg is a place where geopolitics can be translated into observable traces. Not in statements, but in the behaviour of ships. With oil, every panic eventually has to pass through a hose, a berth, a tanker's draught and an insurance invoice.

Alternatives exist, but they are no magic teleport. Iran can use ship-to-ship transfers, floating storage, other terminals including Jask on the Gulf of Oman, or less transparent routes via a "darker" tanker fleet. But what decides is capacity, speed and the willingness of insurers and carriers to bear the risk. When a restaurant's main entrance is closed, guests can use the back door. But if the back door is narrow, the cook is nervous and the bouncer wants a surcharge, lunches will be late.

In practice, then, watch not just the Brent price, but three layers: AIS/satellite behaviour of tankers around Kharg, meaning whether ships actually depart, change draught and disappear from berths; insurance and tanker rates, because that is where fear turns into cost; and regional price differences, because physical shortages often show up in spreads before they show up in headlines.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The plus side is cleanest for oil producers and integrated energy companies, if the risk premium holds: companies such as Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), Shell (SHEL) or BP (BP) have revenues sensitive to the oil price. The distinguishing criterion: how much of their production is tied to the market price, what hedging they have, and whether higher prices are not outweighed by higher taxes, regulation or operating costs.

Tanker companies such as Frontline (FRO), Teekay Tankers (TNK) or International Seaways (INSW) may be worth watching if freight rates and war-risk insurance rise. Careful: it is not an automatic win. When risk closes a route or reduces shipping volumes, a higher rate per ship can run into fewer contracts. Measurable indicators: tanker day rates, insurance premiums, waiting times at terminals, the number of barrels actually loaded and changes in tanker draught after leaving Kharg.

The minus side: airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL), United Airlines (UAL) and American Airlines (AAL), because fuel is a large cost item and passing it on into ticket prices has limits. Companies with low pricing power, high inventories in transit and long supply routes tend to be in a weaker position. Chemicals and industry, for example Dow (DOW) or the European BASF (BASFY), may feel pressure through more expensive inputs and energy.

LPG shipping and liquefied gases are a "watch" area: companies such as Dorian LPG (LPG) or BW LPG (BWLP.OL) may react to changes in routes, insurance and regional price differences, but the impact depends on specific contracts, ship availability and whether the problem spills over from oil into wider operations in the Persian Gulf.

With news like this, follow a checklist, not adrenaline. First: is an export hub hit, or only individual ships? Second: are physical loading volumes changing, or just the price of risk? Third: are insurance premiums and tanker rates rising? Fourth: are the spreads between oil benchmarks widening, for example Brent and WTI? Fifth: do AIS and satellite data confirm fewer departures, longer waits, transfers away from the terminal or unusual clusters of tankers at Kharg? Sixth: is there a diplomatic brake, or is the incident chaining into further actions?

The negative scenario for oil bulls: the investigation and retaliation remain a political and security episode without a lasting restriction on exports. Then, after the initial shock, the market may return to the usual variables: inventories, demand, producer decisions and the dollar exchange rate.

AIS and satellite tracking of tankers is like checking whether couriers are actually leaving the pizzeria, rather than just hearing that "we have lots of orders". When tankers keep arriving at Kharg, loading and leaving, the market can conclude that the kitchen is still cooking. But when ships wait, vanish from usual routes, change transfers or do not leave full, it may mean less oil on the way — and that can eventually feed into more expensive fuel, air tickets and goods transport.

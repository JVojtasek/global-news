In geopolitical oil shocks, compare Brent M1–M2 and M1–M6, VLCC/Suezmax/LR2 tanker rates and crack spreads; only their joint movement will show whether this is a short panic or a more expensive logistical reality.

When oil gets a fright, it is often not because of missing barrels, but because of the shipping bill for fear.

The US said it struck Iranian oil tankers in the Gulf of Oman and near Iran's Kharg Island. According to the American side, these tankers were supposed to help finance Iran's regional proxy groups.

What the market takes away from this is not just the question "how much oil has physically disappeared", but above all "how expensive will it be to get oil safely out through a sensitive region".

This news is not just another chapter about Hormuz. What is more interesting is that the market may start pricing something less visible: not passage through the strait, but the "clean story" of each barrel. When a military strike, sanctions risk and the question of financing proxy groups get mixed into the oil trade, a barrel is no longer just a barrel. It is a barrel with a CV — and some buyers suddenly do not want to explain why they invited home a guest with a suspicious passport.

That is a different mechanism from the classic fear of a physical shortage. The oil may still exist, the tankers may still sail, but some traders, insurers, banks and ports start tightening their checks. The result? Longer paperwork, more expensive insurance, more cautious cargo financing and a bigger discount on oil that carries political risk. It is not only shipping that gets more expensive. So does the proof that you were allowed to touch the oil at all.

It is like a used car lot. Two cars look the same, have the same engine and similar paintwork. Only one has a service book, a clear origin and a boring history, while the other has three screwdrivers in the boot, seven previous owners, and the seller goes by "a mate of a friend". Technically, both will get you there. But the financing, insurance and willingness to take risk will be completely different.

That is why the first moves in oil can be misleading. If it is just a short geopolitical outcry, the market often exhales quickly. But if the behaviour of insurers, shipowners and refineries starts to change, the impact moves from the headline to margins — and there it tends to be more stubborn.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The positive side of the impact may lie with oil and gas producers more sensitive to the oil price, for example Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), Shell (SHEL) or BP (BP). The difference, though, is how much of their production is hedged forward and what their regional costs are. A company that has sold part of its future production at fixed prices does not feel a market rise as quickly as a producer with larger open exposure.

Tanker companies such as Frontline (FRO), Teekay Tankers (TNK) or International Seaways (INSW) are not one pile of ships with one reaction. Tension may help most those who have ships available on the spot market, meaning for immediate charters. Ships tied up in long-term contracts, by contrast, may have more stable income but less chance to capture a jump in rates. And even for the winners, part of the higher rates can be eaten up by war-risk insurance, waiting in ports or longer routes.

Refiners such as Valero Energy (VLO), Marathon Petroleum (MPC) or Phillips 66 (PSX) face a mixed impact. It is not just that oil gets more expensive. What matters is how crack spreads move — the difference between the price of crude and the prices of the products refineries make from it, such as petrol, diesel or jet fuel. Refineries with the flexibility to process different types of crude may be more resilient than those more dependent on a specific type of imported feedstock.

It may hit airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL), United Airlines (UAL), American Airlines (AAL) or Southwest Airlines (LUV), because fuel is one of their sensitive costs. The impact, though, depends on fuel hedging and the ability to pass higher costs on into ticket prices. Chemical companies such as Dow (DOW), meanwhile, have to consider whether oil and naphtha are getting more expensive, or whether they have the advantage of cheaper gas inputs against competitors tied more to oil-based chemistry.

With news like this, do not watch only the Brent price on the front screen. Compare four things: first, the spread between the nearest and second futures contract, and also between the nearest and sixth month — steeper backwardation, meaning more expensive prompt oil versus future oil, shows tension here and now. Second, watch tanker rates for VLCCs on routes from the Middle East to Asia, Suezmax rates and LR2-type product tankers; if oil rises but ships do not, it may be more a short scare than a logistical change. Third, check the diesel crack, the jet crack and the 3-2-1 crack spread; oil may rise, but what matters for refineries is whether fuels rise even faster. Fourth, wait for the behaviour after the first two to three trading days: a one-off jump is emotion, a more lasting change in the curve, rates and margins is the bill for a new reality.

Backwardation is when oil for immediate delivery is more expensive than oil for later delivery. Imagine the neighbours are having a barbecue tonight and the shop has the last bags of charcoal left: a bag for today costs more than a bag delivered in a month. For the market, this means people pay a premium for availability right now. For shares, this can help producers and put pressure on fuel-dependent companies. For ordinary people, such tension can eventually show up in the prices of petrol, diesel or air tickets — not always right away, but through costs that gradually pass down the line.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Beginning in 1843, English botanist Anna Atkins issued *Photographs of British Algae: Cyanotype Impressions*, placing dried seaweed directly onto sensitised paper and exposing it to light.

**What it means.** The work is widely recognised by museums as the first book illustrated with photographs. It joined a new image-making process to the older disciplines of collecting, classifying and publishing nature.

**Risks and impact.** A photogram can preserve an outline and fine structures while losing colour, thickness and habitat. The image looks immediate, but it is still a designed record.

**What can be done.** Treat scientific images as evidence with a method. Keep the specimen name, process, date and limits beside the picture.

**What to watch.** Claims that identify a single “first” in photography can depend on definitions. Atkins' achievement is secure without pretending that every question about priority is settled.

## FACTS

Anna Atkins was born in 1799 and learned botany in a household closely connected to natural history. Her father, John George Children, worked at the British Museum. According to the Getty Museum, she supplied hundreds of drawings for his translation of Jean-Baptiste Lamarck's work on shells.

Photography was barely established when Atkins adopted it. John Herschel announced the cyanotype process in 1842. Paper coated with iron compounds changes under ultraviolet light; after exposure and washing, the exposed areas become blue while objects placed on the sheet leave pale silhouettes.

Atkins used that process for algae. She arranged a dried specimen on sensitised paper, exposed it and washed the sheet. The resulting photogram recorded the plant's form without a camera or lens. She then added a handwritten scientific name.

The Natural History Museum and the Metropolitan Museum describe *Photographs of British Algae* as the first photographically illustrated book. Atkins began distributing it privately in instalments in 1843. The Met notes that she produced several hundred images and assembled copies by hand over roughly a decade. Copies vary because this was not a mechanically identical print run.

## EVIDENCE

The surviving pages make the method visible. A pale branching plant floats against a deep blue field. Where light reached the chemically treated paper, iron compounds formed Prussian blue. Where the specimen blocked light, the paper remained light.

That contact relationship is the process's strength. The image carries a direct physical trace of the specimen's outline. Delicate fronds that would be tedious to draw can appear with striking precision. For a botanist comparing forms, that consistency had practical value.

But “direct” does not mean neutral. A thick specimen and a thin one block different amounts of light. Arrangement changes the silhouette. Exposure and washing affect contrast. The blue is a property of the process, not the colour of the living algae. Labels depend on the maker's identification.

The book therefore combines at least four kinds of work: collecting a specimen, naming it, composing the sheet and controlling a chemical exposure. Photography did not remove judgment. It moved judgment into a new sequence.

The institutional record also supports a careful distinction. Museums often identify Atkins as the first woman to publish a book illustrated with photographs and sometimes as the first female photographer. Yet early photographic practice was dispersed, experiments were not always published, and “photographer” can mean camera-maker, printer or photogram maker. Her documented book matters more than an absolute title.

## PERSPECTIVES

From the history of science, Atkins solved an information problem. Botanical classification depended on seeing small differences, while hand-drawn plates required time, skilled engraving and interpretation. A contact print offered another route from specimen to page.

From the history of art, the sheets exceed their reference function. Atkins chose how each plant would bend, overlap and occupy the blue rectangle. Negative space gives the specimens rhythm. A cataloguing tool became a visual language.

From the history of labour, the book complicates the heroic-inventor story. Herschel devised the cyanotype process. William Henry Fox Talbot demonstrated photographic books in a different form. Atkins turned a process into a sustained publishing system, and later worked with her friend Anne Dixon on other cyanotype volumes. Invention, application and circulation were separate achievements.

From the history of books, each copy is an object assembled through repetition. Every sheet needed coating, drying, arranging, exposure, washing, labelling and binding. What looks effortless was slow craft.

## CONTEXT

Nineteenth-century natural history depended on collections. Plants travelled from coasts and colonies into cabinets, herbaria and museums. Scientific authority grew through names, comparisons and reproducible descriptions.

Illustration sat at the centre of that system. A drawing could clarify an ideal form but also smooth away irregularities. A photograph promised contact with an individual object, yet early processes introduced their own distortions and were difficult to reproduce at scale.

Atkins found a productive middle ground. Her images did not show habitat, colour or three-dimensional structure. They did show contours and branching patterns with an arresting regularity. The limitation became a style.

That is why the book belongs to more than one history. It is evidence about algae, evidence about early photography and evidence about who performed the patient work that allowed a technology to become a medium.

### Practical takeaway

When an image is offered as evidence, ask four questions:

1. What physical object or event produced it?
2. Which choices were made before the image existed?
3. What can the process record well?
4. What does the process necessarily omit?

For Atkins' cyanotypes, the answers are unusually visible: a specimen touched the paper; its arrangement was deliberate; the silhouette could be precise; colour and habitat disappeared.

The same questions work for microscope slides, satellite images and phone photographs. A picture can be faithful to one property while silent about another.

## DEEPER

New technologies are often introduced as replacements. Photography would replace drawing; digital files would replace books; automated recognition would replace expert attention. Atkins' pages suggest a more interesting pattern.

The cyanotype became valuable because it was joined to existing knowledge. Atkins had to know which specimen she held, how it fitted botanical classification and how a reader might compare one page with another. The process accelerated one part of representation, but expertise still surrounded it.

Her blue books also reveal how culture decides what counts as important. Scientific illustration, domestic craft, collecting and correspondence were easier to overlook than a patent or a public institution. Work at those boundaries can disappear because no single profession claims it.

Atkins left objects that resist that disappearance. Each sheet is both a record and an argument: careful looking is not separate from making. The method is part of the meaning.

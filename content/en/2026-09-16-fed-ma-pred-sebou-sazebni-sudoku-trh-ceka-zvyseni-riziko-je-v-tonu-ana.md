When the market is already almost certain of a rate hike, the biggest risk lies not in the Fed's move itself, but in the tone of its commentary and the reaction of yields, the dollar, credit spreads and the breadth of the stock market.

When the market puts the odds of a rate hike above 90%, the Fed's decision itself is no longer the fireworks — it's the siren before the fireworks. According to the report, interest rate swaps tied to specific Fed meetings are pricing in that the FOMC will likely raise its benchmark rate by a quarter of a percentage point from the 3.5–3.75% range. Richards therefore argues that it will be "extremely hard" for the committee not to raise rates.

The most important question here isn't "will they hike or not?", but "what will the market do when it gets exactly what it ordered?"

When the probability of a hike according to market contracts is above 90%, the rate decision behaves like a mother-in-law's visit announced well in advance: the stress has already happened, the fridge is full, and the real surprise would be if she didn't show up. That's why, paradoxically, a pause could be a bigger market shock than a hike. Not because lower rates would automatically be bad, but because it would suggest the Fed sees something the market doesn't see yet — perhaps a faster cooling of the economy, weaker lending or a more fragile financial system.

That's an old lesson of rate markets: an asset's price often reacts not to the event, but to the gap between expectations and reality. If everyone expects rain and water falls, umbrellas don't get more expensive. But if the meteorologist suddenly says "maybe a tornado," a different game begins.

Through the QMA lens, this is mainly a clash of two pillars: rate macro and crowd psychology. The crowd is now packed on the "Fed will hike" side. That doesn't mean it's wrong. It means the market reaction may be smaller to the move itself and bigger to the vocabulary around it: how the Fed describes inflation, the labor market, credit conditions and the further path of rates.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

Higher rates usually push down the prices of existing bonds, because new issues carry a more attractive yield. That's why longer-dated government bonds and funds with longer duration — that is, greater sensitivity to rates — are under pressure.

Tech and semiconductors are sensitive in a different way: not through the coupon, but through the valuation of future profits. Chipmakers such as NVIDIA (NVDA), Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSM) or Advanced Micro Devices (AMD) have part of their value resting on expected growth in the years ahead. A higher discount rate makes a future hundred crowns worth fewer crowns today — like a friend promising you lunch in five years; when money is more expensive, that promised Sunday roast loses some of its charm.

For banks such as JPMorgan Chase (JPM) or Bank of America (BAC), a higher rate can help in the short term through net interest margins, but only if credit quality doesn't deteriorate and pressure on deposits doesn't rise. Insurers such as MetLife (MET) or Prudential Financial (PRU) often benefit from higher yields in their portfolios. By contrast, real estate companies and REITs such as Prologis (PLD) or American Tower (AMT) may feel pressure through more expensive financing and the higher return investors demand.

Consumer segments tied to credit — housing, cars, big-ticket purchases — find the air thinner. Examples are homebuilders Lennar (LEN) and D.R. Horton (DHI), where mortgage rates affect housing affordability.

With news like this, it's better not to measure just "hike versus pause," but four layers of reaction: 1) whether the two-year yield rises or falls after the decision, because it most sensitively reflects the expected path of rates; 2) whether the dollar strengthens, which can worsen conditions for global companies and emerging markets; 3) whether credit spreads widen, that is, the premiums on riskier bonds over safer ones; 4) whether stock gains are driven by the broad market or just a few mega-caps.

A practical filter for false calm: if the index rises after the rate hike, but most stocks in the index don't join in, volumes are weak and earnings estimates aren't being revised up, it may be more of a relief reaction than a firmer foundation for a trend.

The "discount rate" is like a discount for waiting. If someone promises you CZK 1,000 today, it's CZK 1,000. If they promise it in ten years, you want to know what that promise is worth today. The higher interest rates are, the less present value distant future cash has. That's why higher rates often hurt the stocks of companies whose big profits are only due to come later — and why it can also touch ordinary wallets through more expensive loans, mortgages and a more cautious labor market.

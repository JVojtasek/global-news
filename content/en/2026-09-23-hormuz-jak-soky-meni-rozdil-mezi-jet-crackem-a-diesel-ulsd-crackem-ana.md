With shocks around Hormuz, watch not just oil, but above all the jet crack versus the diesel/ULSD crack, regional spreads, jet fuel inventories and refinery utilization — that is where the impact on margins and transport prices is decided.

When jet fuel starts behaving like diesel in work boots, the market is whispering to us: the problem is not just oil, but who can refine it in time.

According to the news headline, jet fuel is tracking the jump in diesel prices because the supply shock around Hormuz is also hitting air traffic. The source is this excerpt: "Squeezed refineries are struggling to supply a world hungry for both diesel and jet fuel." In other words: this is not just about a more expensive barrel of crude, but about a shortage of finished products that trucks and planes actually fill up with.

The most interesting thing about this story is not Hormuz. It is a test of whether the market is dealing with a general fear about oil, or with a very specific problem in middle distillates — the part of the barrel that yields both diesel and jet fuel. The media often watch crude. But for an airline, what matters is not the poster with the oil price, but the receipt for jet fuel.

A refinery is not a magic tap. It is more like a cook who has one chicken while the guests suddenly want only drumsticks. He can adjust the portions a little, but he cannot conjure six drumsticks out of one chicken without a compromise somewhere else. If the world simultaneously wants more diesel for trucks, shipping logistics and industry, and more jet fuel for flights, a crush forms in the same part of the barrel.

But here is a detail an ordinary article often skips: it is not enough to watch the "crack spread" in general. The crack spread is the difference between the price of a finished fuel and the price of crude, a rough indicator of refining margin. For this story, what matters is comparing the jet crack with the diesel/ULSD crack. ULSD is ultra-low-sulfur diesel. If the jet crack catches up with or overtakes the diesel crack, it is not just "oil is expensive", but a sign that jet fuel is starting to demand its own premium.

The second layer is regional. The pressure looks different in the US along the Gulf Coast–New York Harbor axis, different in Europe around ARA, that is Amsterdam–Rotterdam–Antwerp, and different in Asia around Singapore. Jet fuel is a global product, but a plane does not fill up with a global average. It fills up wherever it happens to be standing.

The counterintuitive point: a rise in the oil price does not automatically mean the same impact for every energy company. The key is the difference between the price of the input and the price of the output. If finished products get more expensive faster than crude, refiners can enjoy better economics. But if the shock hits the physical availability of crude, shipping, insurance, feedstock quality or refinery operations, the same news can be painful for part of the sector.

In the QMA framework, this is a clash of five layers: geopolitics raises nervousness, fundamentals show a bottleneck in refining, seasonality can add pressure through travel, crowd behavior simplifies the story to "oil up", and the macro layer deals with inflation in transport.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The upside may go to refiners such as Valero Energy (VLO), Marathon Petroleum (MPC) or Phillips 66 (PSX), if margins on diesel and jet fuel widen and their plants run without major constraints. The mechanism is simple: they buy crude and sell finished products; if the products get relatively more expensive, the room between input and output improves.

What matters, though, is the refinery's configuration. For this story, plants able to produce more middle distillates matter more, especially through units such as hydrocrackers — equipment that uses hydrogen and pressure to turn the heavier parts of crude into cleaner fuels such as diesel and jet fuel. If hydrocrackers are offline or a refinery is running at lower utilization, a high product price is not enough on its own.

Integrated oil companies such as Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), Shell (SHEL) or BP (BP) see a mixed impact. Upstream, that is production, can benefit from pricier crude; downstream, that is refining and product sales, depends on margins, geography, feedstock availability and product mix.

The downside falls on airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL), United Airlines (UAL), American Airlines (AAL), Southwest Airlines (LUV), Lufthansa (DLAKY) or Ryanair (RYAAY). Fuel is one of their key cost items. Some companies use price hedging, that is, financial contracts that dampen swings, but the impact often shows up through pressure on margins, surcharges or ticket prices. Logistics companies such as FedEx (FDX) and UPS (UPS) can suffer similarly, because the combination of pricier diesel and jet fuel raises transport costs.

With news like this, it pays to separate three questions. First: is this a physical problem, meaning a shortage of molecules, or just a geopolitical premium in the price? Second: what is rising more, crude, diesel/ULSD, or jet fuel? That decides refining margins. Third: is the pressure short-lived, or does it reach into the season of high demand for flying and transport?

A checklist for a better market reading: watch the jet crack versus the diesel/ULSD crack, regional spreads between the US, Europe and Asia, inventories of jet fuel and distillates, refinery runs, meaning refinery utilization, hydrocracker outages, airlines' cost hedging and how long route disruptions around Hormuz last. Without that, the sentence "oil is rising" is only half the film.

Think of the jet crack as the difference between the price of flour and the price of a finished croissant at the airport. When flour gets more expensive, the baker feels the pain. But when the croissant gets even more expensive than the flour, the baker may have more room. In practice: when it is specifically the jet crack that rises, the market is saying the problem lies in finished jet fuel, not just in crude. That can improve the position of some refiners but worsen airlines' costs — and an ordinary person may later run into it as pricier plane tickets or transport surcharges.

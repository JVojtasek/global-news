With Snap, it isn't enough to watch revenue and user growth; the key is whether growth is coming mainly from less lucrative regions while the most valuable audience in North America is weakening.

Snap right now looks like a shop that is gaining customers around the world but losing them on the most expensive street in town. Global monthly active users rose 4% year on year to 971 million, while North America declined; revenue, however, jumped 19% to almost 1.6 billion dollars, average revenue per user grew and the net margin improved to -10%.

The most interesting thing about Snap isn't the revenue growth itself. It's the contradiction: the company is earning more even though in North America, the region where social networks usually monetize best, the number of monthly users is falling.

It's as if a restaurant lost some of its regulars from central Prague but saved the bill by raising spending per guest and opening new tables on the outskirts. On the income statement, it looks elegant for a while. But an investor has to distinguish two things: healthy growth and growth from squeezing existing attention harder.

Here is the point that an ordinary report easily skips over: on social networks, not all users are economically equal. A user in a less lucrative advertising region can be great for the size of the community, but for the stock's valuation often carries less weight than a user in a mature advertising market. With Snap, it's therefore essential to look at regional ARPU, meaning how much the company earns on average per user in North America, Europe and the rest of the world. The difference tends not to be cosmetic, but a multiple: on similar platforms, North America is typically significantly more lucrative than the rest of the world, because advertisers there pay more to reach one user. It's not the difference between two kinds of bread rolls. It's the difference between an airport café and a vending machine in a school hallway.

So if the global number of users is growing but North America is weakening, the market won't ask only how many people came. It will ask where they came from and how much can reasonably be earned from them without making the product worse. In practice: user growth in a cheaper region can numerically mask the loss of a smaller but much more lucrative audience.

A counterintuitive truth: a margin improvement at a loss-making platform can be a bullish and a bearish argument at the same time. Bullish because it shows operating discipline. Bearish because if it's achieved mainly through cuts or more aggressive advertising, it can hurt the user experience over time. A social network is a party: when you let in too many advertising hosts with megaphones, the guests start quietly slipping away.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It helps companies tied to digital advertising, if the market believes that advertising budgets remain strong and Snap can monetize its audience better. Examples of the impact are social platforms such as Snap (SNAP), Meta Platforms (META), Pinterest (PINS) or Reddit (RDDT), or advertising technology such as The Trade Desk (TTD).

We see a mixed impact on large advertising ecosystems such as Alphabet (GOOGL) through YouTube. Stronger digital advertising is a plus, but smaller players are at the same time competing for the same user time and the same advertising dollar.

It can hurt mainly companies that have no clear advantage in measuring ad effectiveness or in retaining a young audience. In a consolidating social media environment, a simple rule applies: the less time a user spends on a platform, the more expensive it is to persuade an advertiser to leave its budget there.

With news like this, don't look only at revenue growth. A better framework is a checklist: 1) break users down by region and put regional ARPU next to them, 2) compare user growth with growth in average revenue per user, 3) watch ad load, meaning how many ads the company pushes into content, 4) watch engagement, meaning how often and for how long people come back to the app, 5) compare DAU/MAU, the ratio of daily to monthly users, because it shows whether the app isn't just an occasional visit, 6) read commentary on ad prices, for example whether cost per thousand impressions or cost per click is improving, 7) keep an eye on the costs of acquiring and retaining users, because growth bought with expensive marketing is of a different quality than growth that comes organically.

A warning: when revenue grows faster than users, it isn't automatically quality. It may be a better product for advertisers, higher ad prices, a favorable mix, but also short-term squeezing of attention. With Snap, the key test is simple: whether North America stops being a hole in the bucket, or whether global growth is just pouring water in faster than it drains out.

ARPU, average revenue per user, is like a café calculating how much it takes in on average per guest. It matters whether it has more guests or whether each guest spends more on an extra slice of cake. And above all, it matters where the café is: a guest at the airport can mean more economically than several guests in a cheaper location. For the market, this means a social network's stock can react positively even without stormy user growth, if spending per user is growing in lucrative regions. For the ordinary person, the impact is indirect: they may see more or better-targeted ads, and if they own similar stocks through funds, this metric can move the value of their portfolio.

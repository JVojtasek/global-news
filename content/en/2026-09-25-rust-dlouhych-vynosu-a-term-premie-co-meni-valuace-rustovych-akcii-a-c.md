The key risk is not just the politics around AI, but whether the rise in long-term yields is driven by the real rate and the term premium. That is what changes growth stock valuations and the cost of credit the most.

AI may have the brain of a genius, but when the mortgage on its future gets more expensive, the stock market suddenly counts more slowly. The report links a meeting between Trump and Xi Jinping with worries around AI, but the tradeable core of the excerpt is something drier and harder: yields on the longest US government bonds have climbed to their highest levels in more than two decades on worries about inflation and government debt. That matters, because the long-term yield is the price tag on time: it determines how expensive today's promises of distant profits are.

Most commentary would stop at geopolitics: Trump, Xi, AI, chips, export restrictions. But there is a hidden second referee here: the bond market. Politicians may be arguing over who gets the best chips, while the market is working out how much it costs to finance the future.

In practice, AI companies are often long-duration stocks. Duration in a stock means something simple: a large part of its value lies in expected profits far in the future. When long-term yields rise, those distant profits shrink in today's prices. It is not a moral judgement on AI. It is maths with a receipt.

Not every rise in yields is the same, though. If yields rise because the economy is strengthening and company revenues are improving, stocks can sometimes cope. But if they rise because of inflation nerves, a higher term premium and fear of government debt, that is a different story. The term premium is the extra return an investor demands for lending to the state for a long time and carrying the risk of an uncertain future. In the QMA reading, watching the yield alone is therefore not enough. The breakdown is the key: the nominal yield, the real yield after subtracting inflation expectations, breakeven inflation, the term premium and revisions to company earnings.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The downside is felt most in growth technology companies with high valuations and profits pushed into the future: chipmakers such as NVIDIA (NVDA), chip designers such as Advanced Micro Devices (AMD), and cloud and AI players such as Microsoft (MSFT), Alphabet (GOOGL) and Amazon (AMZN). That does not mean their business is weak; it means the market is recalculating the price of future money more strictly.

Real estate and utilities, sectors often seen as bond substitutes, may also feel pressure. Examples are Realty Income (O), American Tower (AMT) or NextEra Energy (NEE). The things to watch are mainly debt maturities, interest coverage, the ratio of debt to operating profit and the ability to pass higher financing costs on into prices.

The impact on banks such as JPMorgan Chase (JPM), Bank of America (BAC) or Wells Fargo (WFC) is mixed. Higher long-term yields can help interest margins, but only if pressure on deposits, credit losses and losses on bond portfolios do not rise at the same time.

Companies with a shorter payback on capital, strong cash and pricing power may look relatively better: selected energy, industrial or defence companies. Even there, though, the advantage is not automatic; what decides is whether profits grow faster than the cost of capital.

With news like this, do not follow only the headline about AI or geopolitics. A better checklist is: 1) are both nominal and real yields rising, or only inflation expectations? 2) is demand at government bond auctions weakening? 3) is the term premium rising? 4) are analysts cutting expected earnings, or are margins holding? 5) which companies have debt coming due soon, and which have a cash cushion? Publicly available sources include the US Treasury, the TIPS market, inflation breakevens and the Federal Reserve's term premium models.

The term premium is like lending money to a friend for ten years instead of a week. For a week, you might lend it without a second thought. For ten years, you want something extra, because you do not know what will happen to inflation, jobs, politics or your friend's discipline. In markets, a higher premium means more expensive long-term money. Stocks with big promises about the future then look less cheap, long-dated bonds fall in price, and ordinary people may feel it indirectly through pricier mortgages, loans and more cautious companies.

With diesel, it is not enough to look at the price itself — the key is whether the market rewards prompt delivery, because low inventories then raise pressure on cash, margins, and the cost of transport.

Diesel is the blood sugar of an industrial economy: when it falls too low, the world does not collapse dramatically — its hand just starts to shake with every freight invoice. According to the report, the global diesel shortage, fed by wars in Iran and Ukraine and dwindling tank inventories, is likely to persist into 2027. The point is not merely that "fuel will get more expensive," but that the tension is migrating into company margins, logistics, and inflation.

Crude oil is the celebrity, diesel is the accountant. Everyone watches the price of a barrel, but diesel shows how much it actually costs to physically move the economy: trucks, ships, construction equipment, agriculture, mining, diesel generators. When diesel inventories disappear, the market loses its cushion — like having a full pantry at home versus the last can of beans with guests at the door.

The fresh angle is not "crude versus diesel," but "today versus tomorrow." With low inventories, diesel turns into a time option: whoever has fuel now has the right not to panic. When delivery today is more expensive than delivery later, the market is in what traders call backwardation — essentially shouting, "do not let it flow into storage, I need it right now." That is counterintuitive: a high price does not automatically pull inventories in, because storing fuel can hurt economically.

A strong analogy: imagine a hotel during a Taylor Swift concert. A room today costs several times what a room a month out does. The owner will not keep rooms empty "just in case" when today's guest is paying a premium. Diesel works similarly: if the current barrel earns more than a future one, the incentive to drain tanks grows stronger. And the emptier the tanks, the more every refinery outage looks like a late train on a Friday afternoon — suddenly everyone is standing still.

As a model: if a refinery's distillate margin rises by USD 10 per barrel and it processes 300,000 barrels a day, the gross daily contribution can be roughly USD 3 million before maintenance, hedging and operating constraints. Over a quarter, that is USD 270 million. But the second layer is time: if the firm holds 1 million barrels of inventory and the price of inventory rises by USD 10 per barrel, its tanks sit on USD 10 million more cash. Profit can shine while cash flow starts to look like a wallet after a visit to the tire shop.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

Positive side: refiners and fuel traders, provided they have available capacity and are not pinched by regulation. Examples of impact: Valero Energy (VLO), Marathon Petroleum (MPC), Phillips 66 (PSX). What matters for them is refining margins, plant utilisation, outages, inventories, and working capital — with expensive stock, earnings can look good while cash disappears into the tanks.

Mixed impact: integrated energy majors such as Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), Shell (SHEL), and BP (BP). Upstream helps, refining can help even more, but chemicals, taxes, regulation and geopolitics complicate the picture.

The less pleasant side: transport and logistics — for example FedEx (FDX), UPS (UPS), J.B. Hunt (JBHT), Old Dominion Freight Line (ODFL), Maersk (AMKBY). It depends on fuel surcharges: if they are passed through with a lag, the accounting hit arrives before the compensation. Industrial firms with machinery and supply chains are also sensitive, for example Caterpillar (CAT), Deere (DE), as is the consumer sector, where more expensive transport gradually seeps into the price of goods.

A quick impact map: refiners watch crack spreads and outages; carriers watch surcharge timing; machinery makers watch demand from fuel-sensitive customers; retail watches freight embedded in the cost of goods.

In stories like this, it is dangerous to confuse the price of the commodity with company profit. A better frame is five questions: 1) is it crude oil, or specifically distillates? 2) are inventories low, or is just the price high? 3) is today's delivery more expensive than a future one, i.e. is the market punishing storage? 4) how much cash will working capital tie up in more expensive inventory? 5) can the company pass the cost on to the customer quickly?

Warning indicator in the statements: EBITDA may rise, but free cash flow deteriorates, because more expensive inventories and receivables eat the cash. Model: EBITDA +USD 100 million, inventories and receivables -USD 140 million, maintenance capex -USD 40 million, giving FCF of -USD 80 million. Muscle on paper, draft in the wallet.

"Backwardation" is like an umbrella in a storm being more expensive than the same umbrella ordered for next week. You need it today, so you pay a premium. The impact? The market motivates people to pull fuel out of storage right away instead of keeping it as a reserve. For equities, that means a widening gap between firms with physical capacity and inventory and firms that merely pay for fuel as a cost. For a household wallet, it can mean more expensive transport of goods and a slower retreat in prices at the shops.

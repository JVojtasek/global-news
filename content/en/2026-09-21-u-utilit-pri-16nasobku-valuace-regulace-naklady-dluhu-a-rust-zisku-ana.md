Electrification is a powerful long-term story, but for utilities the decisive question is whether a multiple around 16x matches the cost of debt, the regulator, and the actual growth in earnings.

The electric grid is starting to look like a gym in January: everyone suddenly wants capacity, but dumbbells, cables, and trainers do not expand overnight.

The report builds the investment thesis on the fact that US utilities trade around 16x earnings, while electricity demand rises on the back of electrification, industry and grid upgrades. According to the piece, the Global X U.S. Electrification ETF (ZAP) holds 77.2% in utilities and 22.8% in industrial firms, so it combines power operators with infrastructure suppliers. But 16x on its own is not a "cheap" or "expensive" sticker; it starts to mean something only against the sector's own history, the broader market, bond yields, and expected earnings growth.

The most interesting thing here is not 16x itself. The interesting part is that utilities are slowly ceasing to behave like boring bond substitutes and are beginning to resemble companies in a construction boom — with one big catch: their earnings are often set not by demand, but by the regulator.

A classic mistake is to say: electricity consumption is rising, therefore utilities will automatically earn more. For regulated utilities the pass-through works differently. The firm builds lines, substations or generation, those enter what is called the rate base — the regulated asset value on which it is allowed to earn an approved return. If the regulator recognises the costs quickly and fairly, rising investment can lift earnings. If the process drags, interest rates rise, and the public pushes back against higher bills, demand can make for a nice headline but weaker margins.

That is why it is important not to lump all utilities into one box. A regulated distribution company has very different economics from an electricity trader or the owner of power plants selling at market prices. For the first, it is mainly the regulator and the cost of debt; for the second, it is more the price of electricity, capacity payments, and contracts with buyers.

The counterintuitive point: for utilities it is not just about how much electricity is sold, but who pays for expanding the kitchen. A full restaurant is wonderful, but if the city approves your menu prices and the bank raises the rate on your loan for a new oven, a packed dining room does not yet mean a fat profit.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

A positive impact can come in several layers. Regulated utilities such as NextEra Energy (NEE), Duke Energy (DUK), Southern Company (SO), and American Electric Power (AEP) can benefit from rising grid investment. For them it is relevant to watch rate-base growth, authorised ROE — the return on equity recognised by the regulator — leverage, debt maturities, and the gap between capital expenditure and free cash flow.

The second layer is industrial suppliers. Firms such as Eaton (ETN), Quanta Services (PWR), Hubbell (HUBB), and GE Vernova (GEV) sell equipment, services, and solutions for the grid. There it is more practical to look at backlog — the volume of contracted work — the ratio of new orders to revenue, margins in electrification segments, and commentary on delivery times.

The third layer is power generators and nuclear and gas capacity, for example Constellation Energy (CEG) and Vistra (VST). For them it is important to watch forward electricity prices, capacity auctions, and long-term contracts with large consumers.

On the opposite side stand firms sensitive to higher real bond yields. Utilities often carry high debt and compete with the yield on safer assets. A weaker impact can also fall on renewable projects and developers, if they hit interconnection queues, expensive financing, or grid constraints; one name to watch is ReNew Energy Global (RNW), even though its link to the US grid is indirect.

With stories like this, watching the nice word "electrification" is not enough. A control frame for reading such reports: 1) real bond yields, since they set the price of capital, 2) regulator decisions on tariffs and authorised ROE, 3) rate-base growth against debt growth, 4) backlog and margins at grid suppliers, 5) the forward electricity curve and capacity auctions, 6) for an ETF like ZAP, trading volume, the bid-ask spread, and concentration in the largest holdings. For a multiple around 16x it is also fair to ask whether it matches earnings growth and rates: the same multiple can be more attractive with cheap capital and fast growth, but tighter with expensive debt and slow tariff approvals. The biggest risk is confusing structural demand for an immediate rise in earnings.

The rate base is like the value of the kitchen in a regulated restaurant. The bigger kitchen you build, the more meals you can serve, but the prices on the menu are set not by the mood of your guests but by the authority. For the market, that means: rising electricity demand can help utility stocks, but only when the regulator lets firms pass the investment through to bills. For an ordinary wallet, it can mean a more modern and reliable grid over time, but also pressure for higher electricity bills.

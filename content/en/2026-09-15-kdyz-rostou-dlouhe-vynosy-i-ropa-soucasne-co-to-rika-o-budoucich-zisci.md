Watch out for days when long yields and oil rise together: the market is then weighing not just stock valuations, but also the risk that future profits will be smaller.

This morning the market resembles a car whose handbrake someone has pulled while also raising the price of gas and sending a police officer to the intersection. Stock futures are slipping slightly, the yield on the 10-year US Treasury has climbed to its highest level since 2007, oil keeps getting more expensive, and the Fed is starting a two-day meeting with a rate decision on Wednesday. On top of that, bitcoin is weakening ahead of an important Senate vote on new crypto regulation, and Dave & Buster’s (PLAY) shares are falling after weak results.

This isn't news about five separate events. It's one story about the market suddenly repricing the cost of time, the cost of energy and the cost of the freedom to take risk.

The most important thing isn't the rise in the 10-year Treasury yield on its own, but the combination: higher yields plus pricier oil. Higher yields push stock valuations down, because future profits are discounted at a higher rate. Expensive oil at the same time bites into corporate margins and household wallets. That's an unpleasant double slap: first the market says a future dollar of profit is worth less, and then it asks whether that dollar of profit will arrive at all.

As a model: if an investor expects 1 dollar of cash in 10 years, at a 3% discount rate it's worth roughly 0.74 dollars today. At 5%, only about 0.61 dollars. The company didn't have to do anything wrong; only market gravity changed. And with margins it's similarly brutal: a business with revenue of 100, costs of 90 and profit of 10 can see its profit drop to 7.3 if costs rise by 3% and it doesn't pass that on to customers. That's a 27% decline, not a cosmetic one.

The Fed in this is like a firefighter in a kitchen where a pan is burning, but the main switch controls the whole house. Rates can't produce oil, but they can cool demand. If oil is raising inflationary pressure, the Fed may sound tougher even though the cause doesn't lie directly with the American consumer. And the market doesn't like it when the central bank has to brake because of something it can't easily fix itself.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It helps energy producers, if the higher oil price holds: integrated companies such as Exxon Mobil (XOM) and Chevron (CVX) are sensitive through the price of realized production, while oil services such as SLB (SLB) benefit more when producers raise investment in drilling and maintenance.

It hurts long-term growth stories where a large part of the value lies far in the future. Chipmakers such as NVIDIA (NVDA) may have a strong structural story, but a high valuation is sensitive to rising yields. Similarly, software such as Snowflake (SNOW) or other expensive growth stocks suffer when investors' required return goes up.

Housing and debt are also under pressure. Homebuilders such as D.R. Horton (DHI) or Lennar (LEN) watch mortgage rates, which rest precisely on the long end of the yield curve. REITs such as Realty Income (O) and utilities such as NextEra Energy (NEE) also compete with bonds for investors seeking regular income.

Bitcoin and crypto stocks, for example Coinbase (COIN) or miners such as Marathon Digital (MARA), react to regulation differently from conventional companies: it's not just about costs, but about uncertainty over the rules of the game. Dave & Buster’s (PLAY), in turn, is a small thermometer of household entertainment budgets. When people save on leisure spending, weaker results at companies like this may suggest that higher rates and pricier fuel are already creeping into weekend plans.

On days like this, don't just watch the Fed headline. A better framework is five questions: 1) is the 10-year yield rising because of higher real rates, or because of an inflation premium, 2) is oil rising across the board, or only at the short end of the futures curve, 3) is the Fed changing its rate and inflation projections, 4) are credit spreads, meaning the risk premiums on corporate bonds, widening, 5) are consumer companies confirming pressure on margins and foot traffic.

In company filings, look specifically for: interest expense; debt maturities, meaning when debt has to be refinanced; the adjusted EBITDA reconciliation, where you can usually see what is real operating performance and what is accounting cosmetics; and, for consumer companies, comparable sales, foot traffic and average spend per customer. The red warning light isn't one weak line item, but the combination of falling traffic, rising interest costs and a reduced ability to raise prices.

The yield on the 10-year US Treasury is like the base rent on money across the whole economy. When that rent rises, investors want a higher reward from stocks too, companies pay more for debt, and loans tied to market rates get more expensive for people. For the market, it means less appetite to pay high prices for distant future profits; for an ordinary person's wallet, more expensive financing and greater pressure on the budget.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Ladders make a high task look ordinary, yet falls from them remain a substantial and preventable source of injury at work and at home.

**What it means.** The decisive safety choices happen before climbing: whether a ladder is the right tool, whether it is sound, where it stands and how the task will be done without reaching or carrying awkwardly.

**Risks and impact.** A short fall can still cause severe injury. Older adults in one trauma-centre study had worse injuries despite falling from lower average heights.

**What can be done.** Inspect the ladder, use firm level ground, set a leaning ladder at roughly 75 degrees, secure it, keep traffic away and climb facing it with three points of contact.

**What to watch.** Stop if the task requires the top step, sideways stretching, both hands for more than a moment, unstable ground or work near live power.

## FACTS

The ladder is not a platform with narrow steps. It is access equipment with limited stability and a small margin for error. OSHA requires ladders to be used only for their designed purpose, within the manufacturer's rated load and on stable, level surfaces unless secured against displacement. Oil, grease and other slipping hazards must be removed.

For an ordinary leaning ladder, the base should sit approximately one-quarter of the working length away from the wall: one unit out for every four units up. That produces an angle of about 75 degrees. A ladder used to reach an upper landing should normally extend at least 3 feet, or 0.9 metres, above it. If that is impossible, OSHA requires the top to be secured and a grasping device provided.

Those are workplace rules, but the geometry does not change at a house. A ladder in a doorway, driveway or passage can be struck or opened into. Secure it or block the traffic route. Keep the top and bottom clear.

## EVIDENCE

CDC researchers combined three U.S. occupational surveillance systems for a 2014 report on 2011 ladder falls. They counted 113 work-related deaths, an estimated 15,460 injuries reported by employers that involved at least one day away from work, and an estimated 34,000 injuries treated in emergency departments. These are historical workplace figures, not a current annual estimate for every ladder user. Their value is the consistent signal across independent systems: ladder falls are not a fringe event.

The same report found rates varied by occupation, employment and age. Older workers had higher fatality rates, while construction, extraction, installation, maintenance and repair jobs carried particular risk. Prevention therefore cannot be reduced to telling individuals to “be careful.” Equipment selection, scheduling, training and the availability of safer access all matter.

A separate trauma-registry study of 340 adult ladder-fall patients found that people over 66 fell from a lower average height than younger adults but had higher injury-severity scores and greater odds of admission. It was a single-centre retrospective study, so it cannot predict one person's outcome. It does rebut the idea that a modest height makes a fall trivial.

## PERSPECTIVES

To the occasional home user, the job may look too small for planning: one bulb, one gutter, one box on a high shelf. Familiarity is part of the risk. The task begins, the correct ladder is in another room, and a chair or half-open stepladder becomes “good enough.” The first question should be whether the chosen equipment can reach the work without standing on forbidden steps or improvising extra height.

To a worker, the ladder may be the equipment available rather than the equipment best suited to the task. The UK Health and Safety Executive says ladders can be appropriate for low-risk, short-duration work when equipment offering greater fall protection is not justified. That is not a declaration that every short task belongs on a ladder. If the work needs sustained force, heavy materials, long sideways reach or two free hands, a platform or scaffold may be the honest choice.

To an older adult, experience may increase confidence while physical consequences become less forgiving. Asking for help, using a pole tool from the ground or hiring someone with proper access equipment is not a loss of competence. It is a change in the cost of a mistake.

## CONTEXT

Run the ground-level sequence before every climb. First, select. The ladder must be long enough for the working position and rated for the person plus tools and materials. Never place it on boxes, bricks, barrels or another movable object to gain height. A self-supporting stepladder must be fully opened and locked; it is not a leaning ladder in disguise.

Second, inspect. Look for bent or split rails, cracked or missing rungs, damaged feet, loose hardware, contamination and locks that do not engage. A damaged ladder should leave service, not receive a promise to be used gently. Check above as well as below: metal ladders and overhead power lines are a dangerous combination, and nonconductive rails do not make electrical work casual.

Third, set. Use firm, level, clean ground. Apply the 1-in-4 rule to a leaning ladder and support both top rails equally. Tie or otherwise secure the ladder where it could slip sideways or outward. Lock doors that cannot be opened onto it, or guard the area. Never move, extend or shift a ladder while someone is on it.

Fourth, plan the hands. Face the ladder while climbing. Maintain three points of contact—two hands and one foot, or two feet and one hand—and keep the centre of the body between the rails. Use a tool belt, hoist line or helper rather than climbing with an object that blocks vision or occupies the hands.

## PEOPLE

NIOSH's retail guidance describes a common trap: a worker retrieves a box and then descends holding it in one hand. The object blocks the view and makes three-point contact impossible. A container, basket, shoulder bag or second person can move the item while leaving the climber's hands free.

At home, the equivalent may be a paint tin, pruning tool or storage box. Decide how the object will travel before going up. If the answer is “in my hands,” revise the plan.

Someone on the ground can also protect the ladder from traffic and summon help, but a helper is not a substitute for stable setup. Nor should a person stand beneath falling tools. The safest assistance is planned, positioned and specific.

## DEEPER

Ladder advice often concentrates on behaviour after the feet leave the floor: hold on, face in, do not lean. Those rules matter, but they arrive late in the chain. By then, the wrong ladder may already be too short, the feet may already be on loose gravel and the task may already require the climber to twist away from the rails.

This is why the strongest safety habit is a stop rule. If the belt buckle or navel must pass outside the side rails, climb down and reposition. If the top step is needed, get a taller ladder or different platform. If both hands must stay on the work, choose equipment that provides guarded standing space. If wind, rain, ice, fatigue or haste changes the conditions, postpone the task.

The NIOSH Ladder Safety App was built around this systems view. Its tools address selection, inspection, setup angle and proper use, not courage. The agency says the app is not currently receiving updates, so it should complement—not replace—the ladder's labels and current official guidance.

The practical takeaway fits on one line: choose, inspect, set, secure, then climb. Any plan that becomes less safe at one of those verbs should return to the ground.

With hawkish Fed remarks, the key is to tell whether the market is repricing only the nearest decision through the two-year yield, or a whole longer period of expensive money through real yields and the breadth of the stock market.

When even a cautious centrist at the Fed leaves a rate hike on the table, the market does not hear a hawk screeching, but the click of a safety catch. According to Reuters, Boston Fed President Susan Collins said that if inflation disappoints, a further rate hike would be appropriate; at the same time she acknowledged that the latest data are mixed. The point is not that the Fed is necessarily heading for tightening, but that it does not want to let markets celebrate victory over inflation prematurely.

The most important thing about Collins's remark is not the word hike, but who said it. This is not a traditional hawk who would gladly tighten the monetary brake even on the way out of the car park. Collins tends to be seen as part of the Fed's more cautious centre. When a voice like that mentions the possibility of higher rates, the market should read it as a shift in the balance of risks: the Fed is more afraid of a premature easing of financial conditions than of looking too tough for a while.

A simple FedSpeak scale helps here, that is, a way of reading central bankers' public remarks. A hawk who talks tough adds little new information; that is the dog that barks at the postman every day. A centrist who leaves a rate hike on the table matters more, because it shows the centre of the debate moving. And if a dove, that is, a Fed member usually inclined towards lower rates, toughened their language in a similar way, the market should pay the most attention. The second layer is the chorus: if people from different Fed camps repeat a similar line within a few days, it is not solo karaoke but management of financial conditions.

Here is the trick: a central banker's sentence alone can work as a cheaper version of a rate hike. If bond yields jump after such rhetoric, stocks get nervous and the dollar strengthens, the Fed gets part of the tightening for free, without a vote. It is like a parent who does not have to confiscate the child's tablet; a look over the top of the glasses is enough. Suddenly the market sits up straighter.

So the better question is not: Will there be another hike? The better question is: Will the market reprice the length of the period of higher rates? One additional hike is a one-off event. A longer period of higher rates changes the maths of valuations, corporate financing, mortgages and risk appetite. You can see the practical difference on the curve: if it is mainly the two-year yield reacting, the market is dealing with the Fed's nearest decision; if five-year and ten-year real yields, that is, yields adjusted for inflation expectations, are rising too, the market is repricing the whole price of time.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The minus for bonds is straightforward: when the market expects higher rates for longer, yields rise and the prices of existing bonds fall. Longer maturities are more sensitive if the worries spill over into ten-year yields; the short end of the curve, in turn, reacts mainly to expectations about the Fed.

Tech and growth stocks take a hit through the discount rate, that is, through how the market values future profits in today's money. The more distant the profits, the more higher yields hurt. Examples of the impact are chipmakers such as NVIDIA (NVDA), software companies such as Salesforce (CRM) or the healthcare cloud Veeva Systems (VEEV), where part of the value rests on a long growth story. That is not a verdict on the quality of the business; it is valuation sensitivity.

Banks such as JPMorgan Chase (JPM) or Bank of America (BAC) see a mixed impact. Higher rates can help interest income, but at the same time they increase pressure on deposit costs, loan quality and activity in mortgages or mergers. Insurers such as Chubb (CB) can benefit from higher portfolio yields, but here too asset quality matters.

Defensive dividend sectors such as utilities and real estate investment trusts, for example NextEra Energy (NEE) or Realty Income (O), may suffer because their dividends compete with the yields on safer bonds. A stronger dollar, on the other hand, would do less to help companies with dollar costs and foreign revenue in local currencies, while weighing on US exporters.

With remarks like these, it makes sense to distinguish three floors of reaction. First, the two-year yield: it shows expectations for the Fed's nearest policy. Second, the five-year to ten-year real yield: it suggests whether the whole period of more expensive money is being repriced. Third, the breadth of the stock market and credit spreads, that is, the risk premium on corporate bonds: if only expensive growth stocks are weakening, it is mainly a repricing of valuations; if the weakness spreads into banks, industrials and smaller companies, the market is already dealing with the impact on the economy.

Also watch out for the single-comment trap. One remark is not a Fed decision. It only gains weight in combination with inflation, the labour market and whether financial conditions loosen too much again. If yields rise after such rhetoric but stocks keep climbing euphorically and credit spreads tighten, the Fed may have reason to talk even tougher.

The discount rate is like a discount for waiting. If someone promises you a thousand in ten years, at low rates you treat it almost like money today. At higher rates you say: for such a long wait I want a bigger reward, so the present value of that promise is smaller. For the market this means that shares of companies with profits far in the future can get cheaper even without their business getting worse. For an ordinary person the same logic can show up in more expensive loans, more cautious companies and a slower pace of growth in asset prices.

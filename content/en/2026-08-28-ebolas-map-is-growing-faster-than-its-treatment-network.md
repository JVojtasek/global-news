## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Congolese government figures reported by the Associated Press show Ebola has reached two more health zones in North Kivu, bringing the total affected to 60. Médecins Sans Frontières also opened a new treatment centre in Beni.

**What it means.** The response is adding care capacity, but the outbreak’s map is still expanding. A bed treats a patient already found; containment requires surveillance to identify contacts and interrupt transmission before more communities appear on the map.

**Risks and impact.** The outbreak remains concentrated in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, where insecurity, displacement and strained health services impede early detection.

**What can be done.** The useful public measure is whether a rising share of new patients comes from monitored contact lists, community deaths fall and affected health zones stop increasing.

**What to watch.** Over the next two to four weeks, watch the weekly geographic footprint, the proportion of cases linked to known contacts and the interval from symptoms to isolation.

## FACTS

The latest government figures cited by the Associated Press put the outbreak at 5,794 confirmed cases and 2,786 deaths across 60 health zones. Biena and Manguredjipa in North Kivu are the newest affected zones.

Reuters reported the same day that MSF had opened a treatment centre in Beni. Beni had recorded 122 confirmed cases and 87 deaths; provincial data put North Kivu’s case fatality rate near 68%, higher than the national figure of about 48%.

The direction is clearer than any single tally. A Congolese Ministry of Health report based on 19 August data counted 56 affected zones, 5,290 confirmed cases and 2,516 confirmed deaths. A WHO risk assessment counted 54 zones on 13 August. Different reporting dates and later data cleaning require caution, but all three snapshots show continuing geographic expansion.

WHO said on 24 August that transmission was outpacing control efforts. It reported roughly 90 confirmed cases a day during the outbreak’s first three months and said deaths outside treatment centres made up about 60% of weekly fatalities over the preceding six weeks.

## EVIDENCE

The case total measures accumulated harm. The map and surveillance indicators say more about momentum.

WHO’s 14 August risk assessment found that more than 80% of new infections were being identified outside known contact lists, while 60% remained unlinked even after investigation. Investigators cannot interrupt a chain they have not found.

The Congolese report adds another signal. Contact follow-up stood at 82.9% on 19 August, below the response’s 85% operational threshold. It also recorded 23 community deaths among 40 confirmed deaths that day. The ministry warned of incomplete and discordant data requiring regular reconciliation, but the figures point in the same direction as WHO’s assessment.

The new Beni centre matters because shortages delay isolation and care. Reuters reported that responders had estimated a need for as many as 2,000 additional beds; MSF was operating about 400. But capacity may rise while infections spread faster, or beds may be placed too far from emerging transmission.

There is important uncertainty. The latest 60-zone figure comes through AP’s report of government data; the corresponding public daily report was not available for line-by-line review at publication time. The progression from 54 to 56 to 60 zones is strong evidence of expansion, not a perfectly harmonised time series.

## CONTEXT

Ebola control is a chain: recognise a possible case, test it, isolate safely, trace contacts, monitor them and support communities so symptoms are reported early. Treatment centres sit in the middle.

When a patient arrives late, two harms compound. The chance of severe disease is greater, and family or community exposure may already have occurred. Community deaths are therefore both a care indicator and a surveillance warning.

This outbreak is caused by Bundibugyo virus. WHO states that there is no licensed vaccine specifically for this virus; Congolese authorities have begun using an existing Ebola vaccine for frontline workers under a monitored programme while research proceeds. That is a decision for public-health authorities, not a basis for readers to infer personal protection or alter medical plans.

Conflict and displacement turn geography into more than distance. Mobile populations may cross administrative borders, treatment routes can be interrupted, and trust can determine whether contact tracing works. An “affected” zone means another response network to support, not merely another colour on a map.

### Practical impact

For readers outside the affected region, expansion does not by itself imply a new personal exposure. It explains why official regional surveillance matters and why cumulative headlines should not be translated into individual risk.

Organisations operating in affected provinces should use current guidance from the Congolese Ministry of Health, WHO and local authorities. This analysis does not replace clinical, travel or workplace guidance.

For anyone assessing an outbreak headline, ask three questions: Are new cases already known to contact tracers? Are people reaching care earlier? Is the map stable? Those questions distinguish a response that is scaling from one gaining control.

## DEEPER

Public attention rewards visible capacity: a centre opens, vaccines arrive, beds are counted. Surveillance is less visible because success looks like a case found before it becomes a cluster.

A treatment network partly absorbs consequences. A surveillance network changes the outbreak’s path by shortening the interval between infection, detection and isolation. Both save lives, but only one can make tomorrow’s patient less likely.

The durable lesson extends beyond Ebola. In any fast-moving outbreak, inputs are not outcomes. Staff, laboratories, beds and supplies matter because of what they change: linked cases, detection time, community deaths and geographic spread. When those measures lag, a growing response may still be losing the race.

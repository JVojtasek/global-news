With geopolitical oil news, the key is to tell whether the real physical tightness of the market is easing, or only the premium for fear is disappearing.

Oil is now behaving like someone who already has an evacuation bag packed but has just heard that the fire brigade may arrive in time.

On Friday, according to the report, oil prices fell after a sharp rise in the previous session. The market was again weighing two opposing forces: the possibility of diplomatic progress between the US and Iran against continuing threats to Saudi energy infrastructure.

The most interesting thing is not the price drop itself. What is interesting is that the market is probably pricing not only oil as a commodity, but also a time-limited insurance policy against supply disruption.

Geopolitical oil often works like an option: the closer the risk of an interruption to the flow of barrels seems, the more expensive protection against a price spike becomes. As soon as hope for diplomacy appears, part of that premium can evaporate quickly, even though not a single barrel of production has physically changed. That is an important distinction: the price can fall not because the world suddenly has a surplus of oil, but because the market has marked down the price of panic.

A more practical breakdown than the headline looks like this: 1) physical tightness of the market, 2) the geopolitical premium, 3) the demand cycle. If the spot oil price falls but the front of the futures curve stays tight, meaning short-dated contracts are markedly more expensive than later ones, the physical market may still be saying: there are no spare barrels. If, on the other hand, calendar spreads, option volatility and transport or insurance costs also ease, it is more a retreat of fear than a change in fundamentals.

This is a common trap: investors read geopolitics as a war/peace switch. The oil market, however, often reads it as a dimmer. It does not flip from zero to a hundred. It just turns the brightness down or up depending on how close the risk is to an actual supply constraint.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

Lower oil may weigh in the short term on producers such as Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), ConocoPhillips (COP) or Occidental Petroleum (OXY), because lower realised prices reduce cash flow from production. For these companies it makes sense to watch in particular the free cash flow breakeven, capital expenditure and revisions to expected earnings.

Oil consumers, by contrast, can breathe more easily. For airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL), United Airlines (UAL) or Southwest Airlines (LUV), fuel is a large cost item; the impact, though, depends on their price hedging and the strength of demand for flights.

Refiners such as Valero Energy (VLO) and Marathon Petroleum (MPC) are a more complicated case. Cheaper oil helps them only if prices of petrol, diesel and other products do not fall just as fast. That is why the crack spread, the difference between the price of crude input and the value of refined products, matters more for them than the price of a barrel itself.

Petrochemicals such as Dow (DOW) or LyondellBasell (LYB) can benefit from cheaper inputs if end demand holds. Oil services such as SLB (SLB) and Halliburton (HAL) tend to be sensitive only to a more lasting fall in prices, because that can cool producers' investment budgets.

With news like this, following the headline "oil falls" is not enough. A better framework is a checklist: is the futures curve changing, especially the gap between the nearest contracts? Is the Brent-WTI spread changing, the difference between global and US oil? Is option volatility, and the price of protection against a sharp rise, receding? Are refiners' crack spreads holding? Do we see changes in official statistics on inventories, production and exports?

Only when several of these layers move at once does the news have a better chance of moving from the category of geopolitical noise to the category of regime change. For valuations, this means watching the sensitivity of profits to the oil price at producers, margins at refiners, fuel costs at airlines and, at consumer companies, the effect on household wallets.

The geopolitical premium is like booking a pricier taxi to the airport earlier than usual because roadblocks are threatened in the city. When news comes that detours may be agreed and the queues will not be so bad, the need to get to the airport does not go away; you are just no longer willing to pay as much for insurance against disaster. For the market, this means oil can get cheaper without new wells or tankers. For stocks, it shifts pressure from producers towards companies whose costs fall with cheaper energy. For ordinary people, such a move may eventually show up mainly through fuel and transport prices, but not immediately and not always with the same force.

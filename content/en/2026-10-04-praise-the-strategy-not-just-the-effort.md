## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** A famous line of research found that praising children for intelligence can make setbacks harder, while feedback about process can encourage challenge-seeking and persistence.

**What it means.** “Praise effort” is a useful correction to “You’re so smart,” but it is not a spell. Helpful praise points to something real and repeatable: a strategy, a revision, careful attention, useful help-seeking or progress.

**Risks and impact.** Person praise can make one performance feel like a verdict on identity. Empty effort praise creates a different problem: children can hear encouragement that does not help them improve, or praise that quietly denies a poor result.

**What can be done.** Describe what you noticed, connect it to the outcome, and ask what the child might try next. Sometimes the honest response is curiosity rather than praise.

**What to watch.** Notice what happens after difficulty. The useful question is not whether a child can repeat a growth-mindset phrase, but whether they can select another strategy, seek appropriate help and stay engaged.

## FACTS

The distinction is usually described as person praise versus process praise. Person praise attaches success to identity: “You’re smart,” “You’re a natural,” or even “You’re a good girl.” Process praise draws attention to actions that can be changed or repeated: effort, strategy, concentration, revision and help-seeking.

In a set of experiments published in 1998, Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck reported that fifth-graders praised for intelligence after success responded less adaptively to later difficulty than children praised for effort. The intelligence-praised children showed less persistence and enjoyment, and were more likely to choose tasks that protected the appearance of being smart. A 1999 study by Melissa Kamins and Dweck found a related pattern in young children: feedback aimed at the whole person produced more helpless responses after mistakes than feedback aimed at the process.

Home life is not a laboratory. In a longitudinal study of 53 children, researchers recorded ordinary parent-child interactions when the children were 14 to 38 months old. A higher proportion of process praise predicted a more incremental view of ability at ages seven to eight. A later analysis linked early process praise to fourth-grade achievement through those motivational beliefs. Both findings are associations from the same small cohort, not proof that a phrase spoken to a toddler determines later grades.

## EVIDENCE

The strongest conclusion is narrower than the slogan. Feedback changes what a child thinks success means. “You are clever” can make the next hard problem feel like a test of whether the label is still true. “That plan helped you find the pattern” identifies something the child can carry forward. The mechanism is plausible, and short experiments repeatedly show that wording can alter the next choice or response to failure.

The longitudinal work adds something valuable: parents naturally use different kinds of praise at home, and those differences can predict later beliefs. But the original home sample was only 53 children. Families were not randomly assigned to praise styles, and parents who use more process praise may differ in other ways. The follow-up achievement result came from that same cohort, so it is not an independent replication of the original association.

The wider growth-mindset literature is contested. A 2023 systematic review by Brooke Macnamara and Alexander Burgoyne examined 63 intervention studies involving 97,672 participants. In the subset of 13 studies that both met stronger design standards and actually changed students’ mindsets, the estimated academic effect was not statistically significant: an average standardized effect of 0.04, with a 95 percent confidence interval from −0.01 to 0.10.

Other researchers argue that this average conceals meaningful variation. Elizabeth Tipton and colleagues reanalysed the evidence with methods designed for heterogeneous effects and found larger benefits in some academically at-risk groups. The dispute is real. It argues against both miracle claims and blanket dismissal.

## PERSPECTIVES

One framing says that words build beliefs. In this view, children infer a theory of ability from adult reactions. Praise intelligence and difficulty threatens identity; praise a workable process and difficulty becomes information. This account explains why two equally warm compliments can lead to different choices five minutes later. It also gives parents something concrete to change.

A second framing warns that “growth mindset” has been packaged far beyond its evidence. School interventions have sometimes been sold as a low-cost route to achievement while underplaying teaching quality, prior knowledge, poverty, disability and access to help. Macnamara and Burgoyne’s review is a reminder that changing a belief is not the same as changing a report card. A child cannot persist their way through unclear instruction or a missing ramp.

A third framing focuses on context. Tipton and colleagues argue that asking only for one average effect is like averaging rain across a continent: the number can be correct and still hide where the ground is wet. Their analysis suggests that targeted students and supportive environments may matter. Yet this does not justify assuming that every child benefits or that any sentence containing the word “effort” is effective.

The practical middle is less marketable and more useful. Feedback can shape a child’s interpretation of success and failure. It works inside a larger system of teaching, resources, relationships and opportunity. Praise is not the engine of learning. At best, it is one part of the dashboard, telling a child which actions are worth noticing again.

## CONTEXT

Praise became a parenting technology because it seemed kinder than criticism and more modern than command. Then research complicated the idea that all positive words have the same effect. The important shift was from emotional temperature to informational content. A warm judgment about the child and a warm observation about the work may both sound encouraging, but they answer different questions: “Who am I?” and “What did I do?”

The familiar replacement—swap “You’re smart” for “You worked hard”—solves only half the problem. Effort is not automatically effective. A child can labour over the wrong method, misunderstand the instructions or need a skill that has not yet been taught. Praising effort after every result may sound like consolation, especially when the adult plainly knows that the work missed the mark. It can also imply that success is a moral reward for trying, which life does not reliably provide.

More precise process feedback connects action, evidence and next choice. “You grouped the pieces by colour, and that made the edge easier to see.” “Your first opening was confusing, but the new one tells me where the story starts.” “You kept rereading; shall we try drawing the problem instead?” Each sentence preserves warmth while remaining falsifiable. The child can disagree, test the observation or reuse the strategy.

There is no need to turn the kitchen table into a behavioural laboratory. Not every drawing needs an evaluation. A simple “Tell me about it” can return ownership to the child. Celebration is allowed too. The goal is not perfect phrasing. It is to avoid making love sound conditional on performance while giving feedback enough substance to be useful.

## PEOPLE

Consider the child who brings home a difficult page with three crossed-out answers and one correct one. “You’re a maths genius” erases the visible history of the page. “Great effort” notices the struggle but not what changed. “I can see you tried subtraction first, checked it, then drew the groups” treats the page as a record of thinking.

That difference matters for adults as well. A parent reaching for praise is often trying to offer confidence, repair disappointment or keep the evening moving. The quickest compliment may be all anyone has energy for. Research should not turn ordinary warmth into another test parents can fail.

A useful habit is smaller: when there is time, name one true thing. If there is no true and useful praise, ask one honest question. “What part are you happiest with?” “Where did you get stuck?” “Do you want help, or do you want me to look?” Those questions can preserve the child’s agency without withdrawing attention.

## DEEPER

Praise has a hidden power because it tells a child where the judge is standing. A label such as “smart,” “talented” or “good” places the adult above the work, handing down a verdict. Process feedback moves both people beside the work. They can look at the same evidence together.

That does not remove judgment. Children need adults who can say that an answer is wrong, a promise was broken or a skill needs practice. The deeper question is whether a judgment closes identity or opens inquiry. “You are careless” turns one spill into a character sketch. “The cup was near the edge; where could it go next time?” leaves room for responsibility and change.

There is a temptation to replace one script with another because scripts are comforting. They make parenting feel controllable. Say the correct sentence and perhaps the child will become resilient. But resilience is not installed by wording alone. It grows through manageable difficulty, competent instruction, second chances, rest, trust and the experience of discovering that a failed approach is not the same as a failed self.

So the best praise is often modest. It does not predict a destiny or guarantee that hard work pays. It notices a real choice and makes its consequence visible. It can say, in effect: this action mattered here; you may choose it again. That is less dazzling than “You’re brilliant.” It is also a form of respect. The child receives not a flattering identity to defend, but a piece of knowledge about their own work.

## REFLECT

When you praise a child, are you naming who they are or helping them see what they did?

What could you ask when neither a compliment nor a correction would teach as much as curiosity?

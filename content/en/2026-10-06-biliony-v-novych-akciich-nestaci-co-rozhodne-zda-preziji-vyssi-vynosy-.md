The sheer volume of issuance is not enough: the key is whether new shares hold their price even with higher bond yields, and whether capital flows beyond the AI champions too.

When a trillion dollars' worth of shares appears on the market, it is not just a party for bankers; it is a stress test of how many new chairs still fit into an already packed hall.

Fact taken from the source: according to the report, companies raised more than 1 trillion dollars on the equity markets, and the market was expecting a very strong quarter after an enthusiastic start to the year, highlighted by SpaceX's record IPO. The same report, however, says the mood is being spoiled by two things: overheated attention around AI and higher bond yields.

My analysis: what matters is not only how much capital companies raised, but whether the market can digest the new supply of shares without heartburn.

Strong share issuance is often read as proof of investors' appetite for risk. But there is also the other side of the coin: when companies and original owners sell a lot of shares, they are at the same time saying they consider prices attractive enough to sell.

It is like an online marketplace for bicycles. When cycling mania breaks out across town and suddenly the whole neighbourhood lists bikes at high prices, you do not see only strong demand. You also see that sellers have sensed a good moment. The primary market is similar: IPOs and secondary offerings are not just a celebration of confidence, but also a test of whether buyers can bear a larger supply.

QMA framework: liquidity may still be decent, the trend may look healthy, but valuation and market breadth decide whether it is a robust cycle or just a few stars under the spotlight. AI brings a peculiar gravity into this. If investors prefer to finance stories around data centres, chips and cloud infrastructure, the rest of the market has to offer either a cheaper price, clearer earnings, or both. And bond yields raise the bar: the more a relatively safe government bond pays, the more shares have to justify their risk.

A historical parallel is not an exact forecast, but a lesson: periods of rich issuance often look healthiest just before the market becomes more selective. Not necessarily panic. Rather a transition from an "everything goes through" regime to a "show me the numbers" regime.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It may help investment banks and exchange infrastructure, if deals are completed and new issues hold up after listing: examples are Goldman Sachs (GS), Morgan Stanley (MS), JPMorgan Chase (JPM), Nasdaq (NDAQ) or Intercontinental Exchange (ICE). Likewise alternative asset managers such as Blackstone (BX), KKR (KKR) or Apollo (APO) may benefit if an open equity window allows exits of portfolio companies.

Technology is a mixed case. Makers of chips and AI infrastructure such as NVIDIA (NVDA), Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSM), ASML (ASML), AMD (AMD) or Broadcom (AVGO) may keep benefiting if big customers maintain capital spending. But if investors start to want a faster payback on AI spending, high expectations can turn into a brake.

On the opposite side, long-duration growth companies, loss-making software, smaller biotechs and real estate companies refinancing debt tend to be sensitive. Higher yields usually press on the valuations of companies whose earnings lie far in the future, and they can also hurt bond proxies such as utilities or some REITs, for example Boston Properties (BXP) or NextEra Energy (NEE), if investors demand a higher return.

A better trader does not ask with news like this only how much was subscribed, but what the market has actually digested. A practical framework:

- Watch 10-year government yields and inflation-adjusted real yields: the higher they are, the higher the bar for stocks.

- Watch credit spreads on investment-grade and high-yield bonds: widening means risk appetite is fading.

- Watch government bond auctions, especially weak demand and large deviations from the expected yield: they can push rates higher.

- Watch IPO performance after a week and after a month, not just on day one: that is where the real absorption of supply shows.

- Watch discounts on secondary offerings: a bigger discount means buyers want higher compensation.

- Watch whether non-AI companies are raising capital too. If only trophy names work, it is not a broadly open window.

A bond yield is like a safe side job that suddenly starts paying more. When it used to offer a boring but certain reward, a person was more willing to try a risky startup. Once that safe job raises its pay, the startup has to be much more convincing. For the market this means pressure on share valuations, especially expensive and growth ones. For an ordinary wallet it may mean dearer loans, but sometimes also better interest on conservative products.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened:** A useful distinction is moving from military psychology into hospitals, emergency services and other high-responsibility workplaces: some people are not only overworked. They feel morally injured.

**Why it matters:** Rest may ease fatigue, yet leave shame, anger, betrayal or loss of trust untouched.

**What is known:** Researchers use moral injury for the aftermath of doing, failing to prevent, witnessing, or being betrayed around an act that violates deeply held values.

**What is uncertain:** Moral injury is not a formal diagnosis. Definitions and measures still vary, and much of the evidence is associative rather than proof of cause.

**What to do:** Do not diagnose yourself from a checklist. Name the event, the value involved, your actual control and what repair or acknowledgment might still be possible.

## FACTS

At the end of an impossible shift, two people can look equally tired. One needs sleep. The other cannot stop replaying the patient who did not receive a bed, the safety concern a manager dismissed, or the order that felt lawful but wrong.

The second experience may involve moral injury. The U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs describes potentially morally injurious events as acts of commission, acts of omission, witnessing, or betrayal by a trusted authority. The injury is not the event alone. It is the lasting psychological, social or spiritual struggle that follows.

Typical reactions can include guilt, shame, anger, disgust, withdrawal and difficulty forgiving oneself or others. None of those feelings proves a condition. The term was developed largely through military research, though it is now studied among health-care workers, first responders and other groups placed in no-win decisions.

## EVIDENCE

The evidence is substantial enough to take seriously and incomplete enough to resist slogans. A 2025 VA evidence review examined 282 primary studies and 29 systematic reviews published through February 2024. Across that literature, moral-injury measures were associated with PTSD, depression, anxiety, suicidal thoughts, substance use and relationship difficulties. Association does not tell us that moral injury caused every outcome, or that one treatment fits everyone.

Measurement is improving. The VA-supported Moral Injury and Distress Scale was developed to connect exposure with the distress and impaired functioning that follow, and has been studied in veterans, health-care workers and first responders. But a score is not a verdict.

A review of health-care workers during the pandemic found plausible links between moral stress, lack of social acknowledgment and exclusion. It also stressed how scarce the evidence remained. The honest conclusion is not “we have solved this,” but “we can now ask a better question than why an exhausted person cannot simply become more resilient.”

## PERSPECTIVES

The burnout lens asks whether demands exceeded resources for too long. That is often correct. Reducing hours, clarifying priorities and restoring control may help.

The personal-responsibility lens asks what the individual did or failed to do. Sometimes responsibility is real, and repair requires more than reassurance. Yet guilt can also exaggerate control: a nurse did not design the staffing system; a junior employee did not create the incentive that punished honesty.

The institutional lens asks whether leaders placed people in an impossible position, ignored warnings, or abandoned them afterward. Betrayal matters because trust is part of how people make sense of difficult duties. A wellness app cannot substitute for an honest account of what happened.

These lenses are not rivals. A person can be overworked, traumatised and morally distressed at once. Conversely, moral language can be misused to turn an ordinary disagreement into a grand wound. The aim is precision: identify the line that seemed crossed, who controlled the decision, and whether the deepest pain is fear, exhaustion, guilt, grief or betrayal.

## CONTEXT

Three neighbouring ideas are easy to confuse.

**Moral distress** often describes knowing the right course but being constrained from taking it. **Moral injury** refers to the enduring aftermath when an event collides with a person's moral framework. **PTSD** is a clinical diagnosis with defined criteria; it commonly includes intrusion, avoidance and heightened threat responses. Moral injury can accompany PTSD, but the VA notes that it can also exist without it.

That distinction changes the conversation. If the central problem is overload, recovery needs time and a saner workload. If the central problem is danger, trauma-focused care may be relevant. If the central problem is “I became someone I cannot respect” or “the institution I trusted betrayed its own rules,” then meaning, responsibility, acknowledgment and repair move closer to the centre.

It also prevents a cruel mistake. Calling every workplace pain “burnout” can quietly place the repair job on the employee: sleep better, breathe better, return stronger. Sometimes the organisation must explain a decision, change a policy, protect a whistleblower, apologise, or accept responsibility. Personal recovery and institutional accountability are different jobs. Both may be necessary.

## DEEPER

Start with paper, not a personality test. Write five questions and answer them without trying to sound reasonable:

1. **What happened?** Describe the event without judging yourself.
2. **Which value felt violated?** Honesty, care, loyalty, fairness, safety or something else?
3. **What control did I actually have then?** Separate authority, hindsight and impossible constraints.
4. **What repair is still possible?** An apology, truthful record, changed practice, boundary, testimony or act of service may matter. Some harms cannot be undone.
5. **Who can acknowledge this honestly?** Look for a clinician, chaplain, union representative, ethics lead, trusted peer or another person who will neither dismiss the event nor rush to absolve you.

This is reflection, not treatment. If distress persists, disrupts sleep or daily functioning, fuels heavy substance use, or brings thoughts of self-harm, seek qualified local help promptly. Immediate danger requires emergency or crisis support where you live.

The practical lesson is modest: do not demand that a weekend solve a wound made of meaning. Fatigue asks for rest. Fear may ask for safety. Guilt may ask for proportionate responsibility and repair. Betrayal may ask for truth and accountability. Naming the right wound does not heal it, but it can stop us prescribing the wrong medicine.

## REFLECT

- Is the hardest part of your experience the workload, the event itself, or what it made you believe about yourself or your institution?
- What responsibility is genuinely yours—and what belongs to people with more power?
- What would honest acknowledgment look like, even if complete repair is impossible?

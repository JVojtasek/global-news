For airlines in a fuel crisis, the oil price is not the only thing that matters. What decides is the combination of fuel cost, interest coverage and free cash flow after fleet investment.

In the airline business, the winner is often not the one with the nicest planes, but the one with the cheapest thirst. According to the source report, which refers to Berkshire Hathaway's regulatory filing with the US SEC, Berkshire Hathaway has built a $5.4 billion position in Delta Air Lines (DAL), right in the middle of pressure on fuel prices. The key argument: the report also refers to Delta's company materials on its own Trainer refinery, operated through Monroe Energy, which is said to give it an advantage of $0.40 per gallon of fuel, and to stronger interest coverage than at many competitors.

This is not a simple story of "Berkshire believes in airlines again". It is more a test of who in the aviation sector survives expensive jet fuel without having to panic.

Airlines are a peculiar business: the customer sees a seat, coffee and a suitcase, but the shareholder sees three invisible fellow passengers — fuel, labour and debt. When fuel gets more expensive, a weaker carrier does not just have a lower margin. It has worse interest coverage, meaning a thinner cushion between operating profit and interest on debt. And when that cushion thins, the share stops behaving like a "transport company" and starts behaving like a ticket in a lottery for balance-sheet survival.

The unusual part at Delta is the refinery. At first glance, it looks as if a café had bought its own dairy farm to get cheaper milk. A bit odd, capital-intensive, outside the core business. But in a fuel crisis, this may be exactly the kind of oddity that counts. If Delta really does have a fuel advantage of $0.40 per gallon, as the source report states with reference to Delta's company materials on the Trainer/Monroe Energy refinery, it is not just cosmetic cost-cutting. A lower fuel bill raises operating profit, operating profit improves interest coverage, and better interest coverage protects free cash flow — the money the company has left after normal operations and investment.

A historical irony: Berkshire pulled out of airlines in 2020 because the pandemic showed how quickly "cheap shares" can turn into expensive debt with wings. If, according to the report, it is now coming back through Delta, the point need not be "buy the whole sector" but "within the sector, the gap between quality and mere cyclical hope is widening". In the QMA view, it is not about one pillar but about the combination of five: profitability, balance sheet, cash flow, pricing power and capital discipline.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It helps relatively higher-quality airlines, especially Delta Air Lines (DAL), if the fuel advantage and stronger interest coverage are confirmed in results as well. In an environment like this, the market often does not just distinguish "aviation yes/no", but "who has an oxygen mask and who is already gasping for breath".

The impact on the wider group of carriers may be mixed, for example United Airlines (UAL), American Airlines (AAL) or Southwest Airlines (LUV). With them, investors would mainly compare fuel cost per gallon, the ability to pass more expensive fuel on to ticket prices and balance-sheet strength. Higher debt in a fuel storm is like an extra suitcase while sprinting for a connection.

Indirectly, it may help companies tied to the health of air traffic, for example aircraft lessors such as AerCap (AER), because financially more stable carriers cope better with repayments and leases. On the other hand, airlines without fuel protection and with thin interest coverage feel the pressure: the same rise in fuel eats into their profits and the market's trust faster.

For refiners such as Phillips 66 (PSX) or Valero Energy (VLO), the impact is rather indirect and mixed: a tight fuel market can support refining economics, but Delta is interesting precisely because it has part of this game inside its own house.

With news like this, watching the oil price is not enough. A better "cockpit" has four gauges: fuel cost per gallon versus competitors, operating profit relative to interest, free cash flow after fleet investment, and the gap between revenue per seat and non-fuel cost per seat. A full plane on its own is not enough — even a packed bus with wings can lose money if it carries passengers at the wrong price.

This is not investment advice. It is a framework for separating a higher-quality airline from a company that only looks cheap because the market has not yet priced in expensive fuel and debt.

Interest coverage is like a household with a mortgage. When a family's income is comfortably higher than its repayments, it can cope even with more expensive energy. When the budget is stretched, one unpleasant bill is enough to cause stress. With airlines, it is the same: higher interest coverage means a better chance of weathering expensive fuel without emergency financing, cuts or raising the price of services. For the market, this means bigger differences between carriers' shares; for an ordinary person, it can show up in ticket prices, fees and the availability of routes.

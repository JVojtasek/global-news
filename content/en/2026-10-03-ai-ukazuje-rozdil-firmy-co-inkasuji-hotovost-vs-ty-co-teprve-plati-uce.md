With AI, watch the gap between companies that collect the cash and companies that are still paying the bill and must prove a return — the story alone is not enough.

AI is not just a new engine for the market; it is a vacuum cleaner pulling oxygen out of corporate budgets before other sectors have a chance to breathe. The point of the news: capital and investor attention are concentrating around AI infrastructure, while outside technology earnings growth may be slower, and for weaker companies worries about credit risk may grow. So it is not only that tech stocks look stronger — AI is changing how money is distributed across the whole economy.

The most interesting thing is not AI's dominance itself, but an accounting asymmetry: for one party an AI investment is revenue, for another it is a future depreciation charge. A chipmaker sees an order as revenue growth. A company buying AI infrastructure sees capital expenditure that will, over time, flow into depreciation — a cost that will reduce reported profit unless it brings fast enough revenue growth or savings.

Here is a useful framework rather than a fairy tale about a great technology wave: watch the difference between "AI capex" and "AI payback". Capex is capital expenditure; you will find it in the cash flow statement in company results. Payback is the question of whether the additional gross profit or cost savings are enough to cover future depreciation, data centre operations and any financing. In practice: for companies, read capex / operating cash flow, depreciation / revenue, and free cash flow after investment. When capex grows faster than operating cash flow, a company can look like a growth story but run out of breath on cash.

The historical parallel is not the dot-com bubble as a whole, but telecom infrastructure at the turn of the millennium: fibre-optic networks were genuinely useful, the returns just came at a different pace than the financing optimists expected. A technology can be right and the shares of some of those paying the bill can still hurt for a while. That is the counter-intuitive lesson: a revolutionary product does not automatically mean revolutionary margins for everyone who spends on it.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The companies below are examples of impact channels, not investment recommendations or instructions to buy or sell. It helps companies selling shovels in the AI gold rush: chipmakers such as NVIDIA (NVDA), AMD (AMD) and Broadcom (AVGO), semiconductor equipment makers such as ASML (ASML) and Applied Materials (AMAT), and cloud players such as Microsoft (MSFT), Amazon (AMZN) and Alphabet (GOOGL), provided they can turn data centre investment into higher utilisation and service prices.

Utilities and data centre real estate see a mixed impact: demand for electricity and capacity can help companies such as NextEra Energy (NEE), Constellation Energy (CEG), Equinix (EQIX) or Digital Realty (DLR), but demands on the grid, permitting and financing are rising at the same time.

Under pressure may be sectors where AI does not quickly add revenue but does take budget: parts of traditional software, consulting services, media, or highly indebted companies. For financial institutions such as JPMorgan Chase (JPM), Bank of America (BAC) or Wells Fargo (WFC), this is not a direct AI story but a credit channel: if earnings slow outside technology, indicators such as interest coverage — EBIT / interest expense — and credit spreads, tracked for example in the Fed's FRED series, become more important.

A better trader facing news like this does not just ask "who is talking about AI", but "for whom does AI increase cash, and for whom only the invoice" — without deciding on a single indicator or on the story alone. Checklist: 1) capex / operating cash flow, 2) free cash flow after investment, 3) depreciation / revenue, 4) earnings revisions outside technology, 5) interest coverage and credit spreads. The risk is that the market may temporarily reward the investment story while the accounts wait patiently with the receipt.

Capital expenditure is like a café buying an expensive coffee machine. For the machine maker it is great revenue right away. For the café it is a bet: it has to sell more coffee or save staff time, otherwise it just has an expensive machine and less money in the till. In market terms: AI suppliers may have a stronger revenue tailwind, while companies that roll out AI expensively without a quick return may see weaker profits — and for indebted companies that may eventually hurt creditors too.

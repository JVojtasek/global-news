With Hormuz, watch not only the diplomatic deal, but also the return of traffic, shipping insurance, the US stance on secondary sanctions and the shape of the oil futures curve.

Oil is behaving like someone who hears a fire alarm but, instead of running, orders a coffee: according to the underlying market report for this article, traffic in the Strait of Hormuz fell by two thirds against the 10-day average, and yet oil prices kept falling. The same source gives as the reason that Iran and Oman are moving closer to a deal on safe passage and that the US is not yet getting tougher with secondary sanctions against third parties.

The paradox is not that the market is ignoring physical shipping. The paradox is that with oil, legal passability is sometimes traded more than the number of ships on the water.

Hormuz is the bottleneck of the world's oil: when it jams, it hurts not only producers, but also refineries, insurers, carriers and consumers. But the market does not only ask "will the tanker get through?". It also asks: "Will it be possible to insure it, pay for it, finance it and take delivery without someone getting their knuckles rapped by the US?"

And it is precisely US restraint on secondary sanctions, mentioned by the underlying market report on the current situation, that is the underrated detail here. Secondary sanctions are not a punishment for Iran itself, but leverage over banks, traders, shippers and buyers outside the US. It is the difference between a sign saying "the bridge may be damaged" and a sign saying "whoever drives across it will also be fined at home". If that second sign has not been put up yet, part of the geopolitical premium in the oil price may evaporate before physical traffic returns to normal.

That is why oil can fall even at a moment when the tanker reality looks ugly. Today's price does not reflect only the current queue of ships; it reflects the market's bet that the congestion will not grow into a long blockade enforced through sanctions, insurance and the payment system.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

A lower geopolitical premium generally hurts oil producers that benefit from higher barrel prices: for example Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), ConocoPhillips (COP) or European integrated companies such as Shell (SHEL) and BP (BP). But it is not a clean equation — integrated companies also have refining and trading, so the impact can be mixed depending on margins, inventories and hedging.

Refiners such as Valero Energy (VLO), Marathon Petroleum (MPC) or Phillips 66 (PSX) can benefit from cheaper oil, provided their refining margins do not fall at the same time. For them, the key is to watch the difference between the input price and the price of petrol, diesel or jet fuel — not just Brent itself.

On the side of potential winners are carriers and consumer sectors sensitive to fuel: airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL), United Airlines (UAL) or American Airlines (AAL), logistics such as FedEx (FDX) and UPS (UPS), or consumers more broadly through lower pressure on fuel prices. Tanker companies and insurers, on the other hand, have a mixed picture: less panic lowers the risk premium, but uncertainty may still keep rates and premiums higher than in a calm regime.

With news like this, watching the "deal" headline is not enough. A better framework has four points: 1) is traffic in Hormuz returning to the 10-day average, or does it remain significantly lower; 2) are insurance and shipping costs in the region rising or falling; 3) does the US maintain its restraint on secondary sanctions; 4) is only the spot oil price reacting, or the futures curve too, that is, the prices of deliveries in the coming months. If only today's price falls but longer contracts remain tight, the market is saying: "diplomacy helped, but the door is not wide open yet."

Secondary sanctions are like a school that punishes not only the child who broke the window, but also the friends who lent them the stone, the bike and the backpack for the getaway. For the market, this is a huge difference: when the US is not yet using this broader threat, banks, carriers and buyers can be less afraid of taking part in the trade. In practice this can lower the oil price and ease fuel costs for companies and drivers — but only if safe passage really turns into ships passing through regularly again.

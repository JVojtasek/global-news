In the next AI wave, watch the fashionable vocabulary less and the income statement more: margins, cash flow, earnings revisions and whether revenue grows faster than the cost of AI infrastructure.

AI is no longer just a gold rush for shovels; the audit of who is actually digging with those shovels is beginning. According to the report, Morgan Stanley analysts argue that AI hardware stocks may still have room to rise, but the centre of gravity of the story should gradually widen to sectors that are starting to genuinely benefit from AI in their operations. In other words: the market may be shifting from the question "who sells the chips?" to the question "whose margins, revenue or productivity will AI improve?"

The most interesting thing about the report is not the word "diversification". Every other investment commentary says that when it no longer wants to sound like a fan club for a single stock. The fresh angle is the shift from the AI capex phase to the AI P&L phase.

Capex means capital expenditure — companies spend on servers, chips, data centres and infrastructure. P&L is the income statement — instead of promises, it shows whether AI cuts costs, raises revenue, improves margins or speeds up profit growth. The first phase mainly helps those who sell the "shovels": chips, networking gear, cooling, servers. The second phase will start to favour companies that, thanks to AI, serve more customers without costs growing at the same rate.

This matters because the market often makes one mistake: it confuses technological adoption with economic adoption. A company saying "AI" does not yet mean it earns more. The better test is usually boring: is the gross margin rising? Are operating costs falling as a share of revenue? Are earnings-per-share estimates improving? Is revenue per employee growing? If not, AI may so far be just an expensive company gym membership — a nice perk, but the belly is still the same.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

- Makers of chips and AI infrastructure such as NVIDIA (NVDA), Advanced Micro Devices (AMD), Broadcom (AVGO), Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSM) or ASML (ASML) remain at the source of demand. For them, though, the market mainly watches the pace of orders, capacity, margins and the investment plans of large cloud customers.

- Cloud and software such as Microsoft (MSFT), Amazon (AMZN), Alphabet (GOOGL), ServiceNow (NOW), Salesforce (CRM), Adobe (ADBE) or Palantir (PLTR) may benefit if they turn AI into higher usage of services, better customer retention and measurable revenue growth. Practical indicators: cloud revenue growth, remaining performance obligations, customer retention, margins.

- Communication services and digital advertising such as Meta Platforms (META) and Alphabet (GOOGL) can use AI to improve ad targeting, recommendation systems and automated content creation. Here it pays to watch advertising revenue growth against the growth of infrastructure costs.

- Data centres and electrification such as Vertiv (VRT), Eaton (ETN) or Schneider Electric (SU.PA) may profit from higher demands for power, cooling and energy management.

- Companies that mainly buy AI but cannot translate it into prices or savings may end up with higher costs and no better margins.

- Also vulnerable are routine services and outsourcing, for example call centres or simple administrative processes, where AI increases pressure on the price of labour and contracts.

With news like this, it is not enough to watch who is the "AI winner" in a presentation. A better checklist: 1) where AI shows up in the income statement, 2) whether revenue grows faster than infrastructure costs, 3) whether analysts' earnings estimates are actually rising, 4) whether the valuation already assumes a perfect scenario, 5) whether company management cites concrete operating effects, not just fashionable vocabulary.

The QMA framework: split the AI theme into five pillars — demand, margins, cash flow, valuation, revisions to expectations. A regime change occurs when the story moves from "we are spending on AI" to "AI is improving unit economics". In the data, that shows up less in press releases and more in margins, productivity, cash flow and management outlooks.

The AI P&L phase is like a restaurant that has bought a state-of-the-art oven. The first wave made money for the oven maker. The second wave will show which restaurant, thanks to the oven, bakes more pizzas, with less waste and without hiring twice as many people. For the market, this means attention may shift from companies that sell AI equipment to companies that genuinely improve profitability thanks to it. For the ordinary wallet the impact is indirect — if AI raises productivity, over time it may put pressure on service prices and wages and affect the value of companies in pension and investment portfolios.

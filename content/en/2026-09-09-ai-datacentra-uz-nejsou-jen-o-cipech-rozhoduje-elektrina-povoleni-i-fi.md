With AI data centers, watching chips isn't enough; what decides is whether a project has secured electricity, permits, financing and a paying customer.

According to the supplied report from an interview with CNBC at the IPEM 2026 conference in Paris, Blair Jacobson, Co-President of Ares, spoke about the exponential growth in AI data center construction and the role of the private credit market. The point isn't just "AI needs more servers" — what's interesting is that the person saying it is a boss from the private credit world, the financial boiler room where it's decided who gets the fuel to build.

The biggest mistake with AI infrastructure is looking only at chips. It's like watching Formula 1 and caring exclusively about the engine while ignoring the pit stops, fuel, tires and track marshals. A data center isn't a pile of GPUs in an air-conditioned garage. It's the intersection of electricity, land, cooling, transformers, building permits, lease contracts and financing.

That's exactly why it matters that this thesis comes not just from a hardware maker, but from the co-president of Ares, a player tied to private credit — non-bank financing of companies and projects. That shifts the story from "how many chips will sell" to "how many projects are actually financeable." Exponential demand can run into a very non-exponential reality: grid connections, construction capacity and the cost of capital can't be cloned like software.

A historical parallel: at the end of the internet fever, infrastructure was also being built for a future that eventually did arrive. But between a correct long-term thesis and overheated short-term expectations there can be a canyon. Fiber optics weren't a bad idea; the problem was the pace, the debt and the price the market assigned to every kilometer of cable. With AI data centers, both things may similarly be true: the need for capacity is real, but not every project and not every valuation has to make economic sense.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It helps above all companies in the chain where demand turns into physical infrastructure. AI chip makers such as NVIDIA (NVDA), AMD (AMD) and suppliers of custom chip logic such as Broadcom (AVGO) can benefit from a continued appetite for building computing capacity. Semiconductor makers and foundry partners such as TSMC (TSM) are indirectly in the game through manufacturing capacity for cutting-edge chips.

The big winner may not just be the "shiny chip," but the boring box on the wall. Electrical and cooling infrastructure — for example Vertiv (VRT), Eaton (ETN), Schneider Electric (SU.PA) or grid-linked companies like Quanta Services (PWR) — is often the bottleneck. If more data centers get built, UPS systems, switchgear, transformers, cooling and grid connections become more important.

Data center operators and real estate infrastructure such as Equinix (EQIX) or Digital Realty (DLR) face a mixed impact. Higher demand for capacity is a plus, but new projects can be capital-intensive and sensitive to the cost of debt. Private credit players such as Ares Management (ARES) may see more financing opportunities, but also greater risk if projects are built on overly optimistic assumptions about occupancy or electricity prices.

It may hurt ordinary industrial electricity consumers in regions where data centers compete for grid capacity. Indirectly, it may push up the need for investment in transmission grids and energy sources. For utilities like Constellation Energy (CEG) or Vistra (VST) it's potentially favorable, but only if regulation, available capacity and long-term contracts make the economics add up.

A better trader doesn't count press headlines on news like this, but hard evidence. Use a simple 0-to-5 framework: one point for a secured capacity offtaker, one for allocated power, one for building and grid permits, one for clear financing and one for a concrete supply chain for cooling, electricity and chips. A project with a beautiful AI presentation but no electricity is like a luxury espresso machine in an apartment with no power socket.

The risk: "exponential construction" sounds like a party invitation, but the market often prices the dream first and only checks the bill later. With these reports, the key is to distinguish whether it's real construction that has started, signed long-term contracts and secured power — or just an ambitious plan.

Private credit is lending outside classic banks. Imagine that instead of a mortgage from a bank, a wealthy neighborhood club lends you the money to build your house — faster, more flexibly, but usually on carefully watched terms. Market impact: if this kind of financing flows into AI data centers, it can speed up construction and support suppliers of chips, electricity and cooling. But if money gets more expensive, or it turns out there are more data centers than paying customers, the same debt can turn from fuel into dead weight.

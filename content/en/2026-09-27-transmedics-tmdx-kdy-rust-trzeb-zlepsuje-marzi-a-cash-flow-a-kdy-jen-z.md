With TMDX it is not enough to watch revenue growth. The key is whether the logistics network eventually shows up in better gross margin, operating cash flow and a lower cost for each additional case served.

Imagine a transplanted organ not as a parcel on ice, but as a VIP patient with its own catering, driver and onboard service. The source material describes TransMedics (TMDX) as a company that combines perfusion technology for several organ types with its own transplant logistics; it links the recent drop in the share price mainly to fluctuating margins amid higher investment. The analytical thesis rests mainly on growth, expansion outside the US, a possible kidney product with an FDA submission in 2027, and a target of more than 2 bn USD in revenue by 2032.

The most interesting thing about TMDX is not just the medical device. It is an attempt to combine medtech, software, transport and clinical workflow into a single service. Financially that is a much more complicated animal than a classic company that sells a machine to a hospital and then bills for consumables.

The QMA view: TMDX is less like a scalpel maker and more like a company running an emergency network for biologically fragile shipments. And here is the catch: higher investment that hurts margins in the short term can be either a painful tax for building a moat, or an expensive way to buy growth that never fully turns into profit. You will not tell the difference from the headline, but from the accounts.

A concrete anchor: TMDX reported revenue of 441.5 mn USD for 2024, up from 241.6 mn USD in 2023. That is steep growth. But precisely for that reason it is important to look at gross margin too: according to the annual filings, it moved from around two thirds of revenue in 2023 to roughly the high-50s to around 60% in 2024. In other words, revenue is running ahead, but the network, logistics and service are taking a bigger bite out of every dollar.

The mechanism: if a company expands logistics, staff, its fleet, service capacity or international operations, part of the costs can flow through cost of revenue, part through selling and administrative expenses, part through research and development, and part through investing cash flow. The income statement then looks like a pub that hires a cook, buys a van and launches delivery before its Friday nights are sold out. The margin starts sweating before revenue has a chance to catch up.

The counterintuitive truth: for companies like TMDX, not every decline in margin is bad and not every rise in revenue is good. Good growth should, over time, show operating leverage, meaning each additional transplant uses the existing network more efficiently. Bad growth looks similar only until you find that every additional job needs another expensive team, another aircraft and another manual intervention.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

A plus for TransMedics (TMDX): if it is confirmed that investment in logistics increases access to transplants and, over time, improves unit economics, the market may forgive short-term swings in margins. The source material cites the platform, integrated logistics, international expansion and the kidney product as future drivers.

A plus for specialised medtech: companies in perfusion and transplantation, for example XVIVO Perfusion (XVIVO.ST), may benefit from greater attention to organ preservation technology. Not necessarily in the same commercial way: according to the source, TMDX's advantage lies precisely in combining the platform with logistics.

Mixed for hospitals: hospital operators such as HCA Healthcare (HCA) or Tenet Healthcare (THC) may benefit from a higher number of feasible transplants, but they also deal with the price of the service, reimbursement and team capacity. Not everyone near the operating theatre is automatically a winner.

A long-term question for dialysis: if the kidney OCS, after a potential FDA submission in 2027, eventually expanded the number of kidney transplants, that could be unwelcome for part of the volumes at dialysis players such as DaVita (DVA) or Fresenius Medical Care (FMS). But that is a longer scenario, not an immediate effect.

With stories like this, watch five checkpoints: 1) gross margin and cost of revenue, because that is where the cost of logistics shows fastest, 2) whether revenue growth is outpacing cost growth, 3) the trend in operating cash flow and capex, that is, whether growth is swallowing cash, 4) regulatory milestones, mainly the FDA for the kidney product, 5) whether international expansion improves network utilisation or just adds more fixed costs.

Watch one detail: TMDX does not usually give investors a simple metric such as net margin per procedure as a ready-made number on a plate. It can, however, be approximated indirectly: take the growth in OCS revenue and service revenue and compare it with cost of revenue, SG&A, capex and operating cash flow. If revenue grows faster than the supporting infrastructure, the network is starting to work better. If every new wave of growth requires a similarly large new wave of people, aircraft and operating costs, the gym membership turns into an expensive diet with no results.

Gross margin is like the difference between the price of a delivered pizza and what the ingredients, box, petrol and courier cost. When a pizzeria sells more pizzas but has to send an extra car and an extra cook for each one, revenue looks lovely, but the profit just will not appear. With TMDX the effect is similar: the share price may react sensitively not because growth is uninteresting, but because the market wants to see whether the transplant network gets cheaper per operation over time. For an ordinary person's wallet it is a simple lesson: a fast-growing company is not automatically an efficient company.

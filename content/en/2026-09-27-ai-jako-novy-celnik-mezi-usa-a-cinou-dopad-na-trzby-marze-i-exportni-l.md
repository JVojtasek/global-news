With US–China summits, the key is not to follow the rhetoric alone, but how it seeps into revenue, margins, inventories, capex and export licences in companies' next results.

The most dangerous sentence after a geopolitical summit is not "they fell out" but "they agreed to keep talking" — markets often breathe a sigh of relief precisely when the bill is only just landing on the table.

According to North Asia correspondent Stephen Engle, the summit between Donald Trump and Xi Jinping mainly extended the time for trade negotiations. The big disputes, however, remained open: Taiwan, Iran, technology and, above all, the race for artificial intelligence.

This is not a classic trade quarrel over tariffs, where a compromise can be scribbled on a napkin after midnight. It is a dispute over the "nervous system" of the future economy: chips, data centres, cloud, software, energy and military applications of AI.

The new angle is not just "who is allowed to sell chips to China". More interesting is that AI is becoming an invisible customs officer inside the income statement. In the past, a trade barrier could be spotted in the port: the container sits, the tariff is on paper. With AI chips, the barrier often shows up only as a change in product mix, a longer licensing process, a weaker gross margin or inventory waiting for approval, a configuration change or a different buyer.

A fresh detail: extending negotiations may be pleasant for markets in the short term, but unpleasant for companies in their accounts. Milder rhetoric helps valuations, but the uncertainty does not disappear — it just moves from the headlines into the income statements. And there it has a specific address:

- revenue: export restrictions can limit sales to China for chip and equipment makers,

- gross margin: substitute suppliers, logistical workarounds and smaller production runs raise unit costs,

- operating costs: legal teams, compliance and licensing processes are not free,

- capex: companies are building duplicate manufacturing and data infrastructure so as not to depend on a single geopolitical route,

- cash flow: "just in case" inventories tie up cash, even if in a press release they look like sensible caution.

An analogy: the US and China today do not look like a divorcing couple splitting the sofa and the dog. More like two neighbours who share one driveway, both buy bigger cars and both insist the other parks too close. The summit did not solve the parking. It only stopped them from immediately putting up a concrete wall.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

- Companies with a strong domestic or allied manufacturing footprint. Semiconductor equipment and chip makers such as Applied Materials (AMAT), Lam Research (LRCX), ASML (ASML), NVIDIA (NVDA) or AMD (AMD) may benefit from the long-term drive to build capacity outside risky routes — but the risk differs between them. For NVIDIA (NVDA) and AMD (AMD), the key is to watch whether restrictions change the mix of AI accelerators and data centre revenue. For ASML (ASML), it is mainly about the licensing regime for lithography machines and service restrictions. For Applied Materials (AMAT) and Lam Research (LRCX), the more sensitive factor is Chinese factories' demand for manufacturing equipment and commentary on the backlog, meaning orders waiting to be delivered.

- Suppliers of AI infrastructure outside China. Cloud players such as Microsoft (MSFT), Amazon (AMZN) and Alphabet (GOOGL) mainly watch chip availability, energy prices and data centre permitting. When geopolitics forces companies to keep computing capacity closer to customers, pressure on regional infrastructure grows.

- Defence and cybersecurity. Companies such as Lockheed Martin (LMT), Northrop Grumman (NOC), CrowdStrike (CRWD) or Palo Alto Networks (PANW) are examples of areas where the security dimension of US–China relations feeds into budget priorities.

It hurts or complicates life for:

- Companies with high exposure to China and Taiwan. Apple (AAPL), Qualcomm (QCOM), Broadcom (AVGO) or Tesla (TSLA) are examples of companies where the market watches not only demand in China, but also manufacturing dependencies and regulatory risk. For Apple (AAPL) there is a difference between the Chinese consumer and the Chinese manufacturing footprint; for Qualcomm (QCOM) the relationship with Chinese phone makers and licensing income is sensitive; for Broadcom (AVGO) it is more about the mix of chips, network infrastructure and large customers; for Tesla (TSLA), Chinese demand, local competition and production capacity all meet.

- Industry that depends on smooth trade. Boeing (BA), Caterpillar (CAT) or shipping companies such as Maersk are sensitive to world trade cycles, tariffs and the mood between the great powers.

- End customers. If supply chains are duplicated, someone pays for the duplication. Sometimes the shareholder through a lower margin, sometimes the customer through a higher price.

With news like this, a diplomatic photo and vocabulary such as "constructive talks" are not enough. A better checklist for the next earnings season: 1) how large a share of revenue is tied to China or Chinese customers, 2) whether management mentions export licences, 3) whether the product mix is shifting towards less powerful or modified versions, 4) how it explains the change in gross margin, 5) whether inventories are growing faster than revenue, 6) whether capex is going into duplicate capacity, 7) whether the company can raise prices without losing customers. The first impact usually shows in the share price, the second in management commentary and the third — the hardest — in margins and cash.

An export licence is like owning a top-of-the-range coffee machine, with a customer who has the money and wants an espresso — but between you stands the building manager, who decides whom you are allowed to make coffee for. The trade technically exists; it can just get stuck on the permit. For the market, this means a good product does not automatically mean free sales. For stocks, it is pressure on revenue, margins and inventories; for ordinary people, it may eventually show up as more expensive electronics, a slower rollout of AI services or higher company costs that feed into prices.

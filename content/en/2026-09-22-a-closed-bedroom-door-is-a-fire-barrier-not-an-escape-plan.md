## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Full-scale fire research has shown that an ordinary closed bedroom door can keep a room markedly cooler and limit the movement of smoke and toxic gases compared with an open doorway.

**What it means.** The door buys separation, not immunity. Modern home fires can become dangerous quickly, so a closed door is useful because it may preserve a more survivable space and a little more decision time.

**Risks and impact.** The slogan “close before you doze” can become hazardous if heard as “the room is safe.” A door can leak, fail or become impossible to open onto a smoke-filled corridor. A sleeping person also has to hear a working alarm.

**What can be done.** Close bedroom doors at night. Test smoke alarms, check that they can be heard with doors shut, know two routes from each room, agree on an outdoor meeting point and practise the route with everyone who lives there.

**What to watch.** During a real fire, conditions govern the route. If the door is hot or smoke blocks the primary exit, use the planned second way out. Once outside, stay outside.

## FACTS

The US Fire Administration says people may have less than two minutes to escape after a smoke alarm sounds. Its home plan therefore starts before any emergency: working alarms, two ways out of every room, an outside meeting place and practice. Its illustrated guidance adds a useful decision point. Feel the door before opening it; if it is hot or smoke is present, use the second route. Close doors behind you as you leave and never go back inside.

Fire Safety Research Institute, part of UL Research Institutes, has spent more than a decade examining how doors change conditions in residential fires. The mechanism is straightforward. A closed door interrupts the flow path that feeds a fire with oxygen and carries heat and combustion products into another room. It is not the same thing as a certified fire door, and the protection is not permanent.

The practical rule is therefore compact but conditional: close the bedroom door when sleeping, and make the rest of the plan strong enough that the closed door is one layer rather than the whole system.

## EVIDENCE

One of FSRI's clearest demonstrations used a roughly 1,200-square-foot single-storey house. Researchers lit a fire in the living room; two bedroom doors were closed and one was open. After about ten minutes, firefighters suppressed the fire and investigators compared measurements at a height of four feet.

The open bedroom was about 500°F, while the closed bedrooms were about 100°F. Carbon monoxide measured about 6,000 parts per million in the open bedroom and about 600 ppm behind the closed doors. The living room reached about 1,400°F and the hall about 750°F. These are not universal predictions for every house. They are measurements from one controlled full-scale demonstration, valuable because they reveal the direction and possible size of the door effect under those conditions.

A separate FSRI research programme on basement fires also found greater protection for occupants isolated behind closed doors. Its experiments showed a broader point about ventilation: creating a new opening without coordinated suppression can intensify burning and worsen conditions. In other words, “letting the smoke out” is not a safe household improvisation.

The evidence supports a barrier claim. It does not support a guarantee, a fixed number of extra minutes or an instruction to wait inside when a safe exit remains available.

## PERSPECTIVES

Fire-safety campaigns favour memorable language because a habit has to survive a sleepy moment. “Close before you doze” succeeds at that. The cost of a crisp slogan is that it can hide the system around it.

For a fire researcher, the door is a boundary that changes pressure, oxygen and heat transfer. For a parent, it may feel emotionally difficult to place a closed barrier between bedrooms. For a person with impaired hearing, mobility or cognition, the question is not merely whether the door is shut, but whether the alarm, assistance and exit route work in that configuration.

There is also a post-fire-image problem. A scorched corridor beside a comparatively intact closed room is visually persuasive, but a photograph cannot tell a family how its own stairs, windows, locks, alarm placement or assistance needs will behave. The useful response is not fear. It is rehearsal.

## CONTEXT

Home fire behaviour has changed as furnishings and layouts have changed. Synthetic materials can release intense heat and toxic smoke, while open plans connect rooms into larger flow paths. That makes small boundaries more consequential, even when those boundaries were never designed as specialist fire protection.

Fire services once commonly taught occupants mainly to get low and go. That remains important, but research has sharpened the message: control openings where possible, avoid moving through smoke, and do not mistake ventilation for rescue. Firefighters coordinate door control, ventilation and water application because changing one changes the fire.

A household cannot reproduce that tactical work. It can do the simpler preparation: keep exits usable, choose alternatives, practise in darkness or low visibility, and decide who helps a child, an older relative or a person with a disability.

## PEOPLE

Children need instructions they can act on without solving the fire. Teach them that an alarm means leave, that hiding is dangerous and that the family meets at one known place. An adult should be assigned to anyone who cannot self-evacuate; “someone will help” is not an assignment.

Renters may not control the building design, but they can report broken alarms or blocked exits, learn window and stair routes, and avoid adding locks that are hard to operate under stress. People who use hearing aids or remove them at night should test the alerting arrangement in the actual sleeping setup, not assume a hallway alarm will be enough.

## DEEPER

The deeper safety lesson is that resilience often looks unimpressive. A shut door, an alarm test and a meeting point do not feel like emergency technology. Their value appears when one layer fails and another still works.

Try a ten-minute household audit tonight:

1. Close each bedroom door as it would be during sleep and test whether every sleeper receives the alarm signal.
2. Point to the main exit and a second route from each bedroom. Make sure keys, latches and windows can be operated by the people expected to use them.
3. Name one outdoor meeting place far enough from the building for firefighters to work.
4. Assign help explicitly for children, visitors, pets and anyone with mobility, sensory or cognitive needs. Human life comes first; no one re-enters.
5. Practise once without theatrical smoke or panic. Time is less important than noticing the stuck window, cluttered hall or instruction nobody understood.

If a fire occurs, leave by the safe planned route, close doors behind you if doing so does not delay escape, call emergency services from outside and stay out. If heat or smoke blocks the door, keep it closed, use the second exit if possible and call or signal for help. The door is buying separation while the plan creates a way forward.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Cameroonian social forester Cécile Bibiane Ndjebet built a career around a deceptively simple obstacle: women could work land and depend on forests without having secure power over either.

**What it means.** Her projects connect agroforestry and mangrove restoration with land access, local negotiation, training and women's influence over decisions. The social arrangement is part of the environmental technology.

**Risks and impact.** Awards and large planting numbers can obscure harder questions: who controls the land, who receives the harvest, how many trees survive and whether informal agreements remain secure.

**What can be done.** Judge restoration by rights, maintenance and durable benefits as well as hectares and seedlings.

**What to watch.** The strongest evidence will be long-term tree survival, legally and socially secure tenure, transparent benefit-sharing and independent reporting across more communities.

## FACTS

Cécile Ndjebet grew up near Edéa in Cameroon, watching rural women farm, gather forest products and carry much of the work that sustained their households. Yet labour did not necessarily produce ownership or a voice in forest policy. She made that mismatch the centre of her work.

Ndjebet founded Cameroon Ecology around the beginning of this century and, in 2009, co-founded the African Women's Network for Community Management of Forests, known as REFACOF. The network now spans 20 African countries. Her institutions work on forest governance, restoration and women's rights to land and natural resources.

The recognition is substantial. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization announced her as the 2022 Wangari Maathai Forest Champion. Later that year, the UN Environment Programme named her a Champion of the Earth for Inspiration and Action. In 2023 she shared the Gulbenkian Prize for Humanity with two other ecosystem-restoration leaders.

Those honours are not the result to measure. They are signals that an approach once treated as a side issue — putting women inside decisions about land and forests — has moved closer to the centre of restoration policy.

## EVIDENCE

The clearest recent account comes with an important label: a July 2025 Reuters article was written by Ndjebet herself, not reported independently by Reuters News. In it, she described a community process called Lilagle, through which husbands and fathers can formally pass land to wives and daughters in a public ceremony. She reported that the initiative had secured 100 hectares for 35 women and girls, expanded from six to 18 villages, engaged 256 women, and established nurseries holding tens of thousands of fruit and mangrove seedlings.

Those are useful implementation figures, but they remain organizational claims. They tell us about land allocated, people reached and seedlings planted. They do not yet establish how many trees will survive, how household income will change, or whether every transfer will withstand future disputes. Good news becomes more credible when it keeps its measurement gaps visible.

Other institutions corroborate the longer arc. UNEP reported in 2022 that work under Ndjebet's leadership had restored more than 600 hectares of degraded land and mangrove forest. The Gulbenkian Foundation put the figure at 650 hectares in 2023. FAO documented three decades of advocacy, training and community restoration. The sequence is consistent; the precise impact still deserves continuing, preferably independent, measurement.

## PERSPECTIVES

One reading is ecological. Agroforestry places trees and crops in the same working landscape. It can protect soil, diversify production and make restoration compatible with feeding a household. It is less photogenic than a mass planting day because it is an ongoing management practice, not a single event.

Another reading is economic. A tree that produces fruit can make maintenance valuable to the person caring for it. But an asset helps only if that person can keep the harvest and has reasonable confidence that the land will not be taken away. Tenure is therefore not paperwork added after restoration; it shapes whether long-term care is rational.

The political reading is harder. A public ceremony may change a community's expectations, yet customary recognition and formal legal protection are not always the same thing. Working with chiefs and heads of families can unlock land access. It can also leave progress dependent on the people who already hold authority. The test is whether women gain an enduring right to decide, not merely permission for one project cycle.

## CONTEXT

Tree campaigns often report what is easiest to count: seedlings distributed, holes dug or hectares pledged. Forests operate on a different clock. Young trees need water, protection, replacement and years of tending. Restoration may also require natural regeneration rather than planting, different species in different places, and rules that resolve competing uses of land.

Rights determine much of that work. In a 2015 interview with the World Resources Institute, Ndjebet argued that secure access can improve women's ability to obtain credit and technical help, reduce dependence on marriage and increase their influence over management. She also stressed that representation is not enough if women attend meetings without affecting decisions.

That distinction links governance to biology. A seedling's survival depends on rainfall and soil, but also on who may fence it, prune it, harvest from it and object when the land is reassigned. A restoration plan that maps species but not power has left out one of its growing conditions.

## PEOPLE

Ndjebet's biography gives the story a face, but the work is deliberately plural. It depends on women running nurseries and orchards, families negotiating transfers, local leaders recognizing agreements, trainers sharing knowledge and networks carrying lessons between countries.

The women involved are not simply beneficiaries of a climate project. They are farmers and forest users with knowledge, labour and competing needs. Some may prioritize fruit, income or secure inheritance; others may value mangrove protection or fuelwood. Treating those preferences as data is more respectful — and more practical — than assuming one environmental target speaks for everyone.

## DEEPER

The hopeful part of this story is not that planting trees solves inequality. It is that a restoration project can be redesigned once inequality is recognized as a cause of failure rather than an unrelated moral concern.

For any local greening claim, use five questions. **Control:** who can decide what happens on the land? **Benefit:** who receives food, income or other value? **Maintenance:** who is resourced to care for the site after launch day? **Evidence:** are survival and social outcomes measured, not only planting? **Durability:** will the arrangement survive a leadership change, family dispute or funding gap?

These questions do not diminish tree planting. They protect it from becoming theatre. Ndjebet's contribution was to notice that the hands doing restoration need more than seedlings. They need standing — in the household, at the meeting and on the land.

## REFLECT

Think of a project in your town or workplace that depends on people caring for a shared asset. Who is asked to do the work, who can make decisions, and who keeps the benefit? If those answers differ, what small change would make long-term care more likely?

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** A widely repeated comparison says the share of Americans with no close friends rose from 3% in 1990 to 12% in 2021. Later national surveys reported 8% and, under a broader friendship measure, roughly 2% to 3%.

**What it means.** There is credible evidence that friendship has become thinner for some people, but no single percentage settles whether the country is experiencing one uniform “friendship recession.”

**Risks and impact.** A dramatic trend line can turn survey differences into social history. The opposite mistake is to use a reassuring survey to dismiss people who lack closeness or support.

**What can be done.** Treat friend count as one indicator. Also ask whom you can call, how often you meet, whether support is reciprocal and whether the relationship feels close enough.

**What to watch.** Future surveys become more informative when they repeat the same wording, mode and sampling over time and separate close friends, casual friends, relatives, contact and satisfaction.

## FACTS

The famous number came from a real comparison. In Gallup's 1990 telephone survey, 3% of American adults said they had no close friends. In the Survey Center on American Life's online May 2021 American Perspectives Survey, 12% did. Over the same comparison, the share reporting ten or more close friends fell from 33% to 13%, while the share with three or fewer rose from 27% to 49%.

That looks like a clean three-decade collapse. It is not quite a clean experiment.

The Survey Center notes that both studies asked respondents, excluding relatives, how many close friends they had and left “close” undefined. But the 1990 study used telephone interviews with a live person and the 2021 study was online. Someone may be more willing to admit friendlessness to a screen than to an interviewer. The 2021 survey also arrived after a year of pandemic disruption: 47% said they had lost touch with at least a few friends in the previous 12 months, while 46% said they had made a new friend.

The number described a serious signal at a peculiar moment. It did not become false because of that. It became conditional.

## EVIDENCE

Two later projects complicate the story.

Pew Research Center surveyed 5,057 US adults in July 2023 through its nationally recruited American Trends Panel. Eight percent reported no close friends, 53% reported one to four and 38% reported five or more. Among people with at least one close friend, 72% said they were completely or very satisfied with the quality of those friendships.

The peer-reviewed American Friendship Project went in a different direction. Its national adult samples in 2022 and 2023 asked participants to list people they considered friends and allowed their own understanding of friendship, including relatives. More than 92% listed at least one friend. When the researchers followed up with people reporting none, some named people with whom they had regular pleasant interactions. They estimated true friendlessness between 2% and 3%.

That project also found tension inside the reassuring totals. More than 75% were satisfied with the number of friends they had, yet more than 40% wanted greater closeness. A person can possess a network and still feel that its emotional distance is wrong.

The authors are careful about their own limits. Their online samples cannot be called representative of every American, friendship was self-reported, reciprocity was not verified, and allowing family members broadens the category compared with surveys that exclude relatives.

## PERSPECTIVES

The recession case is strongest when it avoids pretending that 12% is the only relevant fact. The 2021 survey also found fewer people with a best friend than in 1990 and fewer turning first to a friend with a personal problem. Those measures point toward a changed role for friendship, not merely a disputed head count.

The sceptical case is strongest when it asks whether the ruler moved. Telephone versus online disclosure, exclusion versus inclusion of relatives, one numerical estimate versus listing names, and pandemic timing can all change the result. A trend should not be built by joining measurements that mean different things and drawing a confident arrow through them.

Public-health language adds another layer. The US Surgeon General's 2023 advisory treated loneliness and isolation as consequential health concerns and stressed that connection includes individuals, relationships and communities. That is broader than the number of close friends. Someone may have three friends and little dependable contact; someone else may have one friend, strong family ties and a community that notices their absence.

The careful conclusion is therefore asymmetric. The evidence is sufficient to take social disconnection seriously. It is not sufficient to claim that every American group has followed one measured decline of the same size for the same reason.

## CONTEXT

Why do these numbers feel like verdicts? Friendship has few official milestones. Marriages, births and jobs leave records. Friendships can deepen, fade and return without a certificate or a final conversation. Surveys become a kind of public memory for relationships that institutions rarely count.

That memory depends on definitions. “Friend” may mean the person who receives a midnight call, a colleague who makes Tuesday bearable, a cousin who is also a confidant or someone seen twice a year with immediate warmth. “Close friend” narrows the circle but does not remove judgment.

The 1990-to-2021 comparison also spans changes that are difficult to isolate: later marriage, geographic mobility, longer or less predictable work, intensive parenting, digital communication and a pandemic. The Survey Center proposed several of these as possible forces. Its data could not assign each one a causal share.

Technology is especially easy to blame because it is visible. A message thread can maintain a distant friendship, interrupt a local conversation or do both before lunch. Counting online contact without asking what it does is no more adequate than counting chairs without asking who would sit down.

## DEEPER

A better personal audit uses four dimensions instead of one dramatic number.

**Availability:** Is there someone you can contact for practical or emotional help? Do they have a realistic way to contact you?

**Contact:** How often do you interact in a form that suits the relationship—face to face, by phone, in messages or through a shared activity? Frequency is not intimacy, but relationships without occasions rarely deepen by accident.

**Reciprocity:** Does care travel in both directions over time? Perfect balance in every week is unrealistic. A pattern in which only one person initiates, listens or repairs is different.

**Closeness:** Does the relationship feel close enough for what you need and what both people want? The American Friendship Project's most useful finding may be that satisfaction with the number of friends can coexist with wanting more closeness.

Pick one relationship rather than “fixing your social life.” Name the next repeatable occasion: a monthly breakfast, a walk after a regular appointment, a call on the first Sunday. Specific recurrence removes the burden of inventing contact each time.

Then make one modest bid for greater depth. Mention something real, ask a question that cannot be answered from a public profile, or offer concrete help. Notice the response over several exchanges. Closeness is built through mutual risk and reliable return, not extracted by a perfect opening line.

If you have no dependable contact, the problem deserves more than a productivity trick. Community organisations, faith groups, volunteering, support groups and recurring classes can provide repeated proximity—the raw material from which friendship sometimes grows. They cannot guarantee chemistry, but they improve the odds of a second meeting.

## REFLECT

The phrase “friendship recession” is valuable when it directs attention toward neglected relationships and the structures that squeeze them. It becomes less useful when a disputed national percentage turns into a diagnosis of every person.

The revealing question is not only “How many friends do I have?” It is: “Who would notice if I disappeared from my routine—and whose absence would I notice?”

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Nepali ophthalmologist Sanduk Ruit helped turn cataract treatment from a scarce urban procedure into a system that could work in lower-resource and remote settings.

**What it means.** The central innovation was not speed alone; it joined affordable lenses, a less equipment-dependent technique, trained local teams, outreach and quality measurement.

**Risks and impact.** Cataract remains a leading cause of blindness, and the World Health Organization says roughly half of people who need cataract surgery still cannot access it.

**What can be done.** When judging a celebrated humanitarian project, look beyond the founder and ask whether it builds local skills, measures outcomes and survives ordinary constraints.

**What to watch.** Coverage matters, but so do surgical quality, follow-up and whether women and rural patients can reach care on equal terms.

## FACTS

Sanduk Ruit returned to Nepal with an awkward proposition. Modern cataract surgery used implanted artificial lenses, but the price, equipment and hospital model put it beyond many people who needed it. His answer was not to accept a second-class operation for poor patients. It was to redesign the delivery system around the same goal: reliable sight-restoring care.

Ruit helped establish the Tilganga Institute of Ophthalmology in Kathmandu and co-founded what is now the Cure Blindness Project. The institute developed local production of intraocular lenses, trained eye-care workers and sent surgical teams to communities far from specialist hospitals. The operation travelled because the knowledge and supplies travelled with it.

The need remains enormous. The World Health Organization reported in February 2026 that at least 2.2 billion people have near or distance vision impairment and that at least 1 billion cases could have been prevented or remain unaddressed. Cataract and uncorrected refractive error are the leading causes. Globally, WHO says, about one in two people needing cataract surgery cannot access it.

## EVIDENCE

Good intentions are not a clinical outcome. Ruit and colleagues published results from their approach, allowing others to examine it rather than rely on photographs of removed bandages.

A 1999 study reported outcomes from low-cost, high-volume extracapsular cataract surgery with posterior-chamber lenses at Tilganga and at a rural eye camp. At two months, 87.1 percent of the Tilganga patients had best-corrected visual acuity of 20/60 or better; results at the eye camp were lower, though most patients improved and recorded complications were limited. The difference matters. Taking care into the field solves distance, but it does not magically erase the difficulties of selection, follow-up and correction.

A later randomized trial, published in 2007, compared phacoemulsification—the equipment-intensive method common in wealthy health systems—with manual small-incision cataract surgery. Both produced excellent outcomes with low complication rates in the study; the manual method was faster, less expensive and less dependent on technology. That evidence supported a method suited to advanced cataracts in settings where costly machines and maintenance cannot be assumed.

No two studies settle every question. Techniques, patient selection, training and follow-up change. The papers show that an affordable model can meet serious quality tests; they do not mean that surgery is interchangeable across every patient or setting.

## PERSPECTIVES

The heroic version of this story focuses on Ruit's hands. It is understandable: surgery is visible, and a founder gives a sprawling system a human face. Yet hero stories can hide the very thing worth copying. A surgeon can treat only the person on the table. A system can train the next surgeon, manufacture the next lens and find the next patient.

The economic version focuses on price. Affordable lenses and a manual technique changed what clinics could do with limited budgets. But cheap care is not automatically equitable care. Travel costs, lost wages, information, fear and household power can still keep a patient away. WHO's 2026 figures show particularly large access gaps in lower-income regions and persistent disadvantage for women.

The public-health version therefore asks a broader question: how much effective coverage exists? That means not merely an operation counted, but an operation that reaches the right person and produces a useful result. It is a less cinematic metric and a more honest one.

## CONTEXT

A cataract clouds the eye's natural lens. Surgery removes that clouded lens and replaces its focusing function with an artificial one. In high-income hospitals, the surrounding infrastructure—sterilisation, biometry, supply chains, trained staff, maintenance and follow-up—can become almost invisible. Move the procedure to a remote district and every invisible dependency steps into view.

Tilganga's model addressed several dependencies together. Local lens production reduced reliance on expensive imports. Manual small-incision surgery reduced dependence on phacoemulsification machines. Training spread skill. Outreach brought screening and surgery nearer to communities. A permanent institution provided a base for standards, teaching and referral.

That combination is why “frugal innovation” is too small a phrase. Removing cost from one component would not have been enough. The design worked on the chain.

## PEOPLE

Ruit's biography belongs in the story because he made consequential choices: learn a modern technique, challenge the assumption that it required a wealthy setting, publish results and build institutions. But the work also depends on nurses, technicians, lens workers, drivers, local organisers, trainees and patients willing to trust a visiting team.

The moral centre is not the rescuer. It is the person who gains the practical ability to recognise faces, work, read, cook or move independently. Restored sight can transform a household, but outcomes differ and no article can tell an individual whether surgery is appropriate. That decision belongs with qualified eye-care professionals who can examine the patient.

## DEEPER

The practical lesson travels beyond medicine. When a solution succeeds in a well-equipped centre, ask what it secretly relies on. Then separate the essential function from the expensive habit surrounding it. Which inputs can be produced locally? Which skills can be taught? Which quality measures must never be relaxed? Which last-mile barrier decides who actually benefits?

Use four tests when reading about a “world-changing” founder. **Evidence:** are outcomes published or independently measured? **Transfer:** can other trained people do the work? **Institution:** is there a durable organisation behind the personality? **Equity:** are the hardest-to-reach people actually reached?

Ruit's work passes attention from the operating theatre to the supply room, classroom and road. That is the quieter good news. A remarkable pair of hands mattered most when it helped create thousands more capable hands—and a system in which they had something affordable and effective to use.

For SMRs, watch mainly the transition from ambition to licensing, the final investment decision, concrete orders in suppliers' backlogs, and the grid connection agreement.

The nuclear industry is trying a trick every parent knows from the kids' room: when the big construction set is too expensive and too slow, break it into smaller blocks. France's EDF wants to build 10 small modular reactors — SMRs — in Europe by 2035, and its Italian unit Edison is to be part of the project. The value of the plan could run into tens of billions of euros, but the key word is "plans" — not "builds".

The most interesting thing about this news isn't "small nuclear is back". The market has heard that many times. What's interesting is that EDF is in effect testing whether nuclear energy can transform from a Renaissance cathedral into serial production.

Specifically, this isn't about an anonymous "small reactor", but about a European SMR line around the NUWARD project. It is a pressurized water concept — related to the technology regulators know from classic nuclear plants. An important detail: this is not a finished product with a European license. In 2024, EDF was reworking its NUWARD project toward a simpler design based on proven pressurized-water elements, precisely to raise the chances of passing regulation and achieving industrial manufacturability.

Big nuclear blocks are like building a new custom opera house every time: beautiful, powerful, but every detail hurts the budget. SMRs promise the opposite — factory-produced parts, a repeatable design, shorter construction. But nuclear reality has one catch: even if you buy a kitchen from IKEA, the health inspector won't approve it just because the Swedish screw looks elegant. In nuclear, what matters is the license, safety documentation, the supply chain, financing, and political patience.

And here is a concrete bottleneck: NUWARD has only gone through early regulatory cooperation, not final construction approval. In Europe, it would have to meet with national regulators — in France with ASNR, in Finland with STUK, in the Czech Republic with SÚJB, and if Italy is to play a role, also with its local framework and the ISIN supervisor. On top of that, Italy has no operating nuclear plants, so the problem isn't just the reactor, but also rebuilding rules, institutions, and political mandate. It's like wanting to open a sushi bar in a city whose health code doesn't yet know raw fish.

A historical brake on optimism: the American NuScale project with UAMPS was terminated in 2023 after estimated costs rose and electricity off-takers showed insufficient interest. That isn't proof that SMRs don't work. It's a reminder that "small reactor" doesn't automatically mean "small risk" or "small bill".

QMA view: this news is less about electricity in 2035 and more about the capital cycle today. If a European nuclear ordering wave gets going, the first money often doesn't flow to electricity producers, but to firms that draw up projects, certify equipment, make components, build concrete boxes, and connect them to the grid. Nuclear is energy dressed up as an industrial megaproject.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The plus side is broad, but uneven. It potentially helps nuclear engineering and project services, for example firms like Assystem (ASY.PA), which operate in technical consulting for the energy sector, or certification and inspection players such as Bureau Veritas (BVI.PA). The mechanism is simple: before the first concrete is poured, there are years of calculating, testing, approving, and documenting.

But here's the difference between a "nice thematic story" and actual exposure. With Assystem, it makes sense to watch whether nuclear contracts show up in orders and backlog — i.e. work contracted for the future. With Bureau Veritas, it's not that the company would "manufacture SMRs", but that it may have a role in inspections, supplier qualification, industrial checks, and safety documentation. These are services that arrive earlier than electricity generation itself.

Another group is suppliers of electrical equipment, grid control, and energy infrastructure: Schneider Electric (SU.PA), Siemens Energy (ENR.DE), potentially GE Vernova (GEV). SMRs aren't just a reactor in a box; they need transformers, turbines, control systems, protections, and a grid connection. But for these companies, SMRs are usually just a small piece of a much larger puzzle. Practically, what matters is whether the plan translates into concrete orders in the grid, turbine, automation, or service-contract segments — not just big words in an energy presentation.

Construction and industrial firms like Vinci (DG.PA), Bouygues (EN.PA), or ArcelorMittal (MT) could benefit from a long investment cycle, if projects move from presentations to orders. For Vinci and Bouygues, this would mainly mean construction, engineering, and project work; for ArcelorMittal, more a broader demand for steel and specialty materials, rather than a pure nuclear bet. The uranium side of the chain can indirectly track players like Cameco (CCJ), but careful here: ten SMRs by 2035 is not, by itself, an immediate shock to uranium demand.

On the minus side, over the long term, could be gas-fired power plants and fossil-fuel suppliers, if SMRs one day displace some gas-based generation. But practically, this isn't a story for next winter. By 2035, gas prices, politics, regulation, and battery technology may all have changed.

With news like this, the biggest trap is confusing "ambition" with "an order". A better checklist: 1) is there a final investment decision — a commitment to put capital into the project? 2) does the reactor have an approved design from the regulator in a specific country? 3) has the project moved from early consultation to a formal application for a permit? 4) are off-takers for the electricity known, or is there a state price guarantee? 5) do contracts show up in suppliers' reports, specifically in orders and backlog, not just press-release sentences? 6) is there a grid connection agreement and a prepared site?

For the market, timing matters most. Supplier shares may react to expectations before plants produce a single megawatt-hour, but disappointment comes the moment the modular fairytale turns into a classic European paperwork battle.

Picture SMRs as a small bakery instead of a giant bakery factory. In theory, you build it faster, you can have more of them, and place them closer to where there's hunger for energy. But you're still baking "nuclear bread" — the state wants to know whether the oven won't explode, whether the staff is trained, and who will pay the bill if construction drags on. For the market, this means: the story can help firms around infrastructure and engineering, but for the ordinary person's wallet, cheaper electricity won't appear just because someone announced a plan for 2035.

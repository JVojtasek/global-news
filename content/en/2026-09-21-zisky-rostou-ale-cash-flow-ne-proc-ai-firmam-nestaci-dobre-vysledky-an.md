It is not enough that AI firms are seeing profits rise; the key risk is whether EBITDA growth actually converts into free cash flow, or whether capex and working capital eat it up.

When the market expects Olympic gold from companies, silver suddenly looks like a disaster.

### What happened — just the essentials in 2–3 sentences

According to a commentary by Liz Ann Sonders of Charles Schwab, an aggregate "repricing of earnings" has taken place: investors are no longer valuing just the AI story, but above all sharply higher expected profits. That lifts the bar for the next earnings season so high that even solid numbers can trigger sharp moves if they are not "wow" enough.

### A fresh angle — added insight

The biggest trap is not that the AI story has to be false. The trap is that it can be true — and the stock can still disappoint in the short term.

This is not just about the familiar "AI capex versus cash flow" theme. The subtler problem is the cash lag: the market often prices in future earnings right away, while the firm has to spend the money on servers, chips, inventories and capacity today. The result in accounting is an optical illusion: the income statement smiles while the cash-flow statement wheezes up the hill.

A mini-example: a firm had revenue of 100, an EBITDA margin of 30%, so operating profit before depreciation of 30. In the past it spent 10 on investment, working capital absorbed 2, and taxes and interest 5. Free cash flow was 13. Now, thanks to AI demand, revenue rises to 120 and the EBITDA margin stays at 30%, so EBITDA is a lovely 36. But capital expenditure jumps to 25, working capital to 6, and taxes and interest to 6. Free cash flow is 36 − 25 − 6 − 6 = minus 1.

That is an important difference from an ordinary growth story: the problem is not demand, but the conversion of profit into cash. If EBITDA grows by 20% but free cash flow falls from 13 to minus 1, the FCF/EBITDA ratio drops from roughly 43% to a negative number. That is no longer cosmetics; it is the financial version of a restaurant with a full dining room and an empty till, because it pre-bought new tables, a kitchen, and half a warehouse of avocados.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It helps firms with direct traction on AI budgets that can also hold margins: chipmakers such as NVIDIA (NVDA) and Broadcom (AVGO), servers and infrastructure such as Super Micro Computer (SMCI), and networking kit at Arista Networks (ANET). For them the market focuses mainly on the pace of orders, production capacity, and whether margins hold up under cost pressure.

A mixed impact lands on hyperscalers such as Microsoft (MSFT), Alphabet (GOOGL), Amazon (AMZN), and Meta Platforms (META). AI can raise their future revenue, but today it often lifts capex — capital spending on data centres, chips, and energy. The difference is whether capex/revenue — investment as a share of sales — grows faster than the future monetisation of AI services.

More sensitive are distributors and integrators such as ScanSource (SCSC), CDW (CDW), and Arrow Electronics (ARW). There margins tend to be lower and working capital more important: more inventory, longer receivables, and price pressure can eat the effect of higher revenue. In practice it is about days of inventory and receivables — when goods pile up faster than invoices turn into cash, revenue growth can feel like running with a backpack of bricks.

Indirect winners can be energy and data-centre infrastructure, for example Constellation Energy (CEG), Vistra (VST), and Eaton (ETN). But here, too, the type of business matters: regulated grids earn through the rate base and the authorised return, merchant generation through market prices, and industrial suppliers through orders, margins, and capacity.

With stories like this, the most important question is not "are profits rising?" but "how much profit turns into cash, relative to what the share price has already priced in?" More useful than a general checklist is a three-point filter: 1) FCF conversion = free cash flow / EBITDA; a problem arises when it falls for several quarters in a row even while EBITDA rises, 2) capex intensity = capex / revenue; the risky situation is when investment grows faster than revenue without a clear improvement in margins, 3) working-capital days; the warning light blinks when inventories and receivables swell faster than revenue. Put differently: a good quarter is not just higher profit, but profit that also reaches the company's wallet.

"Free cash flow" is like the money left over after your paycheck, rent, food, and the necessary car repair. You can have a higher salary, but if the washing machine breaks at the same time, the rent goes up, and you have to buy a new work computer, less may be left in the account than before. For the market, that means a stock can fall even after nice profits, if the firm burns too much cash chasing growth. For an ordinary person it filters through indirectly: tech stocks are a large part of indexes and funds, so their swings can also move pension and investment accounts.

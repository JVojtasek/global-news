## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Psychologists have spent over two decades testing how accurately people predict their own future emotions, and the results are unusually consistent.

**What it means.** People overestimate how intensely and how long a future event — good or bad — will affect their mood. Researchers call this the impact bias.

**Risks and impact.** The bias shapes real decisions: job changes, purchases, relationships, even lawsuits, are often made on inflated emotional predictions that don't match how people actually feel weeks later.

**What can be done.** Ask someone who already lived through a similar event how long the feeling lasted, instead of trusting your own imagined version of the future.

**What to watch.** Whether you're deciding based on an imagined peak moment (the wedding day, the win, the win itself) rather than the months of ordinary life that follow it.

## FACTS

Affective forecasting is the term psychologists use for predicting how a future event will make us feel. Researchers, most prominently Daniel Gilbert at Harvard and Timothy Wilson at the University of Virginia, spent much of the 1990s and 2000s running studies asking people to predict their emotional reaction to events — winning an award, failing a test, ending a relationship, being denied tenure — and then checking back afterward to see how they actually felt.

The pattern that kept appearing has a name: impact bias. People consistently overestimate two things at once — how intense the emotional reaction will be, and how long it will last. This holds for both good and bad events. A pay rise is expected to bring more lasting satisfaction than it does. A painful breakup is expected to hurt longer than it typically does. The direction of the prediction (happy or sad) is usually correct; the size and duration are usually wrong.

A related, older idea helps explain why: the hedonic treadmill, a concept developed by psychologists Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell in 1971. It describes how people tend to return to a fairly stable baseline level of well-being after both positive and negative events, once the initial emotional spike fades. Good and bad fortune move the needle briefly, then it drifts back.

## EVIDENCE

The finding that people mispredict the *duration* of emotional reactions is well replicated across many independent research groups, not just Gilbert and Wilson's own lab. Studies on lottery winners, tenure decisions, election outcomes and romantic breakups have found the same shape of result: real reactions fade faster than predicted ones.

Where the evidence is less settled is on *why* the bias happens. Gilbert and Wilson's own leading explanation is "immune neglect" — the idea that people underestimate their own psychological coping mechanisms, the everyday rationalizing, reframing and distraction that soften a blow faster than expected. Other researchers point to "focalism," the tendency to focus narrowly on the event itself while ignoring how much of daily life will carry on unaffected around it — the rest of a normal day still has to happen even after very good or very bad news.

Both explanations are supported by follow-up experiments, and they are not mutually exclusive; most researchers in the field now treat them as complementary rather than competing. What is not well established is how much the size of the bias varies by personality, culture or the specific type of event — the original studies leaned heavily on US university populations, and cross-cultural replication is thinner.

## PERSPECTIVES

One reading, common in decision-science writing, treats the impact bias mainly as a *practical* problem: a measurable error that costs people money and time, correctable with tools like structured comparison or advice from people with direct experience. This framing is useful for concrete choices — job offers, purchases, moves — but it says less about why the miscalibration exists in the first place.

A second reading, closer to Gilbert and Wilson's own work, treats the bias as a byproduct of something adaptive: an internal coping system that works better than the mind gives itself credit for. On this view, the "error" is really underestimating your own resilience, which is a kinder problem to have than the alternative.

A third, more skeptical perspective inside psychology itself questions how universal the effect is outside curated lab and survey conditions, and whether people, once told about the bias directly, can correct for it in the moment it matters — the early evidence on "debiasing" is mixed. None of these views contradicts the core finding; they disagree about what to do with it.

## CONTEXT

The research sits inside a broader field called hedonic psychology, which studies what actually makes people feel good rather than what they assume will. It grew partly out of economists' and psychologists' shared interest in the gap between predicted utility (what we think will satisfy us) and experienced utility (what actually does), a distinction the economist Daniel Kahneman helped popularize in work that fed into his 2002 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences.

What the research does not claim is that emotional predictions are useless or that big life events don't matter — a serious illness, a bereavement or a genuine achievement still moves people, sometimes for a long time. The finding is narrower and more specific: the *size and duration* of the swing is usually overestimated, in both directions, more reliably than the *direction* of the swing is wrong.

## DEEPER

Underneath the research is a fairly plain question: if people are this bad at predicting what will make them happy, what are they actually chasing when they make a big decision?

Part of the answer is that the mind treats an imagined future as a single vivid moment — the graduation stage, the empty apartment after a breakup, the promotion announcement — rather than as thousands of ordinary Tuesdays that follow it. The peak gets all the emotional weight in the forecast; the long stretch of daily life afterward, where most of actual life happens, barely registers as a variable at all.

That is not really a flaw unique to big decisions. It is a narrower version of something most people already sense about memory and anticipation generally: the mind is a better editor than a predictor. It compresses a decade into a highlight reel when looking back, and it does something similar in reverse when looking forward — imagining a single scene and mistaking it for the whole future.

## REFLECT

When did you last make a significant decision mainly because of how you imagined it would feel afterward?

Looking back at a past prediction like that — a job, a move, a relationship — how did the actual feeling compare to the one you expected, in both size and how long it lasted?

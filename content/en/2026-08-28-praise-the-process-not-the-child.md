## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Decades of research have compared praise aimed at a child's identity with praise aimed at effort, strategies and choices.

**What it means.** Praise is most useful when it tells a child what worked and leaves room for learning. Labels such as “smart” may feel warm, but they can make the next mistake feel like evidence against the label.

**Risks and impact.** The evidence does not support replacing every compliment with “good effort.” Empty or inaccurate effort praise can sound false, especially when a child knows the work was careless.

**What can be done.** Describe the specific process you saw: the question asked, the plan changed, the careful check or the return after frustration. When the result is poor, pair encouragement with a useful next move.

**What to watch.** Notice what happens after difficulty. Does the child protect an image, or stay curious enough to try another strategy?

## FACTS

A child brings home a difficult drawing, a repaired bicycle chain or a page of sums. The adult wants to encourage them. “You're so clever” arrives quickly because it sounds generous and costs nothing.

The research question is not whether children should receive warmth. It is what information praise carries. In a well-known series of experiments, Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck found that fifth-graders praised for intelligence after success were more likely than children praised for effort to favour performance goals and to react poorly after later failure. The study did not prove that one sentence permanently shapes a child. It showed that the framing of success can alter the meaning of the next setback.

Later observational work followed families in ordinary homes. Researchers distinguished process praise — comments about effort, strategies or actions — from person praise, which labels the child. A higher proportion of process praise heard between ages one and three predicted more learning-oriented beliefs several years later. Follow-up work reported associations with academic achievement in fourth grade, partly through children's motivational frameworks.

Those are meaningful findings, not a command to praise constantly. Observational studies cannot rule out every difference between families, and laboratory tasks are smaller than childhood.

## EVIDENCE

The strongest common thread is modest: feedback changes what a child attends to. “You found another way” points toward a repeatable action. “You are brilliant” points toward a reputation that may need defending.

Mueller and Dweck's experiments support a causal effect in a particular setting: children were randomly given different feedback after a task, then faced harder work. The longitudinal studies add realism because praise was recorded in homes, but they mainly establish association. Parents who naturally use more process language may differ in other ways that also support learning.

A 2022 virtual-reality study adds an interesting pressure test. Praise can affect exploration, but its effect depends on wording and context; praise is not a lever that produces the same response in every child. Research on growth mindset has also faced justified debate. Effects are often smaller and more dependent on environment than popular summaries suggest. A child cannot “mindset” their way around poor teaching, an unsafe classroom or a task for which they have not been given the necessary knowledge.

The evidence therefore supports precise feedback, not a parenting spell. It does not support counting praise phrases, policing every adjective or treating one cheerful “you're amazing” as damage.

## PERSPECTIVES

One framing says ability praise is dangerous and effort praise is the cure. It correctly notices that identity labels can raise the stakes of failure. But it often turns a probability into a rule. Children hear thousands of messages from parents, teachers, friends and their own results. One sentence is part of a climate, not a verdict.

A second framing says the whole growth-mindset idea has been oversold. This criticism is useful. Broad school interventions have not always produced the large gains promised by popular books and workshops. It reminds us that motivation cannot substitute for instruction, resources or practice. Yet rejecting every finding because some claims became inflated throws away the more defensible lesson: feedback can direct attention toward either controllable processes or fixed traits.

A third view begins with relationship rather than technique. Children need delight, affection and honest recognition, not a running commentary from a laboratory manual. This view explains why mechanical praise fails. It can, however, become too vague if it offers no help when a parent genuinely wants better words.

The sensible middle is specific, truthful and warm. Name what you saw. Do not pretend effort was excellent when it was not. Do not make love or belonging depend on performance.

## CONTEXT

Praise became a parenting technology because it looked like a humane replacement for punishment. Then self-esteem culture encouraged adults to protect children from feeling inadequate. Growth-mindset research supplied a new vocabulary: praise effort rather than intelligence.

That shorthand is useful until it becomes another label. “You're such a hard worker” is still person praise if it defines the child. “You worked hard” can also mislead when the important change was not more effort but a better strategy. A child who keeps pushing the wrong piece into a puzzle does not need admiration for pushing harder. They may need permission to turn the piece.

The practical distinction is between evaluation and information. Evaluation ranks the person or product: brilliant, perfect, talented. Information identifies a cause the learner can inspect: you checked the units; you asked for help before guessing; you slowed down at the difficult paragraph; you changed your plan when the first one failed.

Useful feedback can also acknowledge outcomes plainly. “That note is in tune” is not forbidden. Nor is joy. The issue is whether praise closes the story or opens the next step.

A simple household test is to ask: if the result went badly tomorrow, would these words still help the child understand what to do? If not, the compliment may be decorating success rather than teaching from it.

## DEEPER

Children gradually build a private answer to a difficult question: what does a mistake say about me?

Adults answer even when they do not mean to. If success repeatedly earns identity labels, failure can feel like exposure. The smart child has not merely got one answer wrong; the title itself seems in danger. Avoiding hard tasks then becomes understandable. An easy victory protects the badge better than an ambitious attempt.

Process feedback offers a different story. A result is evidence about a method, preparation or current understanding. It matters, but it is not a complete biography. That distinction is useful far beyond school. Adults also hide from work that might revise a flattering image of themselves.

Still, a child is not a project for producing resilience. Sometimes the right response to a painting is simply pleasure. Sometimes frustration needs company before it needs a strategy. The deeper aim is not to engineer constant persistence. It is to keep identity large enough that one performance cannot occupy the whole room.

The best praise does something almost paradoxical. It pays close attention without making the child perform for attention. “I saw how carefully you listened to your partner” says: the action mattered, and I was here to notice it.

## REFLECT

Which compliments in your home describe an identity, and which reveal a repeatable choice?

After a setback, does your first response protect confidence, investigate the process, or make room for both?

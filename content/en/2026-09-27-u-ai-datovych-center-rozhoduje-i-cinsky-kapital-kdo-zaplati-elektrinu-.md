With AI data centres it is not enough to watch who builds them and who supplies the chips. The key is what exact role Chinese capital or a Chinese supplier plays, and who ends up paying for electricity, depreciation, permits and regulation.

The American AI data centre boom is starting to look like a sold-out concert that China is trying to get into not through the main entrance but through the van carrying the amplifiers. According to the report, Chinese AI data centre development is lagging, while the US has more AI data centres than any other country and Alphabet (GOOGL), Microsoft (MSFT), Meta (META) and Amazon (AMZN) keep funding new projects.

So it is not only about chips. It is about access to the place where capital, electricity, contracts and demand for computing power are already flowing.

The most interesting part is not that Chinese companies want into American AI data centres. It is how they might try to get there when direct ownership of sensitive infrastructure runs into politics, security and regulation. If the report names no specific Chinese buyers and no single completed deal, it is not fair to turn it into a finished story along the lines of "China is buying the American AI cloud." A better reading: Chinese capital and suppliers are probing where in the American data centre chain the doors are not yet closed.

Those doors need not be the GPU servers themselves. They could be minority stakes in project companies, construction financing, supplies of electrical components, cooling, switchgear, batteries, cabling, land, or capacity leased through colocation, a model in which a company does not own the whole data centre but pays for space, power and connectivity. That is the difference between buying a stadium and renting a VIP box. Both get you close to the game, but the regulator looks at them differently.

The counterintuitive point: a data centre is not a tech app but an industrial project dressed up as the cloud. In accounting terms it behaves less like magical AI and more like building a brewery: large capital expenditures first go onto the balance sheet as assets and only later flow gradually into the income statement through depreciation. Electricity, cooling, maintenance and connectivity then run continuously through operating costs. That is why the boom can lift suppliers' revenue as soon as equipment is delivered, while weighing on cloud companies' free cash flow long before the return fully shows.

And here is the Chinese motivation: if you cannot be the lead singer on stage, you can still sell the cables, lights, speakers, switchgear or the backstage financing. But in the US the backstage has security: foreign investment screening, export restrictions, cybersecurity and political sensitivity around data. So the Chinese element is not just a twist on the familiar AI capex thesis. It is a test of whether value is shifting from "who has the best model" to "who can get access to electricity, permits and a substation."

### Who it helps and who it hurts

On the plus side may be data centre infrastructure suppliers: AI chip makers such as NVIDIA (NVDA) and AMD (AMD), networking and specialised chips such as Broadcom (AVGO), servers and rack solutions such as Super Micro Computer (SMCI) or Dell Technologies (DELL). If construction continues, demand covers not only GPUs but also power, cooling and backup systems: Vertiv (VRT), Eaton (ETN) or Schneider Electric (SU.PA) are typical examples of companies in this layer.

Data centre landlords and operators such as Equinix (EQIX) and Digital Realty (DLR) may be helped by occupancy and pressure for new capacity, but they also carry the risk of pricier electricity, permitting processes and long lead times for transformers. Telecom and fibre infrastructure, for example Lumen Technologies (LUMN), may benefit from data transfer between clusters, but only if demand turns into real contracts, not just presentations in an AI font.

The hyperscalers Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta and Amazon face a mixed effect. On one hand they are building a computing lead. On the other, accounting reminds them that AI is not a free app: first cash flows out in capital expenditure, then come depreciation and operating costs. If Chinese companies took part as suppliers of less sensitive components or financing, that could in theory make part of the costs cheaper. But if they touched data, computing capacity for advanced models or ownership control, geopolitical scrutiny could sharply limit their role.

With news like this it pays to read the headlines less and the earnings notes more. Checklist: 1) capex and the depreciation schedule, 2) commentary on free cash flow, 3) the backlog of power and cooling suppliers, 4) gross margin and the impact of tariffs, shipping and components, 5) electricity prices and the availability of grid connections, 6) mentions of regulation, export restrictions and data security, 7) the deal structure of any Chinese involvement: supplier, lender, minority investor, capacity landlord, or actual operator. The biggest mistake is to confuse "data centres are being built" with automatically higher profits for everyone involved.

Colocation is like a restaurant that does not rent the whole building, just a stall in the food court. It gets space, power, water and customer traffic, but does not own the whole shopping centre. For AI data centres this means a company can get computing capacity or space for servers without building a giant campus itself. The effect? For the market what matters is who collects the rent, who pays for the electricity and who carries the risk that the regulator asks: "And who exactly are we handing the keys to?" For shares this can mean the difference between a nice order for a supplier and a long-term infrastructure bill for an operator.

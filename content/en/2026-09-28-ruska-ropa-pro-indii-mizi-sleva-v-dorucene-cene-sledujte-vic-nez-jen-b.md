In oil shocks, watch not only the Brent price but above all the delivered price per barrel and crack spreads. That is where it shows whether this is an advantage for producers, pressure on refiners, or future pressure at the pump.

The most dangerous barrels are not the ones that vanish from the market. They are the ones that get more expensive for just one buyer.

According to trade sources, Indian refiners expect Russian oil supplies to tighten further in October and November, because Russia is exporting less and China is competing harder for the same barrels. India, the world's third-largest oil importer, may therefore have to reach for more expensive alternatives for the rest of the year.

This is not the classic story of "oil gets pricier because there is not enough of it." A smarter frame is this: India's option on cheaper crude is disappearing.

After sanctions and shifts in trade flows, Russian oil became something like a VIP voucher for a cheaper restaurant for India. It did not mean the world suddenly had more oil; it meant one big buyer had a better input price than the others. But if a hungrier China shows up at the same counter while there is less on offer, the voucher stops being a magic slip of paper and the auction begins.

So the key effect need not show up only in the Brent price. For a refinery, the delivered price per barrel matters more: the benchmark, the discount or premium for a specific grade of crude, shipping, insurance and timing. When the Russian discount narrows or replacement crude from the Middle East or other regions gets pricier, an Indian refinery can face worse economics even when the headline oil price does not shoot up like a firework.

In accounting terms this is less exciting but more important: pricier input crude ties up more cash in inventory, raises feedstock costs and forces the refinery to rely on a wider gap between fuel prices and crude prices. If that gap does not widen, the margin gets squeezed like a lemon at a hotel bar, only without the little umbrella.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It helps producers and sellers of alternative barrels, who can benefit from higher demand for non-Russian oil. Examples of the effect: integrated oil companies such as Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), Shell (SHEL) or BP (BP), or Middle Eastern producers such as Saudi Aramco (2222.SR). It is not an automatic win, but a tighter supply environment usually improves sellers' bargaining position.

On the other side are Indian refiners such as Reliance Industries (RELIANCE.NS), Indian Oil (IOC.NS), Bharat Petroleum (BPCL.NS) and Hindustan Petroleum (HINDPETRO.NS). If they lose the cheaper Russian barrels and have to buy pricier alternatives, the pressure goes straight into input costs.

Chinese refiners such as Sinopec (0386.HK) or PetroChina (0857.HK) are a mixed case: they may gain more access to the crude they want, but strong demand also means a higher price at auction. For US refiners such as Valero Energy (VLO), Marathon Petroleum (MPC) or HF Sinclair (DINO) the effect is also mixed: higher global oil prices hurt on the input side, but wider fuel margins can offset part of the pressure.

With news like this, watching the oil price alone is not enough. Specific diagnostics: the spread between Russian crude grades and Brent/Dubai, sea freight to Asia, diesel and gasoline crack spreads (the gap between fuel prices and the crude price), and refiners' comments on their purchasing mix. If oil gets pricier and crack spreads do not rise, it is more a story of pressure on refiners. If crack spreads rise too, part of the cost can spill over into fuel prices. This is not a recommendation to buy or sell, but a framework for reading commodity news without the headline shock.

A crack spread is like the difference between the price of flour and the price of a croissant. A bakery does not earn money because flour exists, but because it bakes it into something more valuable. A refinery is a similar bakery: it buys crude and sells gasoline, diesel or jet fuel. When its flour, the crude, gets pricier, it has to raise the price of the croissant, the fuel, or its earnings fall. For the market this means a higher oil price need not help all energy companies equally; a producer may have a tailwind, a refiner may feel a draught in its margin, and an ordinary person may feel it mainly when the higher costs reach the pump.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Melatonin has become a common answer to children's sleep trouble, while U.S. poison-center and emergency-department data show a sharp rise in accidental ingestions.

**What it means.** Melatonin is a hormone involved in sleep timing, not a bedtime vitamin or a universal sedative. It may help some children, but the reason, dose, timing and product quality all matter.

**Risks and impact.** Most reported accidental ingestions caused minimal or no effects, yet some required hospital care. Gummies also look edible, and their measured contents have not always matched their labels.

**What can be done.** Discuss persistent sleep trouble and any supplement with a pediatric clinician, work on the sleep schedule first, and store every gummy, vitamin and medicine locked away and out of sight.

**What to watch.** Notice whether the underlying problem is actually sleep timing, and whether any benefit survives without escalating the amount or turning a short experiment into an indefinite routine.

## FACTS

Melatonin is produced naturally by the brain and helps signal biological night. In the United States, supplemental melatonin is sold over the counter as a dietary supplement rather than approved as a drug for children's sleep.

CDC researchers counted 260,435 pediatric melatonin ingestions reported to poison centers from 2012 through 2021, a 530% rise in the annual number over that period. Of the reports, 94.3% were unintentional and 83.8% involved children aged five or younger. Most children were asymptomatic or had minor effects, but 4,097 were hospitalized, 287 received intensive care, five required mechanical ventilation and two died. The researchers cautioned that the available records could not prove melatonin alone caused the deaths.

A separate CDC analysis estimated 10,930 U.S. emergency-department visits for unsupervised melatonin ingestion among children aged five or younger during 2019–2022. Most did not lead to hospitalization. Gummies were the most commonly documented dosage form, appearing in 47.3% of estimated visits; at least ten units were accessible in more than a third of visits involving a solid product.

Those numbers describe exposure and storage risk. They do not show that a correctly selected, clinician-guided use is ineffective or inherently dangerous.

## EVIDENCE

The evidence is strongest on three points. First, melatonin can be useful when the problem involves the body's clock and for some children with developmental conditions. Both the American Academy of Pediatrics and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine say the decision should be made with a pediatric health professional, not treated as automatic first aid for any difficult bedtime.

Second, the bottle may be less precise than it looks. A 2023 JAMA study purchased 25 U.S. melatonin gummy products and measured their contents. Twenty-two were outside a margin of 10% from the declared quantity. Among products containing melatonin, the measured amount ranged from 74% to 347% of the label; one product had no detectable melatonin but did contain CBD. This was a market snapshot, not a test of every brand or batch, yet it demonstrates why a label cannot be assumed to be laboratory exact.

Third, long-term certainty is limited. The AAP says short-term use appears relatively safe while less is known about prolonged use, including possible effects during growth and puberty. That uncertainty is not proof of harm. It is a reason to define the purpose and review point before a child starts taking it nightly.

## PERSPECTIVES

One view treats melatonin as the gentle option: it is sold without a prescription, the body makes it naturally and it does not resemble a conventional sleeping pill. This correctly distinguishes it from many sedatives. It can also hide three separate questions—whether the child needs it, whether the timing is right and whether the product contains what the label promises.

Another view says parents should avoid it entirely because accidental ingestions rose so sharply. That takes the surveillance signal seriously, especially for candy-like gummies within reach of young children. But exposure data combine supervised use, unsupervised access and intentional ingestion. A rise in poison-center calls is not a trial comparing careful treatment with no treatment.

Sleep specialists offer a narrower frame. The AASM says many childhood sleep problems respond better to changes in schedules, habits or behavior, while melatonin can help when a body clock is off schedule or in selected developmental conditions. The AAP similarly describes it as a possible short-term aid alongside an established routine, not a replacement for one.

These positions are not opposites. A product can have a legitimate use and still be marketed in a form that invites casual use. The useful dividing line is not “natural” versus “medicine.” It is a defined problem, a defined plan and a product kept under adult control.

## CONTEXT

Sleep is not a single switch. A child may resist a limit, be anxious, nap late, use a bright screen near bedtime, have a schedule that conflicts with adolescence, or live with a medical or developmental condition that disrupts sleep. The same outward complaint—“won't fall asleep”—can therefore have different causes.

Melatonin is chiefly a timing signal. More is not automatically more effective, and a dose given at the wrong time may answer the wrong question. This is why professional guidance focuses on the reason and timing as well as the quantity. The AAP notes that there are no single dosing guidelines for all children and that melatonin can interact with other medicines.

The product category adds a second layer. U.S. supplements do not undergo the same premarket approval process as prescription or over-the-counter drugs. The AASM recommends looking for the voluntary USP Verified Mark if melatonin is used, while also noting how few products have received it. Verification can improve confidence in manufacturing and contents; it does not decide whether a particular child should take the product.

The most reliable household intervention is less technical. A gummy supplement belongs with medicines, not with snacks. CDC's emergency-visit analysis found that many young children accessed flavored products and that bottles were a documented route when container details were available. “Out of reach” can fail when a child climbs. Locked, high and out of sight is a clearer standard.

## PEOPLE

The parent reaching for a sleep aid is often solving two nights at once: the child's restless night and the household's exhausted morning. A quick, sweet-tasting product offers something routines do not—a feeling that the problem has an immediate, measurable answer.

The child experiences a different system. Bedtime can be a limit, a separation, a body clock or a symptom. A supplement may help one of those and leave the others untouched. That is why a short sleep log can be more revealing than another argument at the bedroom door.

For one week, record bedtime, lights-out time, estimated sleep time, wake time, naps, late caffeine, evening screens and any melatonin given. The page is not a diagnosis. It gives a clinician and parent something concrete to inspect: whether the trouble is consistent, whether timing changes it and whether the apparent benefit is larger than ordinary night-to-night variation.

## DEEPER

Parents are asked to turn uncertainty into routine. Pack the lunch, set the alarm, make bedtime work. A product that converts “maybe tonight” into a numbered dose fits the architecture of family life. It feels like control.

But a number can create false precision. The label may say three milligrams while the gummy contains something else. Even an exact dose cannot tell whether the problem is a late body clock, an inconsistent boundary or worry that appears only when the room goes quiet. Measurement is useful only after the question is clear.

A practical sequence keeps the uncertainty visible. First, name the pattern: trouble starting sleep, staying asleep or waking on time. Second, stabilize what can be observed—schedule, morning light, naps and evening stimulation. Third, bring a short record and the complete product label to a pediatric clinician. If melatonin becomes part of the plan, agree on its purpose, timing, storage and the date for reviewing whether it still belongs there.

This approach does not turn bedtime into a laboratory. It prevents a temporary tool from becoming part of the furniture without anyone noticing. The deeper skill is not rejecting every aid or trusting every label. It is learning to tell the difference between relief tonight and an answer to the problem that keeps returning.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Streaming menus made subtitles feel like a transparent copy of speech, but every timed-text track is an edited layer built under limits of time, space and reading speed.

**What it means.** A subtitle can preserve the meaning and tone of a scene without reproducing every spoken word. Translation adds another set of choices because syntax, idiom and word length change between languages.

**Risks and impact.** Treating a subtitle as a transcript can create false quotations, mistaken accusations of censorship or confusion when an “English” track differs from “English CC” or SDH.

**What can be done.** Check what kind of track you selected, compare it with the original audio, and use a script or transcript before quoting exact words.

**What to watch.** Compression is not automatically poor work. The useful questions are whether essential meaning survived, the line can be read in time and relevant sound information is available to the audience that needs it.

## FACTS

A transcript follows speech through time. A subtitle must also fit a moving image. It appears, remains long enough to read, disappears around cuts and leaves room for faces, signs and action. Those constraints turn transcription into timed writing.

Netflix's current English (UK) guide permits up to 42 characters per line and sets a reading-speed ceiling of 20 characters per second for adult programmes and 17 for children's programmes. Its separate timing guidance gives a minimum duration of four-fifths of a second and tells authors to extend longer text when possible. These are platform specifications, not universal laws, but they make the hidden design problem visible.

Track labels describe different jobs. Translation subtitles primarily carry dialogue into another language for viewers who can hear the soundtrack. Captions or subtitles for d/Deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers also need relevant sound effects, music cues and speaker identification. Terminology varies by country and service, so a label is a clue rather than a guarantee.

## EVIDENCE

The Described and Captioned Media Program defines quality captions as accurate, consistent, clear, readable and equal. “Equal” includes preserving meaning and intent; it does not require squeezing every syllable into every frame. Its guidance says editing may be necessary when time and readability make verbatim text impractical.

Netflix draws a useful distinction. Its UK guide says SDH should include as much of the original content as possible and should not paraphrase dialogue, yet it still allows truncation when reading speed and synchronization require it. Translation templates may be reduced, deleted or condensed. In both cases, timing remains part of accuracy because text that cannot be read has failed its audience.

A 2024 eye-tracking replication in the Journal of Audiovisual Translation tested subtitle speeds of 12, 20 and 28 characters per second. It found that speed and the concurrent video affected processing, with differences across native and non-native viewers. The study does not identify one perfect global speed. It supports the more modest point that reading subtitles and watching images compete for attention.

## PERSPECTIVES

For a translator, literal equivalence is only one obligation. A joke may depend on timing; an idiom may need a different image; a short English phrase may expand in another language. The translator balances meaning, voice, rhythm and the number of seconds available.

For a d/Deaf viewer, dialogue alone is incomplete access. Ofcom's 2024 guidelines say relevant sound, music and tone should be conveyed and speakers identified when it is not obvious who is talking. A door slamming or an alarm sounding can change the scene even when nobody speaks.

For a hearing language learner, subtitles are tempting evidence about vocabulary. They can help, but a translation track may deliberately choose a natural equivalent rather than map word for word. A same-language caption track can be closer to the audio while still editing repetitions or overlaps.

For a filmmaker, text occupies composition. Ofcom advises logical line breaks and placement that avoids obscuring faces or important action. A perfectly literal block in the wrong place can preserve words while damaging the intended performance.

## CONTEXT

The mismatch between speech and text is older than streaming. Silent-film intertitles interrupted action. Cinema subtitles had to remain legible on projected images. Television captions were constrained by broadcast systems and small screens. Digital distribution increased flexibility, but it also multiplied versions: original-language subtitles, translated subtitles, forced narrative captions, closed captions, SDH and captions derived from a dubbed track.

That last distinction explains many online arguments. A film made in Korean may offer English translation subtitles for the Korean performance and “English CC” aligned with an English dub. The dub has its own lip-sync and performance constraints, so the two English tracks can legitimately differ. Neither is necessarily a transcript of the original Korean.

Platform guides also evolve. A number such as 42 characters per line is a house specification, not a natural boundary of human perception. Language, device size, viewer age, familiarity and visual complexity all matter. Standards create consistent working limits; editors still make judgments inside them.

## PEOPLE

Good timed text often becomes noticeable only when it fails. A fast reader may wonder why a line was shortened. A slower reader may miss a dense line entirely. Someone with hearing loss may receive dialogue but lose the off-screen sound that explains a character's reaction.

Viewers also bring unequal conditions: a small phone, a bright room, dyslexia, partial sight, a new language or divided attention. The same track cannot optimize every variable. Offering well-labelled alternatives matters because accessibility is not one generic switch.

The author is present too, even when invisible. A subtitler chooses where a sentence breaks, whether an interruption matters and which sound deserves a label. Those decisions are interpretive labour, not evidence that the words were casually changed.

## DEEPER

Use a four-step check before judging or quoting a subtitle.

1. **Identify the track.** Look for the original language, a translation label, CC or SDH. If there are two same-language tracks, sample both; one may follow a dub while the other translates the original audio.
2. **Separate meaning from wording.** Ask first whether the action, claim, joke or relationship survived. A different phrase can be an accurate translation. An omitted idea is a stronger reason to investigate than an omitted filler word.
3. **Check the source of an exact quote.** Re-listen to the original audio, consult an official script or use a reliable transcript. Quote the subtitle only when the subtitle itself is the object of discussion, and identify the track.
4. **Test access, not just completeness.** Can the line be read before it disappears? Are speakers and important sounds clear? Does placement hide information? More text is not automatically more accurate if it makes the scene inaccessible.

When reporting a suspected change, save the title, episode, timestamp, audio language and exact track label. That turns “the subtitles are wrong” into a claim another person can reproduce. The practical habit is simple: treat subtitles as a designed interpretation attached to a version, not as stenography floating above the film.

## REFLECT

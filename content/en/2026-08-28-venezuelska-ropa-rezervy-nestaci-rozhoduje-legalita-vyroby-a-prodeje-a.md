With Venezuelan oil it is not enough to count the reserves in the ground; what decides is whether, under the given sanctions, technical limits and refining capacity, they turn into a barrel that can actually be sold.

Imagine someone reserving the biggest wine cellar in the area — except that a political rival holds the keys, the lift does not work and some of the bottles are sealed with sanctions tape.

According to sources cited in the report, officials of the Trump administration are working on a deal that would secure the US long-term access to part of Venezuela's oil reserves. The report also says that the US oversees Venezuelan oil exports, so this is not just a commercial contract but a combination of energy, sanctions and geopolitics.

The biggest mistake with news like this is to picture Venezuelan oil as a tap: turn it — and the world market is flooded with cheaper energy. But Venezuela is more like an oil warehouse with complicated wiring.

Venezuelan oil is often heavy and more sulphurous, which means it needs specific refineries and processing. With the extra-heavy crude from the Orinoco region, it is also not just a matter of getting it out of the ground: to move it by pipeline and tanker, it often has to be diluted with a lighter feedstock or upgraded. The value of the deal therefore lies not only in the question “how many barrels”, but “who can actually process this oil, insure it, ship it and legally pay for it”. That is the invisible part of the energy market: oil is not one universal liquid like tap water. It is more like flour — fine, semi-coarse, wholemeal. If you have a recipe for pancakes, a sack of rye flour will not quite save you.

Here is the Venezuelan twist that gets lost in the headlines: reserves are geology, exports are logistics and revenue is politics. A country can have huge reserves, but if it lacks diluents, infrastructure maintenance, shipping insurance, payment channels or sanctions waivers, the barrel behaves like a luxury car without a roadworthiness certificate — it looks great in the garage, but it is not allowed on the road.

A fresh framework: for the US, this deal could be more of a geopolitical option than an immediate flood of supply. An option means a right, not an obligation — in practice the possibility of having secured access in the future if political conditions, the sanctions regime or tensions on other oil routes change. The market might therefore react not only to expectations of new barrels but also to the sense that Washington is buying itself an energy insurance policy for scenarios in which the Middle East, Russia or OPEC complicate supplies.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

So far, all the report documents is that talks are under way on long-term access to part of the reserves; no volumes, prices or specific companies are given. The impacts below are therefore an illustrative framework, not a claim that they have already happened.

On the side of potential winners would be US refineries able to process heavier crude, for example Valero Energy (VLO), Phillips 66 (PSX) or Marathon Petroleum (MPC). The reason is not some magic link to Venezuela but technical compatibility: complex refineries with coking units can get more valuable products out of heavier crude better than simple plants. If the flow of Venezuelan oil became legally and logistically more stable, such refineries could have a wider range of feedstocks. Similarly relevant could be companies with a historical or operational link to Venezuelan upstream, that is, extraction and production, for example Chevron (CVX) — but only within the limits of licences and political rules.

Conversely, a less comfortable scenario could arise for some competing suppliers of heavier crude, for example Canadian oil sands producers such as Suncor Energy (SU) or Canadian Natural Resources (CNQ), if Venezuelan supply came to compete in US refineries over time. For US producers of light shale oil, such as EOG Resources (EOG) or Diamondback Energy (FANG), the impact would not be mechanical: their oil is a different product, but broader supply on the market can weigh on sentiment around oil prices in general.

With news like this, the key is to separate three layers: the political deal, the physical ability to extract and transport the oil, and the refining ability to process it. The headline may sound like “more oil”, but the market is really asking: when, how much, of what quality, through whom and under what sanctions conditions?

- Is the deal signed, or is it just talks according to sources?

- Is the talk about reserves in the ground, or about actual exports?

- Are volumes and a timetable given?

- Does the oil have the necessary dilution, processing and transport route?

- Is the oil light or heavy?

- Which refineries can process it?

- Are the sanctions rules changing, or only the political rhetoric?

“Oil reserves” are not the same as petrol at the pump. It is like having a huge stock of potatoes in the cellar, but no knife, no stove and no permit to open a restaurant. For the market this means: news of access to reserves alone can change expectations, but energy prices will only be affected by whether the oil is actually extracted, diluted, transported, processed and gets into the system. For an ordinary person the impact may show up indirectly — through fuel prices, inflation and company costs — but not automatically, straight from the headline.

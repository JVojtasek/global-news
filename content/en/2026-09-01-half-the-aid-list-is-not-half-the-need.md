## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** The World Food Programme says a funding shortage will force it to reduce food-assistance coverage in the West Bank from 400,000 people to 200,000 starting in September.

**What it means.** The headline “cut by half” describes the number of recipients, not a halving of measured need. WFP estimates that 900,000 people are food insecure there and says need has more than doubled since 2023.

**Risks and impact.** Removing coverage creates an eligibility boundary inside a population already facing lost income, displacement and disrupted livelihoods.

**What can be done.** Readers can distinguish money pledged, people selected and assistance actually delivered; those are three different measures.

**What to watch.** Over the next six months, watch whether WFP publishes the revised targeting method, how many people actually receive September support and whether its stated USD 386 million funding gap narrows.

## FACTS

WFP announced on 1 September that it would cut the number of people receiving its West Bank food assistance from 400,000 to 200,000 beginning this month. The agency attributed the reduction to critical funding constraints, not to improved conditions.

WFP estimates that 900,000 people in the West Bank are food insecure. Its latest quarterly analysis found that 76 percent of households had experienced a significant income decline, 12 percent of people had poor food consumption and one third of families could not afford a nutritious diet. Rural communities and southern governorates were identified as especially affected.

The programme uses several forms of support, including cash, vouchers, food items, nutrition services and livelihood programmes. Its announcement did not publish the revised eligibility formula or specify which combination of those services the 200,000 people leaving coverage had received.

Across Gaza and the West Bank, WFP says it needs an additional USD 386 million over the six months from September to assist an estimated two million food-insecure people.

## EVIDENCE

The central decision is strongly documented. WFP is the primary record, while AP and Reuters independently reported the same start date and change in coverage. What those sources establish is a reduction in people reached. They do not establish that every remaining recipient will receive the same package, or that the excluded 200,000 will receive no assistance from any other agency.

The need figures come from WFP's own food-security analysis. They are useful operational measures, but the underlying household tables and revised beneficiary-selection rules were not included in the announcement. That limits public assessment of who crosses the new threshold.

OCHA supplies independent context for rising vulnerability. Its 31 July report said demolitions, settler violence and related access restrictions displaced an average of 17 Palestinians a day in the West Bank during 2026, twice the average of the preceding three years.

A January agreement between the Palestinian Ministry of Social Development and WFP described cash support for about 200,000 West Bank beneficiaries under coordinated targeting criteria. That figure should not be mistaken for today's entire programme: WFP says its combined assistance had reached 400,000. It does show that selection systems existed before the new cut.

### Where they agree

The cut is a rationing decision made while measured need is increasing. The live uncertainty is not whether coverage will shrink, but who will lose it, what assistance remains and whether September delivery matches the announced figures.

## CONTEXT

Food assistance is often reported as if it were a warehouse count. In the West Bank it also includes cash and vouchers, which depend on functioning markets, household purchasing power and physical access. A transfer can retain the same face value while buying less; a food parcel can exist while a road closure makes collection harder.

That is why three denominators matter. WFP estimates 900,000 people are food insecure. Before the cut it reached 400,000. From September it plans to reach 200,000. The uncovered population is therefore much larger than the newly removed group, even before considering differences in severity.

Targeting is unavoidable when resources cannot cover everyone, but it is not neutral bookkeeping. Agencies may rank households by income, displacement, disability, family composition, nutrition risk or loss of livelihood. Each rule protects one kind of vulnerability and may miss another. Public criteria and appeals matter because small data errors can become lost meals at the threshold.

### Practical impact

When reading the next aid headline, ask three questions: **How many people are assessed as needing help? How many are eligible? How many actually received it?** Do not substitute one number for another.

For this decision, the most informative near-term evidence will be September delivery totals, the geographic and household profile of recipients, and any published route for reassessment. A donor pledge matters only when it becomes usable funding and then delivered assistance.

## DEEPER

The phrase “prioritising the most vulnerable” sounds humane because it is. It is also the language of enforced scarcity. A ranking system cannot create a clean moral boundary where living conditions form a slope.

The 200,001st person is the problem hidden by the round number. That person may be slightly less poor on paper, have a relative assumed able to help, or live outside a selected district. None of those facts guarantees a nutritious meal. The cut therefore shifts risk from an institution's budget to households' informal coping systems: borrowing, selling assets, reducing meal quality or depending on relatives who may also have lost income.

This is not an argument against targeting. It is an argument for seeing what targeting does. It concentrates limited help and concentrates exclusion at the same time.

### Reader outcome

**What changed.** WFP will reduce West Bank food-assistance coverage by 200,000 people from September because funding is insufficient.

**Why it matters.** The decision arrives while WFP estimates need is rising, so the smaller list reflects scarcity of resources rather than a smaller problem.

**What to watch, not what to do.** Over September and the following six months, watch actual recipient counts, published eligibility rules and progress against the USD 386 million regional funding requirement.

**What would change our minds.** Evidence that other programmes fully replace the lost coverage, that food insecurity falls materially, or that restored funding returns WFP reach toward 400,000 would change the assessment of the cut's practical severity.

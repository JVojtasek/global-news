With AI infrastructure projections, the key is to translate the huge overall figure into an annual pace, physical capacity, available energy and actual utilisation — otherwise it can look bigger than what it will really earn.

When someone says "AI infrastructure", do not picture a server room — picture the construction of a new city where computations live instead of people and each one wants its own power plant behind the fence. PwC estimates that investment in AI infrastructure could reach 31.6 trillion dollars by 2050, with the US expected to account for almost half of global spending. Translated from the language of giant numbers: spread evenly from the mid-2020s, that is roughly 1.3 trillion dollars a year globally and about 0.6 trillion a year in the US — not a one-off firework, but a long race in chips, data centres, networks, cooling, electricity and the transmission grid.

The most interesting thing is not the number itself. What is interesting is that AI is starting to behave less like software and more like heavy industry — and that the 31.6 trillion projection is not a revenue forecast, but a map of how much of the physical world would have to be built for the digital promises to have somewhere to live.

With classic software, the magic sentence was: "the next customer costs almost nothing". With AI infrastructure, it is less magic and more excavator. The next model, the next user, the next inference — meaning the actual use of the model after it has been trained — often means another GPU, more memory, more network components and above all more megawatts. In other words: the margins of the future AI economy will be decided not only in code, but also in substations.

The new filter is simple: a big number has to be translated three times. First, from the total sum into an annual pace. Second, from dollars into physical capacity — chips, buildings, electricity, cooling, connections. Third, from capacity into utilisation, meaning whether the machines are actually running and generating paid work. Without this, a projection is like a plan to build an airport based on the number of suitcases people might one day own, not on the planes that will actually land.

The historical parallel is therefore not just the internet bubble, but electrification. When electricity was introduced, it was not only the light bulb makers who won. Demand arose for copper, transformers, grid builders, power plants, standards and regulation. Similarly, AI capex may extend the "technology" story into industry and energy. That is counterintuitive: the smarter the software, the more physical its bill.

And one more catch: 31.6 trillion dollars by 2050 sounds like endless growth, but the market will keep punishing badly timed capacity. A data centre without cheap energy and high utilisation is like a luxury hotel in the desert without a road — nice assets, sad occupancy.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The positive impact most directly concerns makers of computing hardware, because their role follows from their product portfolios: NVIDIA (NVDA) and AMD (AMD) in AI accelerators, Broadcom (AVGO) and Marvell (MRVL) in networking and custom chips, TSMC (TSM) and ASML (ASML) in the supply chain for advanced semiconductor manufacturing. The memory cycle may concern companies such as Micron (MU), because large AI systems need fast memory and data throughput.

The second layer is data centres and their "plumbers": Arista Networks (ANET) and Cisco (CSCO) in networking, Vertiv (VRT), Eaton (ETN) and Schneider Electric (SU.PA) in power, cooling and electrical infrastructure. The energy part may play into the hands of producers and operators of power sources such as Constellation Energy (CEG), NextEra Energy (NEE), Vistra (VST) or GE Vernova (GEV), but only if demand turns into contracts, permits and grid connections.

The hyperscalers face a mixed impact — Microsoft (MSFT), Alphabet (GOOGL), Amazon (AMZN), Meta Platforms (META). They are the engine of demand, but they also pay the bill. For them, it is not just about AI revenue growth, but whether capital spending raises depreciation and pressure on free cash flow faster than the benefit to customers.

Qorvo (QRVO) is rather a marginal example: part of the story may concern it through connectivity, RF and power components, but it is not a pure representative of AI accelerators. With names like this, it is fair to distinguish between a direct beneficiary and a company the market has merely stuck onto a big theme.

A framework for similar projections: the number on its own is not enough. There are four control questions: 1) how much of the total falls on one year, not on the whole quarter-century, 2) who is actually spending the capex, 3) whether they have access to electricity and the grid, 4) whether the capacity runs at high utilisation and revenue from AI services appears before accounting depreciation and interest start biting into margins. The biggest risk is not that AI will not be important; the risk is that a good megatrend gets paid for at too optimistic a price.

"Capex" is big investment spending — like when a café buys not just more coffee for its stock, but a new coffee machine, oven, fridge and bigger premises. In AI, capex is the purchase of chips, servers, buildings, cooling and electricity. The impact? For the market, it means more orders for infrastructure suppliers, but for the companies paying the bill, also more pressure: expensive equipment has to earn money. For ordinary people, it may show up indirectly through electricity prices, pressure on the power grid and how cheap and accessible AI services become.

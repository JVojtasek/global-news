A flat cobalt price does not have to mean calm; with DRC quotas, watch above all the concrete volume of the restriction against normal exports and the DRC's roughly 70–75% share of global mine output.

Cobalt right now resembles a quiet restaurant where the chef has his hand on the main gas valve — the guests are still eating calmly, but everyone sensible is watching his fingers.

Spot cobalt prices were practically unchanged over the past month, but market news brought an important political element: the Democratic Republic of the Congo may cut its quota if the market needs rebalancing again. That matters because in recent years the DRC has supplied roughly 70–75% of global cobalt mine output; it is not a doorman at the side entrance, but the person holding the key to the main door. The IEA also warns that the cobalt market may face a deficit from 2026 because of stricter export restrictions from the DRC; on the corporate side, strong half-year results from CMOC and Glencore caught attention.

The most interesting thing about the whole story is not that cobalt may become scarcer. The market has heard that in various forms for years. What is interesting is that the price is standing still for now, while the political lever over supply is tightening.

That is a different type of risk from the classic commodity cycle. With copper or oil, people often watch whether demand is rising and whether miners can keep up with supply. With cobalt, it is also extremely important who sits at the export gate. The DRC is the key jurisdiction for cobalt, so a quota works like an administrative tap: it may not change the physical hunger for batteries this morning, but it can change the price of certainty that the material will actually arrive tomorrow.

An important nuance: the DRC dominates mainly mining, while a large part of refining and downstream processing is concentrated outside the DRC, especially in China. That is why a quota in the DRC is not just a local political footnote. It is a potential restriction on raw material entering the global pipeline that then feeds refineries, cathode materials and the battery chain. When you pinch the start of a hose, the end does not run dry immediately — but everyone starts checking their tanks.

And here is the counterintuitive point: a flat spot price does not have to mean calm. Sometimes it only means the market is waiting for confirmation of whether this is political rhetoric or a real restriction of flows. Miners' shares can then react earlier than the commodity, because investors are pricing the possibility of future supply tightening — a bit like people starting to buy umbrellas when the forecaster has not yet said rain, but has been staring at the radar suspiciously long.

With quotas, however, you need to strictly separate possibility from maths. The news supplied does not give a concrete proposed quota volume, so the fair conclusion is not "the market will lose X tonnes", but: an export restriction from the DRC could theoretically affect the part of the market that represents roughly three quarters of global mine output. The real impact would only be calculated as the difference between normal DRC exports and the permitted quota, adjusted for stocks, exemptions, delayed deliveries and buyers' ability to reach for alternatives.

The IEA's warning about a deficit from 2026 is also worth reading with the same caution. Without a published deficit size and scenario in this report, we do not know whether this is tension at the edge of the market or a structural hole. In a market measured in the order of hundreds of thousands of tonnes a year, the difference between single thousands and tens of thousands of tonnes can be crucial: the first is an annoying pebble in your shoe, the second an untied sole in the middle of a marathon.

With CMOC and Glencore, three things need to be separated: the cobalt price, volumes and the overall business mix. Strong growth in profit or EBITDA does not have to be a purely cobalt story; for diversified miners, other commodities, costs, trading activities and the production mix also feed into the results.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The plus side: miners and traders with access to production or stocks. Examples of the impact are CMOC Group (603993.SS, 3993.HK), which announced a jump in H1 profit, or Glencore (GLEN.L), whose adjusted EBITDA for H1 2026 rose to 10.1 billion USD. More broadly, producers of basic materials with exposure to battery metals may also benefit.

The minus side: companies further down the chain that buy cobalt as an input. These include makers of cathode materials and batteries such as Umicore (UMI.BR), LG Energy Solution (373220.KS) or Panasonic Holdings (6752.T). For carmakers such as Tesla (TSLA), General Motors (GM) or Ford (F) the impact is mixed: it depends on battery chemistry, contracts and the ability to pass costs into the price of the car.

The relative winners may be technologies and manufacturers that reduce dependence on cobalt, for example LFP batteries, where BYD (1211.HK) or CATL (300750.SZ) are often mentioned.

With news like this, it pays to distinguish whether the market is dealing with a real shortage of the metal, political uncertainty, or simply better results from the big miners.

An export quota is like a concert organiser letting only a limited number of people into the hall, even though a long queue is standing outside. With cobalt this is sensitive because the DRC is not a small side entrance, but the source of roughly three quarters of global mine output. For the market, this means the metal does not have to get more expensive straight away, but everyone starts worrying about who gets in. For miners' shares this may be supportive, for battery makers unpleasant, and for the ordinary person it may eventually show up in the prices of cars and electronics, or in pressure to switch to cheaper battery chemistries.

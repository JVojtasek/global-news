## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** “Worry time” turns an all-day mental interruption into a short, scheduled period for reviewing written concerns.

**What it means.** The point is not to suppress a thought. It is to separate noticing a worry from immediately obeying its demand for attention.

**Risks and impact.** Research suggests only small average benefits, and a clinical dissertation found no advantage over monitoring alone. The technique is an experiment, not a cure.

**What can be done.** Write each worry in one sentence, return to the present task, then review the list for 10 to 20 minutes at a regular time away from bedtime.

**What to watch.** After one or two weeks, check whether worry occupies less of the day and whether actionable problems are producing actual plans. If distress remains overwhelming or disrupts daily life, seek qualified support.

## FACTS

“Worry time,” also called worry postponement, is a component used in some cognitive-behavioural approaches. The instructions are simple: notice a worry, record it briefly, postpone fuller attention until a chosen period, and return to the activity already under way. At the appointment, review the list rather than letting it accumulate indefinitely.

The [NHS Every Mind Matters guide](https://www.nhs.uk/every-mind-matters/mental-wellbeing-tips/self-help-cbt-techniques/tackling-your-worries/) suggests a short daily period of about 10 or 15 minutes. It then asks a practical question: is there something that can be done? If so, make a specific plan covering what, how and when. If not, acknowledge the limit and redirect attention.

A [Royal National Orthopaedic Hospital NHS guide](https://www.rnoh.nhs.uk/patients-and-visitors/patient-information-guides/patients-guide-postponing-worries) uses a similar sequence, but suggests about 20 minutes and advises against placing it just before bed. It distinguishes practical worries—current problems open to action—from hypothetical “what if?” worries that cannot be acted on now. That distinction is the working part of the method.

## EVIDENCE

The strongest available synthesis is modest, not magical. A [2023 meta-analysis by Annika Dippel, Jos Brosschot and Bart Verkuil](https://www.piekeren.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Dippel.2023_Worry-postponement-meta-analysis.pdf) included seven randomized trials with 999 participants; 250 described worry as a burden. Compared with merely registering worries, postponement produced small effects on worry duration and frequency. The abstract reports standardized effects of 0.313 and 0.189 respectively.

Those numbers support “may help a little,” not “switches worry off.” The interventions lasted from roughly one week to 28 days. None included long-term follow-up, instructions varied, and most studies had some risk-of-bias concerns around self-reported outcomes.

There is also contrary evidence. In [Kathleen Tallon’s dissertation](https://rshare.library.torontomu.ca/articles/thesis/Short-Term_Efficacy_of_a_Worry_Postponement_Intervention_for_Generalized_Anxiety_Disorder/14653938), 67 adults with generalized anxiety disorder were randomized to postponement, worry monitoring or assessment only. Worry declined across the study, but postponement did not outperform the control conditions. The fair conclusion is that the routine is low-cost and plausible, while its specific and lasting effect remains uncertain.

## PERSPECTIVES

One framing treats the method as stimulus control. If worry repeatedly appears everywhere—at a desk, in bed, during a conversation—the whole day becomes its cue. A fixed appointment may teach a different pattern: a thought can be noticed now without becoming the next task. This framing explains why consistency matters, but it can make an untidy human process sound more programmable than it is.

A second framing is problem-solving. The scheduled period is valuable because it forces a fork in the road. Practical worry should end in a next action; hypothetical worry should not masquerade as planning. This explains why writing “email the landlord at 9 a.m.” is different from replaying “what if everything goes wrong?” It also leaves out worries that contain both real uncertainty and real action.

A third framing is simply attention management. Writing a thought down provides an external reminder, so the mind need not keep refreshing it. Tallon’s negative comparison matters here: monitoring itself may account for some improvement. The notebook, not postponement alone, may be doing useful work.

These interpretations can coexist. The evidence does not yet show which ingredient—scheduling, recording, refocusing or planning—deserves most of the credit.

## CONTEXT

Worry often presents itself as preparation. Sometimes it is. Mentally rehearsing an interview can expose a missing example; thinking about a bill can prompt a call. The difficulty is that useful preparation has an exit. It produces information, a decision or an action. Repetitive worry can keep paying attention without buying any of those things.

The technique therefore does not ask whether a concern is “valid.” A valid concern can still arrive at an unusable moment. Nor does postponement mean denying it. Suppression says the thought must not exist. Scheduling says it has been recorded and will receive a defined hearing.

The practical-versus-hypothetical split also needs care. “I might lose my job” may be hypothetical today, while “my contract ends Friday” is actionable. The same topic can move categories as circumstances change. At review time, rewrite vague entries as questions: “What can I check?” “Who can answer?” “When will I decide?” If none has an answer, the task may be tolerating uncertainty rather than producing more analysis.

This is why worry time should have a closing ritual: mark the next action, discard stale entries, and leave the list. Without an ending, the appointment can become concentrated rumination—the same loop in a smaller room.

## PEOPLE

The method is most relevant to someone whose attention is repeatedly pulled away by ordinary, non-urgent concerns: a message awaiting a reply, a future appointment, an expense not yet due. A one-line note can protect the current hour without pretending the concern is trivial.

It is less suitable as a stand-alone answer when anxiety is persistent, severe, linked to compulsions or trauma, or is making sleep, work, eating or relationships hard to manage. The [NHS guide](https://www.nhs.uk/every-mind-matters/mental-wellbeing-tips/self-help-cbt-techniques/tackling-your-worries/) presents worry time among self-help strategies, not as a replacement for assessment or treatment.

There is no prize for mastering this alone. A clinician can help distinguish a trainable habit from a condition needing fuller care. Immediate danger, inability to stay safe or thoughts of self-harm call for urgent local help, not a later appointment with a notebook.

## DEEPER

A worry feels important partly because it is unfinished. The mind mistakes recurrence for evidence: if the thought keeps returning, perhaps it must deserve another hearing. Yet repetition adds urgency more reliably than it adds knowledge.

An appointment changes the relationship without settling the argument. It grants the worry a place but not the entire house. That is a small act of authority over attention, and attention is one of the few resources that cannot be saved for later. Whatever receives it becomes, for that moment, the shape of the day.

The deeper test is not whether the scheduled period feels calm. It may not. The test is whether thinking becomes more honest. Did the concern produce an action? Did new evidence appear? Did the feared event happen? Or did the mind merely circle the same blank space?

A useful worry session ends with fewer open loops, not greater certainty. Some entries become calendar items. Some become questions for another person. Some remain unresolved and are released for now. The discipline lies in allowing uncertainty to remain uncertain without turning the whole day into its waiting room.

Try the routine for seven days. Keep the appointment short and regular. At the end, compare how much time worry took, how often you returned to the present task, and how many entries became concrete actions. Keep it only if it earns its place.

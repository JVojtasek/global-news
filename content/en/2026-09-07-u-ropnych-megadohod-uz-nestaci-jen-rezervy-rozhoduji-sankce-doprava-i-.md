With oil megadeals, it is not enough to watch the size of the reserves: what decides is whether a barrel makes it through the whole funnel, from rights through capital and sanctions to insured shipping, a refinery and a paid invoice.

Oil in the ground is like a Ferrari without the keys: it looks expensive, but it will not drive you to the petrol station on its own. The published market report this commentary is based on says that Trump's deal with Venezuela gives the Pentagon an equity stake in North America Blue Energy Partners, which has obtained concessions to 65 billion barrels of proven reserves. If that volume were actually turned into an economically viable business, NABEP would rank among the world's largest oil players by size of reserves.

The most important thing is not the 65 billion barrel figure itself, but who is sitting at the table. The Pentagon as a shareholder moves oil out of the commodity column and into the strategic insurance column. That is a different sport from a classic oil concession, where the investor mainly worries about the price of a barrel, drilling costs and return on capital.

A strange hybrid is being born here: in accounting logic, the company may hold reserves, but in political logic, Washington may hold a lever over energy security, Venezuela and American refineries. State capitalism in this form does not mean the government nationalises everything. It means foreign policy moves straight into the company's ownership structure. As if a father-in-law joined your family business: he brings money, but starts deciding over Sunday lunch who you are allowed to trade with.

The less visible point: in a deal like this, the Pentagon can act as a political co-author of the barrel's route, not just as a passive investor. With oil from a sensitive jurisdiction, value is not created the moment a geologist says: here it is. Value is created only when the barrel passes through the chain: ownership rights, sanctions waivers, insurance, tanker, refinery, payment. One weak link in that chain can turn reserves into a museum exhibit.

The counterintuitive point: a deal like this can be both bullish and bearish for the market at the same time. Bullish, if it improves the chances of investment, licences, service contracts and a gradual return of barrels to the market. Bearish, if the geopolitical label scares off banks, partners and buyers, because every barrel will carry legal and sanctions risk. Reserves on their own are not cash. The market will want to see not only capex, permits, drilling rigs, export logistics, oil quality and real buyers, but also whether the barrel turns into a compliance hot potato that banks and insurers toss from hand to hand.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It could help oilfield services, if the concessions turn into real drilling. Companies such as SLB (SLB), Halliburton (HAL) or Baker Hughes (BKR) could benefit from a scenario like this through higher demand for drilling technology, field maintenance and infrastructure. The key yardstick: order backlog, service contract margins and the number of active projects, not headlines about reserves.

It is also interesting for refineries able to process heavier crude, for example Valero Energy (VLO), Marathon Petroleum (MPC) or PBF Energy (PBF). Venezuela has historically been associated mainly with heavier crude, so the impact depends on whether the barrel actually reaches a port and whether it will not be more expensive logistically or legally. The criteria: shipping costs, tanker availability, the sanctions regime, blend quality and refining margins.

American shale, on the other hand, for example EOG Resources (EOG) or Diamondback Energy (FANG), could face pressure if additional oil supply reached the market over time. But careful: heavy Venezuelan crude is not a perfect substitute for light shale oil, so the impact would not be mechanical. With shale, watch hedging, production costs, capital spending discipline and the pace of production growth.

LPG is more an item to watch than a direct winner. Without clear investment in gas infrastructure, processing and exports, the impact is indirect.

With news like this, the biggest trap is mistaking a political map for an income statement. A better framework is not just a yes/no list, but a funnel: 1) is there a legally enforceable concession, 2) is someone financing real capex, 3) are there permits and export routes, 4) are service companies reporting orders, 5) are banks and insurers willing to handle the transaction, 6) do refineries have an economic reason to buy the barrel, 7) is the sanctions regime really changing, or is this just a political and security episode with no production impact.

The negative scenario is simple: the deal may remain a big headline with little production. Then, after the initial enthusiasm, the market would be dealing more with legal disputes, investigations, elections, sanctions and banks' reluctance to finance the project. The positive scenario requires boring things: wells, pipelines, service crews, insurance, invoices. On the stock market, boredom often beats fireworks.

Proven reserves are not the same as oil at the pump. It is like having a giant freezer full of food in the cellar, but no electricity, no key to the cellar and no car to take it to market. For shares, this means the reserves figure can raise attention and the valuation of the story, but money is made only when the oil is extracted, sold, insured, shipped and paid for. For ordinary people, it will show up in petrol prices only if the political deal turns into an actual supply of barrels to the world market.

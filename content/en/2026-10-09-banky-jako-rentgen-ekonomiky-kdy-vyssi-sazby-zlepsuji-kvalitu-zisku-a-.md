In bank results the key now is to tell whether higher rates genuinely improve earnings quality, or merely mask dearer deposits, weaker loans and a thinner capital cushion.

The market right now resembles an airport X-ray: bank results will show what the economy really has in its suitcase, while AI stories and oil beep on the next belt. Next week the big banks' earnings season starts, including JPMorgan Chase (JPM), Wells Fargo (WFC) and Goldman Sachs (GS), and attention is focused mainly on how the stocks react after the Fed rate rise mentioned in the report. On top of that comes uncertainty around OpenAI's revenue and cooling oil, two themes that can shift the mood outside finance itself.

It is not just about whether banks "beat estimates". That is accounting karaoke: someone hits the note, but the audience may boo anyway. More important is whether the results show that dearer money is starting to change the behaviour of households, companies and investors.

With banks it is not enough now to watch one nice margin. It is better to picture the results as a holiday bill in three columns: how much the hotel earned on rooms, how much it had to give guests in discounts, and how many guests ended up breaking the minibar and not paying. In banking language this is mainly net interest margin, meaning earnings from the gap between interest on loans and interest on deposits; deposit costs; deposit beta, meaning how quickly a bank must pass higher rates on to savers; and credit quality: provisions, the charge-off ratio (the share of unpaid loans written off), loan growth or contraction, and CET1 capital adequacy, put simply the bank's safety cushion.

At this stage of the cycle big banks are less like ordinary companies and more like a blood test of the economy. With JPMorgan Chase (JPM) the market may look at whether a strong deposit base still keeps deposit costs under control and whether growth in net interest income isn't outweighed by rising provisions or loan write-offs. With Wells Fargo (WFC) the sensitivity of ordinary banking business to rates matters more: net interest margin, deposit beta, loan volume, and whether clients are starting to brake on consumer and corporate borrowing. With Goldman Sachs (GS) the X-ray points elsewhere: less about classic deposits, more about companies' willingness to do deals, underwriting and transactions, plus whether CET1 capital and risk-weighted assets are limiting return on capital.

So the fresh filter is simple: a healthy bank result has margin without deposits fleeing, loan growth without desperate pushing of loans, and provisions without the smell of burning. A risky result can look great in the headline, but inside it turns out the bank is only temporarily benefiting from higher rates, while savers want more, borrowers weaken and the capital cushion thins.

The unexpected link with OpenAI is that both themes test the same question: how much are investors willing to pay for future revenue when money isn't free? For AI companies and related tech names, uncertainty around revenue resembles a luxury restaurant that is fully booked, but where it is not yet clear how many guests will pay for the full menu and how many will just have tap water. For banks the question is the reverse: cash is already flowing, but the market is checking whether it will start to be eaten into by loan losses, dearer deposits and weaker capital markets.

Oil adds a third filter. Cooler oil can ease inflationary pressure, which is more pleasant for consumers and for companies dependent on transport. At the same time, though, it can signal weaker demand, and so a worse outlook for energy producers.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It can help banks with a resilient deposit base and diversified income, typically big houses such as JPMorgan Chase (JPM), if they show that net interest margin isn't bought with a jump in deposit costs and that provisions, the charge-off ratio and CET1 capital stay under control. Wells Fargo (WFC) is an example of a bank where the market may be sensitive to net interest income — the difference between what the bank collects on loans and what it pays for deposits — but also to deposit beta and loan growth or contraction. Goldman Sachs (GS), in turn, shows the temperature of investment banking: if companies trade less and list on the stock exchange less, it hurts fee income, even though classic interest margin is not the main engine of the story.

On the tech side, uncertainty around OpenAI's revenue may weigh on sentiment along the AI chain: chipmakers such as NVIDIA (NVDA), cloud platforms such as Microsoft (MSFT), Amazon (AMZN) and Alphabet (GOOGL), but also software companies that build valuations on future AI monetisation. That is not automatically negative; more a reminder that AI enthusiasm must eventually meet the invoice.

Cheaper oil can bring relief to airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL), logistics such as FedEx (FDX) and consumer companies with high transport costs. Oil producers and drilling services, by contrast, such as Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX) or Schlumberger (SLB), may face pressure if the price fall reflects weaker demand.

With stories like this it is useful not to watch only the "profit better/worse" headline. A better framework has six questions: 1) Is banks' income growing because business is strengthening, or only because rates are temporarily helping net interest margin? 2) Are deposit costs and deposit beta rising faster than the market expected? 3) Are loans deteriorating through provisions and the charge-off ratio, or is the client staying healthy? 4) Is loan volume growing healthily, or is lending being choked? 5) Does CET1 capital hold an adequate cushion for a worse scenario? 6) Does the stock react opposite to the numbers, for example a good profit and a falling price? That often means the market is weighing the quality of the result, not its cosmetics.

The main risk: the market may celebrate lower oil one day as good news for inflation and read it the next day as a warning of weaker demand. The same data event, a different story.

Deposit beta is like a child who finds out the neighbour gets more pocket money. The higher the deposit beta, the faster a bank must raise interest on deposits so that clients don't run off elsewhere. The impact is simple: when banks' deposit costs rise quickly, their margin can thin, even if rates look favourable at first glance. For the market this means a handsome bank profit may be of lower quality; for ordinary people it affects interest on savings accounts, the availability of loans and the mood in stocks.

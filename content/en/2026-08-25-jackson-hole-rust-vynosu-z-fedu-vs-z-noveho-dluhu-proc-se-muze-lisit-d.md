With news like this, distinguish whether yields are rising because of the Fed or because of a glut of new debt — the impact on stocks can be completely different.

This time Jackson Hole is not just a festival of central bankers, but an auction for the last free chair at the table of capital. In the current Jackson Hole preview, Warsh is portrayed as a voice that wants markets to price reality unfiltered, while Bessent is buying long-dated US Treasuries because, in his view, yields no longer reflect fundamentals. On top of that, AI hyperscalers issued 220 bn USD of debt by 10 August, against 12.5 bn USD in the comparable period of 2025 — and the Treasury is competing for practically the same capital.

This is not just a "rates up/down" debate. It is a clash of three different channels that often get lumped together: expectations for Fed rates, the term premium and the supply of debt. The term premium is the extra return an investor demands for holding a long-dated bond and taking on the risk of time. When a lot of long-term debt suddenly appears — government and corporate — the market may want a higher yield simply because its plate is full, not necessarily because it expects a tougher Fed.

The fresh parallel is not the dot-com bubble in stocks, but the build-out of fibre-optic telecom networks at the turn of the millennium. Back then the long-term thesis was right: the internet really did change the world. But a large part of the infrastructure was financed with debt, and some companies found that being right about the future is not enough when, in the meantime, the oxygen of capital becomes more expensive. AI may similarly be the "right direction", but debt financing changes the pace, the margins and the sensitivity of valuations.

Warsh's stance can be read as faith in the market as an uncomfortable auditor. Bessent's stance as faith that the auditor is currently hysterical. The practical difference? If yields are rising because of the Fed, a change in rate expectations will help. If they are rising because of the term premium and a glut of issuance, one dovish speech is not enough. The market wants to know: who else is going to turn up with an outstretched hand?

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The plus side: the investment banks and bond syndicates that arrange the issues — for example JPMorgan Chase (JPM), Goldman Sachs (GS) or Morgan Stanley (MS). For them, a high volume of corporate issuance is business traffic, not a philosophical question.

The impact is mixed for AI hyperscalers such as Microsoft (MSFT), Alphabet (GOOGL), Amazon (AMZN), Meta Platforms (META) or Oracle (ORCL). Debt lets them finance data centres, chips and energy infrastructure without immediately diluting shareholders. But higher yields raise the cost of future expansion and raise the bar for returns on AI investment.

Makers of chips and AI infrastructure such as NVIDIA (NVDA), Broadcom (AVGO), AMD (AMD), Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSM), Vertiv (VRT) or Super Micro Computer (SMCI) can benefit from continuing orders. The risk is the second round: if hyperscaler financing gets more expensive, the market will start examining whether demand for capacity will grow as fast as the debt.

The minus side is long-duration growth stocks — software, fintech and companies whose valuations rest on distant earnings. Examples of the impact could be companies such as Toast (TOST), Snowflake (SNOW) or Datadog (DDOG). Not because they necessarily issue the same debt, but because higher long-term yields make the maths of future earnings less valuable.

The risk with news like this is mistaking "yields are high" for "the Fed must be hawkish". Sometimes the problem is simpler: there is a lot of paper for sale on the market and little patient capital.

The term premium is like the surcharge for booking a table on New Year's Eve. When the whole city wants to eat out on the same night, the restaurant charges more — not because it has changed the chef, but because demand for seats exceeds capacity. In the market, this means long-dated bonds can carry a higher yield even without new inflation or a tougher Fed. For stocks this matters: higher long-term yields make corporate financing more expensive and lower today's value of profits that will only arrive many years from now. For the ordinary person, this may eventually show up as more expensive loans, more cautious companies and greater nervousness in pension portfolios.

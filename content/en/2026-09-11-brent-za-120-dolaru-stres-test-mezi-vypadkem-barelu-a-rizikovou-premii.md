In oil shocks, watch the difference between an actual loss of barrels and a risk premium on shipping; confusing the two is often what leads to overshooting market conclusions.

Oil at 120 dollars isn't a prophecy of a gasoline apocalypse; it's the price tag on the possibility that the Persian Gulf turns into a shipping lottery. Goldman Sachs, according to commentary by Daan Struyven, still treats continued oil exports from the Persian Gulf as its base case, but calls Brent at 120 USD plausible if attacks on energy infrastructure and tankers intensify and exports stop growing.

The key isn't only how many barrels physically disappear from the market. More important is that oil is a market of the last barrel: the price is often set not by the average barrel, but by the hardest, most expensive and riskiest barrel that someone has to deliver right now.

That's why the wording about stagnating exports matters more than it seems. If the market was counting on exports from the region continuing smoothly, even a mere halt in supply growth can act like tightening the tap. Not because oil would run out immediately, but because the behavior of every link in the chain changes: shipowners want higher rates for risky routes, insurers raise the price of war-risk cover, buyers build up inventories earlier than they planned, and traders pay more for certainty of delivery.

This is the oil version of financial leverage. A small change in the probability of trouble can trigger a large change in price, because logistics can't be inflated with a mouse click. A tanker isn't an email. When a ship, port or terminal drops out of the equation, replacing it takes weeks to months, and the market charges for that uncertainty in advance.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The upside is cleanest for oil producers with prices tied to Brent and limited hedging of their output. Examples: Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), Shell (SHEL), BP (BP), TotalEnergies (TTE) or APA (APA). A higher Brent can translate into higher realized prices for them, but only where it isn't wiped out by hedging contracts, taxes, service costs or production outages. In their reports, it makes sense to watch the realized price per barrel, production volume, capital expenditure, free cash flow after dividends, and the notes on the hedge book, i.e. how much output has been sold in advance at a fixed price.

Oilfield service providers such as SLB (SLB) and Halliburton (HAL) can also benefit, if high prices last long enough for producers to raise their budgets. Tanker companies such as Frontline (FRO) or International Seaways (INSW) can profit from higher freight rates, but at the same time they carry operational and security risk.

On the other side are the airlines Delta Air Lines (DAL), United Airlines (UAL) and American Airlines (AAL), because fuel is among their most sensitive costs. Refiners such as Valero Energy (VLO) and Marathon Petroleum (MPC) are a mixed case: pricier crude is a cost, but margins depend on whether gasoline, diesel and jet fuel also get more expensive. Chemical companies such as Dow (DOW) or LyondellBasell (LYB) face pressure through inputs and energy. And in the end it also hits consumers through transport, food and inflation.

With news like this, it's better not to ask only where the price can go, but why. Checklist: 1) is it a real loss of barrels, or a risk premium on shipping; 2) are insurance and freight costs rising; 3) are inventories and the shape of the futures curve changing, i.e. the difference between near and distant contracts; 4) do companies have hedged prices, or do they float fully with the market; 5) is there a threat of a second round through inflation and central banks. The biggest trap is mistaking a geopolitical headline for an automatic gain in energy stocks.

A risk premium is a surcharge for nervousness. Imagine a courier bringing you a pizza through a neighborhood where streets sometimes get blocked. Even if no street is closed today, the courier asks for more, because he risks a delay, a detour or damage. It's similar with oil: the barrel may still be flowing, but the market pays extra for the possibility that the barrel's journey will be slower, more expensive or more dangerous. The impact? Energy can get more expensive before an actual shortage occurs, which puts pressure on companies' costs and on household wallets.

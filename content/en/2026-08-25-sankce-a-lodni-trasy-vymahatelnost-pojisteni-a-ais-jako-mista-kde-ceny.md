With sanctions, watch above all enforceability and concrete market traces — tanker rates, the Brent–Dubai spread, the discount on Iranian oil, insurance, AIS behaviour of ships, flows to Asia and OFAC lists; a sharp headline alone does not yet tell you where the market will actually get stuck.

Sanctions against Iran are not an on/off switch for oil; more of a dimmer, where nobody knows who currently has a hand on the dial.

According to the report, the US administration is launching a framework called "Operation Economic Outcast", aimed at further isolating Iran and putting economic pressure on it. Alongside that, the report also carries two side explosives: Polestar (PSNY) claims the administration left it in uncertainty ahead of a US ban, and the government is proposing a new six-figure fee for H-1B visas.

The biggest mistake with news like this is to expect an immediate, cinematic explosion in the oil price. Modern sanctions often work not like a cut cable, but like a tax on fear: every carrier, bank, insurer and trader asks themselves, "Will this cargo, payment or ship end up hanging around my neck?"

That is the essence of today's sanctions world. The state does not have to physically guard every barrel. It is enough to convince private companies that contact with Iranian flows of money, oil or goods may cost more than the profit from the trade. It is like a publican who does not throw out a troublesome guest himself, but calls the taxi drivers, the beer supplier and the landlord: "Whoever helps him has a problem too." Suddenly the doors do not need to be locked; it is enough that everyone around steps back.

The newer and more important layer is not just "oil versus geopolitics", but a second market alongside the oil market: the market for passage. A barrel may exist, and it may even have a buyer, but if the ship, the insurance policy, the financing and the legal check of origin all get more expensive, it behaves in practice like an item in an online shop that is in stock, but the courier tells you: "Delivery unknown, shipping cost will be a surprise." The price then pays not just for the oil. It also pays for the nerves of everyone who touches it.

The counterintuitive point: harder pressure does not have to mean less oil on the market straight away. Often it first means less visible oil. The history of sanctions shows that flows can be redirected, disguised through intermediaries, discounted against comparable barrels or sent through more complicated logistics. What is expensive for the market is precisely the opacity: traders pay a premium for the possibility that tomorrow it will not be clear where a barrel came from, who insured it and whether the payment will go through.

That is why the first impact may show up more in spreads, insurance, shipping and bank caution than in the headline oil price itself. In other words: oil does not have to leap straight away like a cat at a cucumber; it may first start hissing in the pipes of financing and logistics.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

A higher geopolitical risk premium may help oil and gas producers, especially the upstream part of the business, meaning the companies that extract the raw material. Examples: Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), Shell (SHEL) or BP (BP). It is not an automatic benefit for the whole energy sector: refining and petrochemicals may suffer if more expensive inputs cannot be passed on to customers.

It may hurt airlines and transport, where fuel is a sensitive cost item. Typical examples are Delta Air Lines (DAL), United Airlines (UAL), FedEx (FDX) or UPS (UPS). If the market starts to reprice the risk of more expensive oil, these stocks often deal with the question of margins before the question of revenue.

Financial services are on both sides. Large global banks such as JPMorgan Chase (JPM), Citigroup (C), HSBC (HSBC) or Standard Chartered (STAN.L) may face higher compliance costs, meaning the costs of checking that they are not financing a prohibited flow. On the other hand, specialist providers of screening, legal and payment processes may benefit from the world becoming bureaucratically more complicated.

The side mention of Polestar (PSNY) points to another risk: rules for China-linked supply chains in electric vehicles may hit not just a specific brand, but also sentiment around EV companies in general, including Tesla (TSLA), Rivian (RIVN) or Lucid (LCID), depending on their exposure to production, components and regulation.

And the proposed six-figure fee for H-1B visas? If it passed in a hard form, it would raise the cost of importing specialist talent. That is sensitive mainly for tech and AI companies such as Microsoft (MSFT), Alphabet (GOOGL), Amazon (AMZN), Meta Platforms (META), and also for IT outsourcing such as Infosys (INFY), Tata Consultancy Services (TCS.NS) or Cognizant (CTSH).

With sanctions news, it is not just the headline that decides, but enforceability. A better framework: look at the difference between political rhetoric and a concrete rule. Is it a ban, or just a threat? Are the targets Iranian entities, or third parties too? Is there a transition period? Does the measure concern oil, payments, insurance, ships, technology, or persons?

With oil, watch mainly measurable data traces, not just dramatic statements. First, tanker rates, meaning the rates for shipping oil on key routes: if they are rising faster than oil itself, the problem may lie in shipping capacity and risk. Second, the Brent–Dubai spread, meaning the difference between the price of European Brent crude and the Dubai benchmark for the Asian market; a widening or a sudden narrowing may indicate whether Asia is paying more for replacement barrels or, on the contrary, taking discounted flows. Third, the discount on Iranian oil against similar barrels: a bigger discount often says buyers want compensation for legal, payment and reputational risk.

The fourth block is logistics: the availability of insurance, premium rates on risky routes, the involvement of smaller or harder-to-trace tankers, and the behaviour of AIS, the ship transponders that show position. More AIS switch-offs do not automatically mean a supply outage, but they increase opacity. The fifth block is flows to China and other Asian buyers: if volumes hold up but the discount and the complexity of routes grow, the market is not dealing with a shortage of barrels, but with a more expensive way to reach them. And the sixth block is OFAC, the US sanctions office: new entries on sanctions lists, affected ships, intermediaries, banks and insurance links often say more than a political slogan.

With banks, watch updates to sanctions lists and the tone of regulatory fines. With the H-1B topic, the key is whether the proposal survives legal challenges, exemptions and practical implementation.

Secondary sanctions are like a school that punishes not only the pupil who copied, but everyone who lent them a crib sheet, a pen or a backpack. The impact? Companies and banks would rather back away even from deals that might have gone through, because they fear the consequences. For the market, this means more uncertainty, more expensive logistics and occasionally sharper moves in energy prices; for the ordinary person, it may show up through fuel prices, air fares, or swings in the shares of companies sensitive to oil and regulation.

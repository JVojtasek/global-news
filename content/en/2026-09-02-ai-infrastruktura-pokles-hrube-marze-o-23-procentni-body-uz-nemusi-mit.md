With AI infrastructure, watch whether revenue growth is coming at the expense of margins; a drop in gross margin of 2–3 percentage points below company guidance is a warning mainly when it exceeds the normal quarterly fluctuation of the company and cannot be explained by mix or a one-off effect.

In the AI factory today, the talk is not only about the smartest worker, but about the conveyor belt that keeps up with bringing him parts. According to the report, Credo Technology Group (CRDO) below the 200 USD mark is presented as attractively valued thanks to demand for AI infrastructure: Q1 revenue reached 479 million USD, +114.7 % year on year, adjusted gross margin 68 % and non-GAAP net margin 49.3 %. Management also expects Q2 revenue of 525–535 million USD and a gross margin of 67–69 %.

The most interesting thing about Credo is not just the growth. What is interesting is that the AI market is slowly moving from the phase of admiring the brains to the phase of dealing with the nervous system. NVIDIA (NVDA) and similar accelerator makers are the brain of the data centre. But a brain without nerves is a philosopher in a concrete room: he may have brilliant ideas, but he cannot send them anywhere.

Credo belongs to the layer that handles connectivity and data transfer inside AI infrastructure. That is less sexy than GPUs, but often more critical than a layperson thinks. With AI clusters, it is not just about how many compute chips you have, but how fast they talk to each other. When thousands of chips are waiting for data, it is like a restaurant full of Michelin chefs where the only waiter carries the orders on a scooter with a flat tyre.

Here is the point: a high gross margin at an infrastructure supplier is not automatic proof that the product cannot be replaced. In hardware, it can also be inflated by a favourable customer mix, the ramp-up phase of production, a one-off strong order, accounting adjustments or a high concentration of buyers. The better reading is therefore different: if revenue grows at triple-digit rates and gross margin holds around 68 %, so far it does not look like growth bought with massive discounts. But margin on its own is not a halo. It is more of a smoke detector: useful, as long as you know it sometimes beeps because of burnt toast.

That is why the 2–3 percentage point threshold is only a working warning threshold, not a law of physics. It makes sense mainly against the company's own guidance and against the usual spread of margins in recent quarters. With more diversified players such as Broadcom (AVGO) or Arista Networks (ANET), the interpretation of margins tends to be different than with smaller, fast-growing suppliers, where one large customer or the product mix can move the margin more. Marvell Technology (MRVL), in turn, is an example of a company where several end markets meet, so gross margin alone without the revenue mix can be misleading.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It helps companies tied to data centres, AI connectivity and high-speed network infrastructure: Credo Technology Group (CRDO), Broadcom (AVGO), Marvell Technology (MRVL) or Arista Networks (ANET) are examples of companies where the market is watching whether AI capex flows not only into GPUs, but also into the network layer.

Indirectly, it can support accelerator makers such as NVIDIA (NVDA) and Advanced Micro Devices (AMD), because strong demand for networking suggests that customers are not building display cases for chips, but entire factories for compute. That is a qualitatively different story from a one-off purchase of hardware.

Hyperscalers such as Microsoft (MSFT), Amazon (AMZN), Alphabet (GOOGL) and Meta Platforms (META) see a mixed effect. On one hand, better infrastructure helps them run AI services. On the other hand, every cluster expansion means more capital expenditure, pressure on free cash flow and the question of whether AI investment will pay back in service prices.

It is less favourable for companies that sell general software without a direct link to AI infrastructure, for example Progress Software (PRGS) as a contrasting example to watch. Their story rests more on contract renewals and cost discipline than on the infrastructure wave of data centres.

With news like this, do not look only at revenue growth. Watch three levers: pace, margin and conversion into profit. For Credo, the Q2 guidance is 525–535 million USD against Q1 479 million USD, i.e. roughly +9.6 to +11.7 % quarter on quarter. If in the following periods the pace stayed at least in the high single digits and gross margin held near the 67–69 % band, it would tell a story of robust demand without visible price bleeding.

The warning framework: a drop in gross margin of 2–3 percentage points carries weight mainly when it is below the lower edge of company guidance and at the same time larger than the normal quarterly fluctuation of the given company and its closest peers. In practice: for Credo, against guidance of 67–69 %, the sensitive zone would start roughly below 65–66 %, but only if management does not explain it by mix, the ramp-up of a new product or a one-off effect. For Broadcom and Arista, the market would often read a similar move more strictly, because their business is broader and more stable; for Marvell or Credo, it is necessary to check more closely the mix of AI, enterprise, carrier and any customer concentration. When gross margin holds but net margin weakens markedly, watch operating costs, new investment and customer concentration. In the valuation of similar companies, what often decides is whether the market believes in a long cash-flow ramp, or only in a short burst of enthusiasm around AI.

Gross margin is the difference between what a company sells a product for and what it costs to make it. Imagine a coffee stand: when you sell a cup at a high price and the beans, milk and cup cost you relatively little, you have room to pay rent, people and growth. But when you have to start giving every second coffee at a discount, revenue can still grow, but the business is no longer as healthy. For Credo, a high gross margin means the market does not yet see major price bleeding; it does not, however, automatically mean its products have no competition. For stocks this matters, because revenue growth with a firm margin tends to be much stronger for valuations than growth that has to be bought with discounts. For the ordinary person, it says in practice: the AI boom is not happening only in apps, but also in expensive infrastructure whose costs may over time feed into the prices of cloud and AI services.

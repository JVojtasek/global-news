## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Five Whys travelled from Toyota-style shop-floor problem solving into meeting rooms, templates and post-incident reviews, often reduced to five lines on a form.

**What it means.** Repeatedly asking why can push a team beyond a symptom, but the fifth answer does not become a root cause merely because it is fifth.

**Risks and impact.** A straight chain can conceal several interacting causes, reward confident guesses and end at “human error,” producing blame or a fix that leaves the failure intact.

**What can be done.** Define the event precisely, observe the work, branch plausible explanations, test each important link and trial a countermeasure before treating the conclusion as established.

**What to watch.** If another informed group can build a different chain from the same event, or if the proposed fix cannot be tested, the analysis is still a hypothesis.

## FACTS

Five Whys is a questioning technique, not a statistical test or an investigation standard. The number is memorable, but it is not a required stopping point. An Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality job aid says to ask why five times **or as many times as needed**, then design and test a countermeasure.

The familiar example starts with a stopped machine. An overload blew a fuse; the bearing lacked lubrication; the pump was not delivering enough; its shaft was worn; and metal scrap entered because there was no strainer. That sequence turns attention from replacing a fuse to controlling contamination.

Its value is the movement from a visible event toward a process condition that can be changed. Its weakness is equally clear: every answer determines the next question. If the second answer is an assumption, the remaining three may create a polished explanation of the wrong problem.

## EVIDENCE

Toyota’s 2003 account of the “Thinking Production System” described TPS as a strategy for developing people, not a collection of devices. It traced the system to trial and error around practical problems. The context matters: the method was tied to observation, technical knowledge and learning in the work, rather than a remote facilitator counting questions.

Lean Enterprise Institute author Michael Ballé made that dependence explicit in 2012. He wrote that most technical problems are multi-cause and multi-impact, and that potential factors should be identified and tested before the first why. Someone with less intimate knowledge of a machine could take the classic chain to a completely different conclusion.

The limitation is not merely theoretical. In a peer-reviewed critique in *BMJ Quality & Safety*, safety scientist Alan J. Card argued that Five Whys can create an arbitrary stopping point and encourage a single causal path in systems where events have multiple contributors. The article was published online on 2 September 2016 and appeared in the journal’s August 2017 issue.

AHRQ’s companion use of fishbone diagrams offers one practical correction. Teams first list possible causes across categories such as people, materials, methods, measurement, environment, and policies and procedures. They can then apply repeated whys to the important branches rather than forcing every event into one line.

## PERSPECTIVES

For a manager, Five Whys is attractive because it is fast, cheap and legible. Those are real advantages. A ten-minute inquiry can expose a missing handoff or an absent standard that everyone had learned to work around.

For the person doing the work, the same exercise can feel different. “Why did you do that?” easily becomes an interrogation, especially when hierarchy is present. Questions about conditions—what information was available, what normally happens, what changed—are more likely to reveal the system.

For a quality or safety investigator, a single chain is often insufficient. Equipment state, staffing, incentives, design, maintenance and timing may combine. The NHS Wales guide positions Five Whys for easier or moderately difficult problems and advises other improvement tools for complex problems.

For a customer, the label matters less than recurrence. A neat root-cause slide is not evidence of learning if the defect, delay or outage returns.

## CONTEXT

Management methods often become simpler as they travel. A practice embedded in a factory’s technical routines becomes a worksheet; a coaching habit becomes a rule; “keep asking” becomes “stop at five.” The export makes the idea accessible, but it can remove the conditions that made it useful.

That is especially risky around the phrase “root cause.” A plant can sometimes isolate a dominant physical mechanism. A service failure may instead emerge from several individually ordinary conditions: an ambiguous instruction, a queue, a missing alert and a deadline. Removing one contributor may reduce risk without revealing a single deepest root.

There is also a difference between explanation and intervention. “Training was inadequate” sounds causal, but it is too broad until the team identifies which decision required which knowledge, how competence was checked and why the existing system did not make the right action easier. “The employee forgot” describes the last visible act while leaving the surrounding design untouched.

Toyota’s own account is useful here because it resists tool worship. The “T” in its formulation stood for thinking. Five Whys is best remembered as a discipline for extending inquiry and developing knowledge, not as a certificate attached to the final box.

## PEOPLE

Good causal analysis needs the people closest to the process, but it should not make them defendants. Operators, customer-support staff, maintenance workers and users often know the exceptions that formal procedures omit. Their participation improves the model only when they can contradict a manager without penalty.

Language changes the room. “Why did you fail to check?” points at a person. “What made the check unavailable or easy to miss at that moment?” opens questions about workload, interface design, tools and standards. Individual choices still matter; the aim is to understand rather than preselect where responsibility must end.

The investigation team also needs diversity of knowledge. Frontline experience shows what actually happens, technical specialists can test mechanisms, and data owners can check whether the pattern extends beyond one memorable case.

## DEEPER

Use an evidence-backed version of Five Whys instead of a five-line ritual.

1. **State the event.** Record what happened, where and when, what should have happened and the measurable gap. “Orders are late” is too broad; “18 of 120 Tuesday orders missed the 16:00 dispatch cut-off” can be investigated.
2. **Go to the current state.** Observe the work, inspect records and build a short timeline. Separate facts from recollections and assumptions.
3. **Branch before narrowing.** List plausible contributors. For a complex case, use a fishbone diagram or event timeline so that equipment, methods, information and conditions are not squeezed into one chain.
4. **Mark every answer.** Label each link “observed,” “supported by data” or “hypothesis.” Ask what evidence would disprove it. Do not let seniority substitute for verification.
5. **Stop by usefulness, not arithmetic.** Continue while another why produces a meaningful, testable process condition. Stop when the team reaches an actionable cause it can influence and support with evidence—not automatically at question five.
6. **Test the countermeasure.** Change one relevant control, define the expected result and monitor recurrence. If the outcome does not improve, reopen the chain rather than defending it.

This process takes longer than filling five boxes, but it prevents an even costlier cycle: solving the wrong cause, celebrating closure and meeting the same failure again.

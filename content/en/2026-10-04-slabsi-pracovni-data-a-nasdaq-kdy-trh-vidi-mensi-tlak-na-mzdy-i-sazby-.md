Weaker jobs data help stocks only when, in the detail, they look like cooling pressure on wages and rates, not the start of a crack in demand, hours worked and company profits.

Wall Street sometimes resembles a person who cheers because the car has started rattling strangely — since it means they may not have to drive so fast. The September jobs report was unremarkable, which eased fears of further Fed rate rises. The result: stocks rose, the tech-heavy Nasdaq most of all, because technology is extremely sensitive to rate expectations.

This reaction is not just the classic "bad data = good for stocks". The more precise reading is: the market is not celebrating economic weakness, but a lower risk that the central bank will have to tighten.

And here is the difference from the usual commentary on rates: a jobs report is not one number but a check-up of the car before a long journey. Payrolls say how many jobs were added. The unemployment rate shows how many people lack work. Hourly wage growth is, for the Fed, either an inflation brake or an accelerator. Hours worked reveal whether firms still need output or are already cutting shifts. Participation says how many people take part in the labour market at all. And revisions to previous months are the accounting eraser: sometimes you discover the engine has not been rattling since yesterday, but for three months.

That is why a weaker headline is neither bullish nor bearish on its own. A report in which payrolls are weaker, wages are cooling and hours worked hold up reads one way — for the market that is the story of "the Fed does not need to push so hard". A combination of weaker payrolls, rising unemployment, shorter hours and negative revisions reads quite differently — that starts to suggest firms are not just hiring less but may begin cutting.

The key is that the labour market is, for the Fed, like a thermometer in a furnace. When it is too hot, there is a risk of rising wages, price pressure and further tightening of monetary policy. When the temperature drops a little, the market tells itself: perhaps there is no longer any need to stoke the boiler with rates.

The fresh point: on a day like this, tech stocks often react not primarily to their own business but to "the mathematics of the distant future". Companies such as NVIDIA (NVDA), Microsoft (MSFT), Apple (AAPL) or Alphabet (GOOGL) hold a large part of their value in expected future profits. And future profits are like a cake promised for your next birthday: the higher the "price of waiting" in the form of rates, the less it tastes good today.

That is why an unremarkable jobs report can lift the Nasdaq more than industrial or bank stocks. Not because a weaker labour market automatically benefits technology, but because it reduces pressure on the discount rate — the rate the market uses to convert future money into today's value.

But watch the boundary. There are two kinds of weak data: "soft landing" and "something is breaking". The first helps stocks because it tames the Fed. The second hurts because it threatens revenue and profits. The difference is like a runner slowing down to last the marathon, versus slowing down because they are running out of oxygen.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It mainly helps growth and technology names whose value is sensitive to rates: chipmakers such as NVIDIA (NVDA) and Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSM), cloud and software such as Microsoft (MSFT), Salesforce (CRM) or ServiceNow (NOW), and also big platforms such as Alphabet (GOOGL) or Meta Platforms (META). The mechanics are simple: lower expectations of further policy tightening raise the appeal of more distant profits.

The difference, though, lies in the type of exposure. For software and platforms, the effect often shows first through valuation: the market recalculates future cash flow. For semiconductors it is a double game: lower yields can help valuation, but if jobs data later showed a sharper cooling of the economy, investors may start asking about corporate investment, demand for servers and the order cycle.

Banks such as JPMorgan Chase (JPM) or Bank of America (BAC) may react relatively more slowly. Lower rate pressure can help through credit risk, but at the same time it can narrow part of the interest margin if the market starts to expect falling yields. For cyclical companies — for example industrials such as Caterpillar (CAT) or carriers such as Union Pacific (UNP) — it depends on whether weaker jobs mean just cooling or a drop in demand.

Consumer companies have a double exposure. Amazon (AMZN) may benefit from the tech re-rating, but its e-commerce business would suffer if a weaker labour market hit household wallets. Likewise, retailers such as Walmart (WMT) or Target (TGT) watch whether people are merely changing jobs less, or starting to save.

With news like this, do not just watch the "jobs weaker/stronger" headline. A better checklist: 1) what did payrolls do, and also the revisions to past months? 2) are wages cooling, or still pushing on inflation? 3) are hours worked holding, or are firms cutting shifts? 4) is unemployment rising because of weaker demand, or is participation changing? 5) does the market take the data as a lower risk of higher rates, or as a recession threat? 6) what are bond yields doing, and do consumer-sensitive sectors confirm the move? A one-day rally may be relief, not a change of trend.

"Discount rate" sounds like a spell from a bank vault, but it is just the price of waiting. Imagine someone promises you a thousand crowns in five years. If in the meantime you can safely earn decent interest elsewhere, that future thousand carries less weight today. Tech stocks are similar: much of their value lies in profits that are due to arrive only later. When the market believes rates no longer need to rise, future profits look more valuable — and stocks can jump. For an ordinary person, though, this does not mean cheaper living right away; it mostly affects asset prices, pensions in investment portfolios and market mood.

Watch whether demand for AI infrastructure is long-term and how it shapes chipmakers' investment in HBM, which is a key indicator of where the memory and PC markets are heading.

The personal computer market is experiencing an unexpected paradox: despite a fall in units sold, average selling prices are rising. As recent market analyses show, this phenomenon is a direct consequence of growing demand for memory chips for artificial intelligence infrastructure, which is leading to shortages and higher costs for PC makers.

It is not just about a chip shortage; it is about a shift in value. Imagine a restaurant that makes the best steaks in town suddenly facing huge demand for a special spice used only on those steaks. The spice is suddenly scarce and expensive. The restaurant either has to raise the price of its steaks or sell fewer of them, but at a higher margin, because customers are willing to pay extra for quality and exclusivity.

Something similar is happening in the PC market. AI is that "special spice", which requires huge quantities of memory chips, especially HBM (High Bandwidth Memory). These chips are key to training and running large AI models because they offer significantly higher data throughput than standard DRAM. Making them is technologically demanding and capacity is limited – it requires complex 3D stacking of memory layers and advanced packaging. This means memory makers such as Micron Technology (MU) or Samsung are shifting their capacity and investment (CAPEX) towards producing these premium HBM chips for AI data centres, where margins, according to analysts' estimates, are significantly higher than for ordinary DRAM. Conventional memory for ordinary PCs is thus becoming scarce, which pushes its prices up. PC makers then either pass this higher price on into end products or focus on premium models with higher margins, while sales volume falls. So this is not a simple shortage, but a strategic allocation of resources towards the most lucrative segments of the market, which in this case is AI infrastructure.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

- Memory chip makers: Companies such as Micron Technology (MU), Samsung Electronics (005930.KS) and SK Hynix (000660.KS) benefit from higher memory prices and strong demand for HBM chips for AI. Their gross margins are improving, which may show up positively in their financial results and in their ability to fund further investment in research and development (R&D) and production capacity. - Makers of premium PCs: Companies focused on more expensive, more powerful machines (e.g. gaming PCs or workstations for professionals) can better absorb higher component costs and maintain margins thanks to customers' willingness to pay more for top performance. Examples may include some models from Dell Technologies (DELL) or HP Inc. (HPQ) aimed at the business segment or demanding users, where demand for performance is less price-elastic. (Note: This is an educational example of impact, not a recommendation to buy/sell or a promise of returns.)

- Makers of cheaper PCs and laptops: Companies relying on sales volume in lower price segments may face pressure on margins, because they cannot so easily pass higher memory costs on into end prices. This may affect their competitiveness and profitability. (Note: This is an educational example of impact, not a recommendation to buy/sell or a promise of returns.)

- Consumers: Ordinary customers looking for affordable computers may face higher prices or limited availability, which may lead them to postpone purchases or choose less powerful alternatives. (Note: This is an educational example of impact, not a recommendation to buy/sell or a promise of returns.)

With news like this, be careful to distinguish whether it is a short-term trend caused by a temporary shortage or a structural change in the market. In this case, demand for AI infrastructure appears to be long-term, which suggests that pressure on memory prices and the shift of value towards premium segments may persist. Watch chipmakers' investment in new production capacity and their focus on HBM, which may be a key indicator of future developments.

When people talk about "building infrastructure for artificial intelligence", picture it as a huge, ultra-modern factory for brains. These "brains" need enormous amounts of special "memories" to work – memory chips, specifically the high-bandwidth kind (HBM). But there is a limited supply of these super-memories, and making them is complicated, as if you had to stack miniature bricks on top of each other with surgical precision. When suddenly everyone is trying to build as many of these "AI brains" as possible, demand for those memories skyrockets. Memory makers then logically sell where they get the most money for them – to those AI factories. For us ordinary people, this means that when we want to buy an ordinary computer, which also needs some memories, they are suddenly more expensive because fewer are available. It is like everyone rushing to buy one type of building material, which then gets more expensive for every other project. For the market, this means companies making those super-memories for AI prosper, while makers of ordinary computers have to raise prices or accept smaller profits if they want to hold prices. For your wallet, it means a new computer will probably cost more than it would have before the AI boom.

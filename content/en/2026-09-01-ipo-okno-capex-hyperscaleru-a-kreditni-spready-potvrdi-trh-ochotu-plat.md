With AI financing, watch the headlines about enthusiasm less and watch more whether the IPO window, hyperscaler capex and credit spreads confirm the market's willingness to pay for growth.

The AI fever has two cash registers: one buys chips, the other sells dreams to investors. Kevin Foley, co-head of global investment banking at JPMorgan (JPM), spoke on CNBC about AI financing, the outlook for the IPO pipeline, higher rates, M&A and the mood in markets. The essence is not one particular deal, but the question of whether Wall Street is still willing to finance growth stories now that money is no longer almost free.

The IPO pipeline is not just a thermometer of mood. It is a recycling station for optimism.

When a technology company goes public, it is not just a new ticker that is born. Liquidity is born: employees can partly cash in their shares, venture funds have something to show their investors, later funding rounds get a reference price and banks collect fees. That is how capital returns to the system and can fund the next wave of startups. When the IPO window jams, it is not just a problem for a few founders in hoodies. It is like a restaurant where everyone pays with the company card, but the accounts department suddenly demands a receipt for every truffle.

With AI, it is even more interesting. The first wave is hard infrastructure: data centres, chips, networking gear, cooling, electricity. That is where companies with real orders and big customers dominate. The second wave is applications and models, where people often pay for the promise of future productivity. Higher rates punish exactly those distant profits, because a future dollar is worth less today. The counterintuitive part: AI can be enormous in the long run, and yet some AI valuations may have a short-term problem if the market starts wanting proof of monetisation instead of a presentation with a rocket on the last slide.

A practical reality check: an IPO of a company like Arm Holdings (ARM), which sells a licensing model within the semiconductor chain, is different from the IPO of a pure AI application company that is so far mainly burning cash. Both can carry the AI label, but one resembles a toll gate on a motorway and the other a food truck outside a concert: when the crowds stop coming, you quickly see the difference in resilience.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It helps investment banks if IPOs and M&A get going again: JPMorgan (JPM), Goldman Sachs (GS) and Morgan Stanley (MS) earn from advisory work, share underwriting and deal financing. Exchange infrastructure such as Nasdaq (NDAQ) or Intercontinental Exchange (ICE), the owner of the NYSE, could also see a positive impact if new listings pick up.

In technology, the impact is less black and white. Makers of AI infrastructure such as NVIDIA (NVDA), AMD (AMD), Broadcom (AVGO), Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSM) or ASML (ASML) benefit mainly from the capital spending of big customers. Networking and server supply chains such as Arista Networks (ANET), Dell Technologies (DELL), Super Micro Computer (SMCI) or Vertiv (VRT) are tied to the data centre build-out. For them, the most important thing is not the number of IPOs, but whether Microsoft (MSFT), Amazon (AMZN), Alphabet (GOOGL) and Meta Platforms (META) keep up high budgets for AI infrastructure. Here, watch mainly capex, that is, capital expenditure on long-term infrastructure, and management comments on whether spending is accelerating, stabilising or shifting into next year.

It hurts mainly companies that need outside capital before they show profitability: young AI software firms, late-stage startups waiting to list and growth technology companies with valuations built on distant cash flows. Private equity and venture players such as KKR (KKR), Blackstone (BX) or Apollo (APO) may suffer from weaker exits, but in a colder market they can also buy more cheaply. That is a typical Wall Street paradox: a closed door for the seller is sometimes an open window for the buyer.

With news like this, do not look for one magic sentence from a banker. Watch five verifiable indicators: the number of new S-1 and F-1 prospectuses for technology IPOs, changes to price ranges before pricing, how shares behave after the lock-up period, that is, after insiders' shares are released, hyperscaler comments on capital spending, and the trend in credit spreads, that is, the premiums investors demand for riskier debt.

How to read it more practically: if companies repeatedly cut their IPO price range or trade below the offer price even several weeks after listing, the willingness to pay for a story is weakening. If, after the usual lock-up period, often around 180 days, the share price holds up despite insider selling, the market is absorbing the supply more healthily. And if AI stocks are rising but credit spreads on riskier bonds are widening at the same time, that is a yellow warning light: the equity crowd is celebrating while the debt market is charging a higher insurance premium. A healthy market does not look like everything shooting up on day one. A healthy market looks like quality companies being able to raise capital without a circus cannon.

Think of the IPO pipeline as an airport runway for startups. When the weather is good, planes take off, gates free up and the airport makes money. When fog arrives in the form of higher rates and cautious investors, flights are delayed, companies burn cash and both employees and funds wait for their payout. For the market, this means fewer new shares, less money flowing back into the technology ecosystem and more pressure on AI companies to show not just a story but accounting reality.

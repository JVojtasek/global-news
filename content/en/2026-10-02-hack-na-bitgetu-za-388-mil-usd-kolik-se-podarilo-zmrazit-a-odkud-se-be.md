With crypto hacks, watch the frozen-to-stolen ratio, the size of the protection fund against the loss, and whether the damage is paid from genuinely strong reserves or only from client trust.

Crypto's biggest illusion is that anything visible on the blockchain must also be within reach — a bit like watching a thief through binoculars while the thief is already sitting on the plane.

According to the excerpt provided, Bitget froze roughly $1.1 million of the almost $388 million stolen last week after a cyberattack; CEO Gracy Chen also told CNBC that the platform "does not expect a major recovery" of the stolen funds. The report adds that Bitget topped its Protection Fund back up to $300 million, out of its own reserves.

This is not just about a hack. It is a hard lesson that "on-chain traceability" is not the same as "economic enforceability". The numbers in the source show a brutal mismatch: $1.1 million frozen out of almost $388 million is about 0.3%. In other words, the map exists, but the door to the vault is in another jurisdiction, behind another bridge, and possibly already repainted through mixers, exchanges and rapid transfers.

The fresh frame is this: after a hack at a crypto exchange, the key question is not only "how much was stolen" but who absorbs the loss. If the client bears it, it is a reputational fire. If the exchange bears it from reserves, it is a test of its balance sheet. If the token ecosystem bears it indirectly through trust, it is a tax on uncertainty.

A Protection Fund looks like an airbag. But an airbag in a car only makes sense if the frame still holds after the crash. The excerpt says the fund was topped back up to $300 million from the company's own reserves. That is positive for calming clients right away, but the analytically more important question is: how much reserve is left after that, and how quickly could the platform withstand a second blow? The report provided does not say.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It hurts above all crypto exchanges and fintech services that live on trust in custody — that is, safekeeping of assets. Examples of exposure can be found among listed companies tied to crypto trading and retail order flow, such as Coinbase Global (COIN), Robinhood Markets (HOOD), Block (SQ) or PayPal (PYPL). That does not mean they have the same problem as Bitget; the mechanism is about sentiment and regulation: after a big hack, investors and clients ask again who holds the keys, how assets are segregated and who pays for the damage.

It potentially helps cybersecurity and forensic services. Listed firms such as Palo Alto Networks (PANW), CrowdStrike (CRWD) or Cloudflare (NET) are not direct beneficiaries of this particular loss, but events like it increase pressure on exchanges, custody services and fintechs to spend more on prevention, monitoring, private-key management and incident response.

The impact on crypto itself is mixed. Bitcoin or ether need not fall just because one platform was hacked. But repeated incidents raise a "trust risk premium": some capital may want higher compensation for holding assets in infrastructure where a mistake is not a shoe return but occasionally an open channel into outer space.

With news like this, it makes sense to follow four questions:

- Frozen-to-stolen ratio — here, according to the source, about $1.1 million out of almost $388 million, roughly 0.3%. The lower the number, the smaller the practical return on tracing.

- Size of the protection fund against the loss — here the fund stands at $300 million after the top-up, while the amount stolen was almost $388 million. A fund is not the same as unlimited insurance.

- Source of the top-up — here, own reserves. That reassures clients, but it also shifts the question to the strength of the balance sheet.

- The second blow — one incident can be absorbed on the books; a series of incidents tests whether this was a fortress or just nicely painted scenery.

A Protection Fund is like a kitty for broken windows in an apartment block. When someone breaks one window, the building manager pays for it and the tenants feel calmer. But when a thief carries off half the building, it is not enough to know the kitty has been refilled — you want to know how much money the manager has left for the next mess. For the market, this means it is not just the theft itself being assessed, but the platform's ability to remain credible without fear spilling into client outflows and pressure on the whole crypto sector.

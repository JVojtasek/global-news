## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Psychologist Robert Provine spent decades studying yawning, laughter and other behaviours that usually happen before people have time to explain them.

**What it means.** A yawn is not merely a sign of boredom. It clusters around changes of state, can be triggered by seeing or thinking about yawns and appears across many vertebrate species.

**Risks and impact.** Popular explanations outrun the evidence. Contagious yawning is not a dependable empathy test, and an individual yawn cannot reveal someone’s motives.

**What can be done.** Treat the behaviour as a clue about state and context, not a moral signal. Persistent or unusual symptoms belong in a clinical conversation, not an internet theory.

**What to watch.** Competing explanations—arousal, coordination, brain temperature and social attention—may describe different functions rather than one universal cause.

## FACTS

Provine’s method was to make the ordinary strange enough to measure. At the University of Maryland, Baltimore County, he asked what happened before and after a yawn, which visual features could release one and whether familiar folk explanations survived simple experiments.

His work helped show that yawning is a stereotyped action pattern: once it begins, it tends to run through a recognisable sequence. It often appears near waking and sleeping or during other transitions in alertness. Seeing a yawning face can trigger a yawn in some observers; so can hearing one, reading about one or even discussing it. Not everyone responds, and the same person may not respond every time.

The old oxygen story performed poorly. Experiments cited in Provine’s research found that breathing extra oxygen or modestly elevated carbon dioxide did not abolish yawning. That result did not reveal the true function, but it removed one tidy explanation from the front row.

## EVIDENCE

Provine used images that isolated parts of the yawning face. His 1989 paper treated faces as “releasers” and asked which visual information mattered. The larger contribution was methodological: a behaviour associated with etiquette and anecdote could be placed under controlled observation.

Later researchers proposed a thermoregulatory account. Studies reviewed by Andrew and Gordon Gallup reported that yawning varies within a window of ambient temperature, consistent with a possible role in cooling the brain. The evidence is intriguing, particularly because it generates testable predictions. It is not a final explanation of every yawn in every species.

The social account is equally complicated. Contagious yawning has been linked to empathy because it resembles an automatic response to another person’s state. Yet a 2017 review found the evidence inconsistent and inconclusive, highlighting confounders including visual attention and social inhibition. A Duke study of 328 people found age explained a small part of susceptibility, while empathy measures did not strongly explain the variation.

The honest conclusion is narrower than the headline: contagious yawning is a stable and measurable phenomenon for many people; why susceptibility differs remains largely unexplained.

## PERSPECTIVES

For an evolutionary biologist, yawning’s wide distribution suggests an old mechanism rather than a uniquely human social message. Its function may involve changing arousal, coordinating activity or regulating physiology. Similar-looking behaviour need not perform exactly the same job in every animal.

For a social psychologist, contagion matters because the stimulus is another individual. Attention, familiarity and group relationships can influence who notices whom. But a correlation between social closeness and yawning does not turn the reflex into a clean meter of compassion.

For the person across the table, the etiquette story is immediate: “You are bored with me.” That inference may be wrong. Fatigue, anticipation, stress, a state transition or the sight of someone else yawning may be enough. Behaviour can be socially consequential without being socially intentional.

Provine’s lasting lesson was restraint. Natural behaviour deserves rigorous study precisely because everyone already carries a confident explanation for it.

## CONTEXT

Science often progresses by making a question smaller. “Why do we yawn?” may bundle several questions: What initiates the motor pattern? What immediate physiological change follows? Why did the pattern persist through evolution? Why is it contagious in some species and individuals? What does context change?

An answer at one level does not automatically answer the others. A thermal effect could help explain immediate physiology without explaining social contagion. A social-coordination benefit could coexist with an older bodily mechanism. A laboratory video can establish susceptibility without reconstructing the evolutionary origin of the response.

This layered view also explains why the subject attracts overclaiming. A visible, familiar reflex invites a single story, while the research produces conditional findings. “Yawning equals empathy” is memorable. “Attention, age, relationship and physiology interact, and much variance remains unexplained” is closer to the evidence.

## DEEPER

Provine’s career showed how to investigate behaviours people rarely report accurately. Rather than relying only on memory, he observed timing, manipulated cues and compared predictions. The approach can be borrowed in daily life without pretending to conduct a laboratory experiment.

If a yawn seems meaningful, notice what is actually observable: time of day, sleepiness, recent waking, a long period of stillness, another yawn in the room. Separate that from the story added afterward. The story may be reasonable, but it is not the same as the behaviour.

There is also a medical boundary. An ordinary yawn is not a diagnosis. A marked new change, repeated yawning alongside other concerning symptoms or severe daytime sleepiness deserves context-specific advice from a qualified professional. The reflex alone cannot identify a disorder.

The practical takeaway is humane: do not use a contagious yawn as a character test. It may tell you that a nervous system responded to a cue. It cannot reliably tell you whether the person is attentive, kind, rude or emotionally perceptive.

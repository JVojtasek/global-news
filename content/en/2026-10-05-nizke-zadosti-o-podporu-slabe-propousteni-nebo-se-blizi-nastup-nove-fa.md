Low jobless claims show weak layoffs, not necessarily a strong labour market. The key is to watch whether new hiring, wages and spending follow.

The US labour market right now resembles a restaurant where nobody quits, yet the waiter at the door keeps saying: "Reservation? Not today." According to the US Department of Labor, new claims for unemployment benefits fell to 197,000 in the week ending 26 September, the lowest since 1969. The catch: low layoffs don't yet mean strong hiring.

This isn't a classic "hot" labour market. It's more a Velcro labour market: people are stuck to their jobs, companies aren't letting them go, but they aren't sticking many new people on either.

The most important distinction is between two questions that often get thrown into the same bag: "Are companies laying people off?" and "Are companies expanding their teams?" Jobless claims mainly answer the first. When they're extremely low, they tell you companies aren't yet cutting costs through mass firings. But they say less about the appetite to take risks, open new positions and plan for growth.

The historical parallel has a bite to it: 1969 was also a period of a very tight labour market, but that didn't mean the economy had an endless party ahead of it. Low claims can be a late-cycle phenomenon: companies hold on to people because they're hard to find again, while managers at the same time put the brakes on new hiring because of uncertainty, rates or weaker demand. That's the counter-intuitive point: extremely low layoffs can sometimes signal frozen movement rather than a boom.

For the market, that's awkwardly ambiguous. Bonds may read low claims as a sign of a resilient economy, and so less pressure for quick rate cuts. Stocks, though, may focus on the second half of the sentence: without new jobs, wage growth, consumer confidence and the willingness to buy more expensive things weaken over time.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The plus side: companies tied to everyday spending may benefit from people not losing their pay cheques en masse for now. Retail chains such as Walmart (WMT) or Costco (COST) are usually more resilient in this kind of environment, because they sell everyday goods. More defensive food companies, such as Seneca Foods (SENEA), may be watched precisely because demand for basic food tends to be less sensitive to the hiring cycle than cars or kitchen renovations.

It's mixed for consumer cyclicals. Home Depot (HD) and Lowe's (LOW) aren't automatic losers just because companies aren't hiring; their sales turn more on mortgage rates, house prices, credit availability and households' willingness to take on big projects. Weaker hiring can, however, sour household psychology: someone who sees fewer job opportunities around them will think twice about a big purchase.

The minus side: staffing and recruitment firms such as Robert Half (RHI), ManpowerGroup (MAN) or Korn Ferry (KFY). They live off movement in the labour market. When nobody is being laid off but nobody is being hired either, it's like a dance floor where the music is playing but everyone is standing by the wall.

For bonds, the effect cuts "both ways". Low claims ease fears of a sharp recession, which can keep yields higher. Weak hiring, though, eventually tempers wage pressure and growth, which can push yields lower. That's exactly why this is a data event to watch, not a simple verdict.

With reports like this, beware the single-number trap. A checklist for a better reader of the labour market: 1) jobless claims = layoffs, 2) hiring = companies' appetite to grow, 3) continuing claims = how hard it is to find a new job, 4) wages = pressure on inflation, 5) spending = whether pay cheques are flowing into company revenue. Only the combination shows whether this is healthy strength or just a frozen operation.

"Low jobless claims" means few people are newly losing their jobs. Picture a company as a bus. The good news is that almost nobody falls off while it's moving. The less good news: the driver hardly stops at any stops, so new passengers can't get on. For the market, it means steadier household incomes now, but less new growth ahead. For stocks, it's mixed: defensives are calmer, recruiters and cyclicals more sensitive. For an ordinary wallet: your job may be safer, but looking for a new one may hurt more.

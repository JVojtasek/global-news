## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Cold mornings can lower tyre pressure enough to switch on the dashboard warning, which may disappear as the tyres warm during driving.

**What it means.** The change can be ordinary gas physics, but the disappearing lamp does not prove the tyres were correctly inflated or that there is no leak.

**Risks and impact.** Underinflation can worsen handling, heat build-up, tyre wear and energy use; a damaged tyre or rapid unequal loss needs attention.

**What can be done.** Inspect the tyres, find the vehicle placard, measure all tyres cold with a gauge and adjust them to the specified pressure.

**What to watch.** Recheck after several cold mornings; one tyre falling faster than the others points away from weather alone.

## FACTS

You start the car on the first properly cold morning of the season. A yellow symbol shaped like a flattened horseshoe appears. Ten minutes later it vanishes. The easy conclusion is that the car changed its mind.

It did not. The direct answer is that colder air usually lowers the pressure inside a tyre. Driving then flexes and warms the tyre, raising the measured pressure enough to move it back above the warning threshold. NHTSA explicitly notes that a tyre-pressure monitoring system, or TPMS, may illuminate briefly on a cold morning and then switch off as tyres or outside air warm.

That is an explanation, not an all-clear. NHTSA says an on-and-off warning should still prompt an inspection and pressure check. A tyre that begins the morning near the threshold is still marginally low, even if heat later hides the symptom.

The correct reference is normally the vehicle manufacturer's cold inflation pressure on the driver-door placard, door edge or owner's manual. It is not the maximum pressure moulded into the tyre sidewall. That sidewall number describes a tyre limit under specified conditions, not the everyday setting chosen for the weight and handling of your particular car.

“Cold” has a practical meaning. NHTSA defines it as a vehicle that has not been driven for at least three hours. Transport Canada similarly advises checking before driving, when the tyres are at ambient temperature. A reading after a motorway trip answers a different question because the tyres have warmed.

## EVIDENCE

The mechanism follows a familiar rule: when the amount of gas and the tyre's volume are broadly stable, pressure changes with absolute temperature. Real tyres flex and their volume is not perfectly fixed, so workshop rules such as “one psi for every ten degrees Fahrenheit” are approximations, not diagnostic laws.

That distinction matters. If all four tyres lose a similar small amount when the weather turns colder, temperature is a plausible common cause. If one tyre drops much more, repeatedly needs air or falls rapidly, a valve, bead, wheel or tread leak becomes more likely. Physics can explain a pattern; it cannot inspect a nail.

Pressure also declines gradually even without obvious damage because air molecules move through tyre materials over time. Seasonal cooling can therefore reveal maintenance already overdue. The cold did not necessarily create the whole shortage; it may simply have pushed a slowly declining tyre across the warning threshold.

TPMS adds another layer. Direct systems use pressure sensors associated with the wheels. Indirect systems infer a problem from wheel-speed and other vehicle data. Both are warning systems rather than precision workshop gauges. The NHTSA compliance procedure for the US standard tests whether systems provide the required warning under defined conditions; it does not turn the dashboard lamp into a live guarantee that every tyre is at its ideal placard value.

NHTSA recommends a manual monthly check, including the spare where applicable. FuelEconomy.gov also links proper inflation with lower rolling resistance and better fuel economy. For electric cars, the same resistance affects range. The safety principle is unchanged by the powertrain.

## PERSPECTIVES

One response is to treat every warning as evidence of a puncture. That can produce unnecessary alarm, especially when a simultaneous weather change affects every tyre.

The opposite response is more tempting: if the lamp goes out, ignore it. That confuses crossing a software threshold with correcting the underlying pressure. The tyre may have warmed from, say, slightly below the threshold to slightly above it while remaining below the placard recommendation when cold.

The sensible middle is pattern recognition backed by measurement. Look first for visible damage, a flattened tyre, vibration or handling change. Then measure rather than guess. Compare the readings with one another and with the placard. Finally, repeat the check under similar cold conditions.

There is also a convenience trap. Service-station gauges may be damaged, and compact inflators vary in accuracy. A modest handheld gauge kept in the car makes comparison easier. Consistency is often more useful than chasing false decimal precision.

## CONTEXT

Recommended pressures can differ between front and rear axles and may change for heavy loads. Replacement tyres do not automatically change the vehicle placard figure, but unusual tyre sizes or specialist applications require qualified advice. The owner's manual is the bridge between a generic article and the actual vehicle.

Do not bleed air from a warm tyre merely because its reading exceeds the cold placard number. Warming normally raises pressure. If you adjust a warm tyre, recheck it cold later.

A steadily illuminated symbol generally indicates significant underinflation. A symbol that flashes for roughly a minute and then stays on may indicate a TPMS malfunction rather than a simple low reading; NHTSA advises system inspection. Dashboard designs differ, so consult the manual.

Visible cuts, bulges, exposed material, a rapidly deflating tyre or a vehicle that pulls or vibrates deserves prompt professional inspection. Adding air is not a repair for structural damage. If a tyre is visibly flat or unsafe, do not keep driving simply to warm it until the lamp disappears.

## DEEPER

A five-minute cold check is enough to turn a vague warning into useful information.

First, park safely and let the car stand for at least three hours. Walk around it. Look for an obviously low tyre, embedded object, cracking, bulge or unusual wear.

Second, open the driver's door and photograph the tyre-information placard. Note front and rear values and whether they change with load. Use the owner's manual if the label is missing or unclear.

Third, remove each valve cap, press a reliable gauge squarely onto the valve and record the reading. Include the spare if it is designed for routine pressure maintenance.

Fourth, add air to the placard value. If a tyre is high while genuinely cold, release small amounts and remeasure. Replace the valve caps.

Fifth, repeat the readings after a few days of similar temperatures. Four tyres moving together suggests weather and normal loss. One tyre separating from the group deserves inspection.

The warning light is doing a useful but limited job. It tells you that a threshold has been crossed; it cannot tell you why. The gauge supplies the number. The placard supplies the target. A repeat check supplies the story.

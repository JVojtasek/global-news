With AI infrastructure, do not just watch the size of the investment, but above all whether expensive capacity turns into revenue faster than depreciation starts eating it.

The AI bull is not charging down Wall Street today because it has horns — but because someone has to pay a giant bill for electricity, chips and concrete.

The report claims that the technology sector is still well below its estimated fair value, despite massive investment in AI infrastructure. The main argument: hyperscalers and new cloud players keep planning capacity expansion on the scale of gigawatts, driven by names such as OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta (META). This is supposed to extend the AI investment cycle at least until the end of the decade.

The most interesting thing about this report is not the claim that "tech is cheap". That is the stock-market equivalent of a "homemade lemonade" sign — nice, but not much use without the recipe. The real insight lies elsewhere: AI is ceasing to behave like a software boom and starting to behave like an industrial cycle.

In other words: the winners will no longer be only those with the best model. They will be those who can reserve electricity, GPUs, cooling, land, network connections and financing before everyone else. Multi-gigawatt AI infrastructure is not "a bigger server room". It is a small energy policy wearing a hoodie with a cloud logo.

Here is the counterintuitive part: aggressive CapEx, meaning capital expenditure on future capacity, need not automatically be negative for stocks. With an ordinary company, an investor would say: "It is burning cash, get out." With AI infrastructure, the question is subtler: is the company buying a cost, or a ticket into a capacity-constrained market? If there is a shortage of computing power, electricity and HBM memory, capacity can have value in itself. But if it turns out by 2027–2030 that everyone has built too many "AI hotels" and the rooms are not full, depreciation — the accounting wear and tear on expensive infrastructure — will start to hurt.

Publicly trackable material for this framework: capital expenditure and commentary on AI infrastructure in the reports of Meta (META), Microsoft (MSFT), Alphabet (GOOGL) and Amazon (AMZN); the announcement of the Stargate project by OpenAI/SoftBank/Oracle; and supplier commentary from chip and memory makers, especially NVIDIA (NVDA), Broadcom (AVGO) and Micron (MU).

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The plus side: makers of AI accelerators such as NVIDIA (NVDA) and AMD (AMD), networking and specialised chips such as Broadcom (AVGO), makers of advanced HBM-type memory such as Micron (MU) and SK hynix, and the manufacturing chain around TSMC (TSM) and ASML (ASML). If gigawatts of AI capacity are being built, someone has to supply both the "brains" and the "blood vessels".

The second wave of winners may be more boring, but very important: energy and data-centre infrastructure. Examples: Vertiv (VRT) in cooling and power, Eaton (ETN) and Schneider Electric (SU.PA) in electrical systems, GE Vernova (GEV) in grid and power equipment. The AI boom, after all, has a physical body.

The hyperscalers have a mixed impact: Microsoft (MSFT), Alphabet (GOOGL), Amazon (AMZN) and Meta (META). On one hand, they are building a moat around the business. On the other, their depreciation, energy costs and pressure for returns are rising. With Meta it is especially interesting: AI can help advertising performance, but at the same time it raises capital intensity.

Under pressure may be companies that sell an "AI story" but lack access to capacity, distribution or unique data. Part of software may also suffer if customers shift budgets from overpriced SaaS tools to AI infrastructure.

A better trader would not just watch the "undervalued" headline with news like this. Checklist: 1) is CapEx growing faster than AI revenue? 2) are margins deteriorating because of depreciation? 3) do suppliers confirm demand in orders, not just in presentations? 4) is the constraint the chip, the electricity, or the customer? 5) for memory such as Micron (MU): is HBM demand holding, or is the classic memory cycle of oversupply returning?

A practical threshold for reporting: if AI investment grows faster than monetisation for several quarters in a row and management starts talking more about "long-term potential" than about capacity utilisation, that is a warning sign. If, on the other hand, suppliers confirm full capacity, long delivery times and stable prices, the story has a firmer foundation.

CapEx is like a café buying a giant croissant oven. In the short term its wallet hurts, because the oven is expensive. But if the queue stretches round the corner, the oven makes money. If the queue disappears, an expensive lump of iron remains and the owner is paying off a dream. The same applies to AI: the market is now watching whether giant data centres become profit factories, or luxury warehouses of hot air.

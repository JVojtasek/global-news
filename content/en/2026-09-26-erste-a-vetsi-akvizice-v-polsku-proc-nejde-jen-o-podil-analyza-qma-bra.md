With bank acquisitions it is not enough to watch the size of the stake. The key is the price, the capital impact and the degree of control behind it.

When a bank buys another piece of another bank, it is often not just buying profit. It is buying a place at the helm, a better view from the deck and the right to tell the crew which way the ship sails.

Erste Group Bank has announced that it plans a public offer to acquire an additional stake of up to 26% in Erste Bank Polska. The logic is simple: to strengthen its exposure to Poland, which Erste describes as a key market.

At first glance this is dull banking arithmetic: plus 26% of the stake, more Poland in the consolidated results, done. But with banks, not every percentage point is equal. The first few points of a stake are like a season ticket to the stadium: you can watch the match. A larger stake is like sitting in a box with your own door to the dressing room. You are still not the club's only owner, but you have a completely different influence on tactics.

The less obvious point is this: the market will not only weigh the offer price, but also whether Erste is buying "growth" or "control over growth". Poland is attractive to Central European banks because it is a large domestic banking market with its own monetary cycle, local clients and deposit base. For a bank, deposits are like flour for a bakery: without it you can have the finest brand in town, but you will not bake bread. Whoever has cheap and stable client deposits has the raw material for lending.

The counterintuitive truth: buying a minority stake can sometimes be less about immediate profit and more about optionality, meaning the future ability to make strategic decisions, coordinate more closely, share products and manage capital better. That sounds abstract, but it is similar to buying the neighbouring flat in your building: the rent from it is nice, but the real value grows as you gradually own more units and start having a say in the owners' association.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It may help above all Erste Group Bank (EBS.VI), because according to the report it is the direct buyer, and a larger stake would increase its economic ties to the Polish market. But the other side of the coin also applies: if the offer is expensive or capital-intensive, investors will watch its effect on capital adequacy, that is, the bank's safety cushion.

Polish banking peers such as PKO Bank Polski (PKO.WA), Bank Pekao (PEO.WA) or Santander Bank Polska (SPL.WA) may also see a positive response. Not because the transaction would automatically lift their profits, but because offers like this hold up a new mirror to valuations: how much a strategic player is willing to pay for Polish banking exposure.

Conversely, Erste's investors may come under pressure if they start to see the offer as too big a mouthful. For a bank, an acquisition is not like buying a new office chair. It is more like adopting another organism, with its own regulation, credit risk, currency exposure and local politics.

With bank offers of this kind it pays to watch five things: the offer price against the price before the announcement, the final size of the stake after the transaction, how it is financed, management's comments on capital, and whether the target market really fits the long-term strategy. "We are buying a bigger stake" on its own is just a headline; what matters is the maths behind it.

A public offer is like someone going round the neighbours in a block of flats and saying: "Who will sell me their flat at this price?" The effect? If the price is generous, owners may take notice. If the buyer gets more flats, it has more influence over the whole building. On the market, this means the target bank's shares may react to the offer price, and the buying bank's shares to the question of whether it is paying too much for the key to the building.

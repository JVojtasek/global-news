## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** A Library of Things applies a familiar civic idea to unfamiliar stock. Instead of only books, members borrow drills, sewing machines, pressure washers, tents, kitchen equipment and other objects that are useful occasionally but expensive or awkward to store.

**What it means.** The service changes the unit of value from owning an object to completing a task. One maintained carpet cleaner can serve many households, while each borrower avoids deciding whether a one-day need justifies a permanent purchase.

**Risks and impact.** Sharing does not make products weightless or free. Inventory, insurance, cleaning, repairs, staff time and collection journeys matter. The environmental gain also depends on whether a borrow genuinely replaces a purchase.

**What can be done.** Households can compare cost per likely use, not sticker prices alone. Communities can begin with a small catalogue shaped by local demand and publish clear rules for safety, maintenance and late returns.

**What to watch.** The strongest services will measure repeat use, purchase avoidance, item downtime and access—not just membership totals or the number of objects on a shelf.

## FACTS

Libraries of Things are organized collections of physical objects available for temporary use. Shareable's current toolkit lists tools, kitchen equipment, camping gear, event supplies and emergency equipment, and says formal services operate in many countries. Some sit inside public libraries; others use community hubs, lockers or independent nonprofit premises.

The London-based Library of Things began with a short experiment in West Norwood library in 2014. Its account says a 2016 test used two shipping containers and 400 objects with 1,000 participants to learn which products people wanted to borrow. The service later developed software, self-service lockers and partnerships with libraries and councils.

Its published impact page reports more than 75,000 rentals and 39,000 members through September 2025. Those are organization-reported figures, not an independent census of the wider movement. ReLondon documented a harder operational lesson: when public buildings closed during the pandemic, rentals and income fell, exposing the dependence of a sharing service on reliable physical access.

The durable fact is modest but useful. A household can obtain temporary use of an object without becoming its owner. Everything beyond that—money saved, waste avoided and community benefit—depends on what would otherwise have happened.

## EVIDENCE

Library of Things publishes its impact methodology, including assumptions that deserve to remain visible. Its 2024 update assumes that half of borrows prevent a purchase, then combines that estimate with average purchase prices and product weights. The organization explicitly notes limitations: prices vary, some people would buy second-hand, product types differ, and lower demand does not instantly reduce manufacturing.

That transparency is more informative than a giant avoided-waste number by itself. It separates observed activity—an item was borrowed—from a counterfactual claim—the borrower did not buy something because of it. The first can be counted directly. The second usually comes from surveys and modelling.

ReLondon's 2022 case study also shows that the model has real fixed costs. A £10,000 emergency grant helped the London enterprise retain capacity and reopen; the case records new locations and jobs after the disruption. That is evidence of a functioning service, not proof that every Library of Things will become self-financing.

The sound conclusion is conditional. Shared inventory can reduce household spending and material demand when items are durable, genuinely substitute for purchases, circulate often and are maintained well. Claims should shrink when those conditions are not measured.

## PERSPECTIVES

For a borrower, the decision is practical. A costly item used once a year consumes money and storage space. Borrowing can be better even with a fee. But a last-minute job, a distant collection point or an unavailable item can erase the advantage. Access has a time cost.

For a library or community group, the romantic image of neighbours sharing objects quickly becomes an operations problem. Which items are safe to lend? Who checks a returned pressure washer? How are missing parts recorded? What happens when ten people want the same carpet cleaner on one weekend? Shareable's long toolkit devotes substantial attention to labelling, inventory, repairs, storage, volunteers and disposal because these details determine whether trust survives.

For manufacturers and retailers, borrowing is not simply lost demand. A shared service may favor robust, repairable products and expose users to equipment they later choose to buy. It can also challenge a model built on selling many lightly used objects.

For environmental advocates, the important distinction is circulation versus addition. A well-used shared item may displace purchases. A poorly chosen catalogue can merely add stock, journeys and administrative work. Good news here is not that ownership has become bad. It is that communities have built another credible route to use.

## CONTEXT

The ordinary consumer market bundles several things together: access, ownership, storage, maintenance, depreciation and disposal. Buying feels simple because one payment transfers the whole bundle. The hidden obligations arrive later, in cupboards, garages, repair decisions and waste streams.

A Library of Things unbundles that package. The member pays or registers for access; the operator retains the object and the maintenance problem. That arrangement is especially plausible for products with high purchase prices, low frequency of use and reasonably predictable inspection needs. It is less attractive for personal, consumable, heavily customized or constantly used goods.

The analogy with books is helpful but incomplete. A missing page is different from a damaged power tool. Physical safety, liability and cleaning can make a Things collection more expensive to operate than shelves of print. Community enthusiasm cannot replace competent checks.

The service also introduces a coordination bargain. Individual ownership guarantees availability but duplicates capacity. Shared ownership uses fewer objects more intensively but requires reservations, returns and patience. Neither arrangement wins everywhere. The question is whether a community can make sharing easy enough for the right category of need.

### Practical impact

Before buying, write down four numbers: likely uses in the next two years, total purchase cost, realistic borrowing cost including travel, and storage space required. Then check the local catalogue and its deposit, cancellation and late-return rules. If availability is uncertain for a deadline, keep a backup plan. If no service exists, a demand survey for five or ten high-cost, low-use items is a better starting point than a room full of donations.

## DEEPER

The quiet innovation is institutional, not technological. These services make restraint convenient. They do not ask a household to abandon a task or rely on a neighbour's goodwill; they create a catalogue, a booking system and an accountable steward between need and object.

That matters because many sustainable choices fail when they require heroics. A circular economy becomes ordinary only when the alternative to buying is dependable, legible and close enough to use. The item on the shelf is therefore only half the design. The other half is the promise that it will work, arrive with all its parts and be there when the borrower needs it.

### Reader outcome

**What changed.** A growing family of community services has extended the lending model from information to physical tools and equipment.

**Why it matters.** They offer a practical way to lower the cost and material footprint of infrequent needs, while revealing the real work required to share safely.

**What to watch.** Look for utilization, item condition, purchase avoidance, equitable access and stable operating funds—not catalogue size alone.

**What would change our minds.** Evidence that borrowing mostly adds trips without replacing purchases, or that maintenance failures make useful items unreliable, would weaken the case. Consistent circulation and independently checked substitution data would strengthen it.

## REFLECT

The next time an uncommon task creates an urgent desire to buy, pause before comparing products. Ask whether you need the object or only its function for an afternoon. That distinction does not settle the decision, but it makes cost, convenience and ownership visible as separate choices.

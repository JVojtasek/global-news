With strong jobs data, the key is to distinguish whether the market is repricing only the Fed's next step or a whole longer era of higher rates — the second variant tends to be more painful for long bonds and distant profits.

When the labour market looks like a packed pub on a Friday night, the central bank has a hard time pretending it cannot hear the noise. According to the supplied report, important labour market data are due this week, mainly payrolls, that is, the number of new non-farm jobs, and JOLTS, the statistics on job openings and people quitting their jobs. The report argues that with inflation measured by the personal consumption expenditures index, PCE, at 3.7 % for July and a tight labour market, the scenario of a Fed rate hike in September may gain strength.

It is not just about whether people have jobs. It is about whether the Fed has the luxury of waiting. For a central bank, a strong labour market works a bit like an airbag: while the economy keeps going, the Fed can afford to brake longer and harder, because it is less afraid of an immediate crash in the form of a recession.

The counterintuitive point: good news from the economy can be bad news for stocks. Not because employment in itself harms companies. What harms them is a higher discount rate, that is, the conversion of future profits into today's value. The higher the rates, the less the market pays for profits that arrive only several years from now. A growth stock is like a promise of dinner in five years; if your rent goes up in the meantime, you are willing to pay less for that promise.

More important, and less visible, is the battle over the length of the wait. Long bonds do not suffer only because the Fed may raise rates next month. They suffer mainly when the market starts rewriting the whole story: rates not only higher but also higher for longer, and perhaps with less willingness on the Fed's part to rescue the economy quickly at the first wobble. That is the difference between one more expensive instalment and a new mortgage price list for years. That is why the reaction of thirty-year bonds can be harsher than the labour market report itself suggests.

With payrolls and JOLTS, the combination matters. Payrolls say how many people were actually added to payslips. JOLTS shows how many doors in the economy are still open. When there are many vacancies and few people lose their jobs, wage pressure can stay stubborn. And the Fed then deals not only with today's inflation but with the risk that more expensive labour spills over into the prices of services.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

Long-term bonds tend to take the negative impact: when yields rise, the price of existing bonds usually falls. Also sensitive are the shares of companies with a large part of their expected profits in the future, for example growth software such as Snowflake (SNOW) or Datadog (DDOG), or broader technology valuations at companies such as Microsoft (MSFT) or NVIDIA (NVDA). This is not a verdict on the quality of the companies but on the sensitivity of valuations to higher rates.

Financial services face a mixed impact. Large banks such as JPMorgan Chase (JPM) or Bank of America (BAC) can benefit from higher interest on loans, but at the same time face more expensive deposits and the repricing of bond portfolios. For regional banks, for example C&F Financial (CFFI), the key is to distinguish direct exposure to the interest margin from the risk of losses on securities and the sensitivity of client deposits.

Real estate investment trusts, that is REITs, for example Realty Income (O), and homebuilders such as Lennar (LEN) or D.R. Horton (DHI) may also come under pressure, because higher rates make financing and mortgages more expensive. By contrast, companies with immediate cash flow, low debt and pricing power may hold up relatively better.

With data like this, do not look only at the payrolls number itself. A better framework has three steps: 1) payrolls show the number of new jobs, 2) JOLTS hints at whether the labour market is really loosening, 3) the reaction of the two-year US Treasury yield shows whether the market is rewriting its Fed outlook. If after strong data it is mainly the two-year yield that rises and stocks weaken, the market is reading the news as a risk of higher rates, not as joy over growth.

An even more precise filter: watch the difference between the short and long ends of the yield curve. The two-year yield is more sensitive to expected Fed moves; the ten-year and thirty-year yields reveal more about whether the market is changing its view of a longer era of more expensive money. If it is mainly long yields that rise, it is not just about one Fed meeting but about repricing the whole future path of rates — and that is more painful for long bonds and for stocks with a long wait for profits.

Beware of a trap: a single data event rarely decides things on its own. The Fed watches a chain of data — inflation, wages, employment and financial conditions. A strong report is therefore not an automatic verdict, but it can change the probabilities of scenarios: higher rates for longer, higher yields, less willingness to pay for distant profits.

The discount rate is the market's 'discount for waiting'. Imagine someone promises you a thousand crowns today, or a thousand crowns in five years. When interest rates are low, waiting does not hurt that much. When interest rates are high, you say to yourself: in five years? You'll have to give me a better price for that. In the market this means that stocks based on future profits can get cheaper even while the company keeps growing; it is not necessarily a punishment for bad business but a repricing of a long wait. For the ordinary person, it can mean a more expensive mortgage, more attractive interest on cash and greater swings in pension or investment portfolios.

## FACTS

Imagine a couple nine years into a marriage who notice, almost with relief, that they've stopped arguing. No more slammed doors, no more raised voices over the dishwasher. They tell themselves it means they've finally figured each other out. It's a generalized scene, not a real couple — but therapists who work with couples describe some version of it constantly: the fight that never happens again is often not the sign of peace. It's the sign that someone has quietly stopped bringing complaints to the table at all.

Relationships rarely fail because of one dramatic event. They fail because a surface-level irritation — money, chores, time, tone of voice — gets treated as the whole problem, when it's usually standing in front of something else. A widely used problem-solving method called the "five whys" was built for exactly this trap: asking "why" repeatedly, each time aiming the question at the answer just given, until a shallow symptom gives way to something closer to a root cause. It was designed for factory defects, not marriages. But the logic travels well, because most human conflict has the same shape: a visible complaint sitting on top of layers nobody has bothered to open.

The other thing worth knowing up front: love and hate are not opposites that cancel each other out inside a relationship. Psychologists describe a specific pattern — the "love–hate relationship" — where both emotions run at full strength at the same time, especially when the stakes are high. That combination, not its absence, is often what people are actually fighting about.

## CONTEXT

The five whys technique was developed at Toyota, credited to Sakichi Toyoda and popularized by the engineer Taiichi Ohno, who called it "the basis of Toyota's scientific approach" for tracing a defect back through its causes instead of stopping at the first plausible explanation. In the classic factory example, cross-threaded bolts on an engine block don't trace back to "a bad batch of bolts" — they trace back, five questions later, to a broken foot on a parts shelf that nobody had gotten around to fixing.

It's a method built for machines, and it has real critics. Teruyuki Minoura, a former Toyota executive, argued it's too basic to find root causes reliably. The medical researcher Alan J. Card has gone further, arguing the technique should be abandoned altogether, because the fifth "why" is an arbitrary stopping point that may or may not be the actual root cause, and because different people asking the same five questions routinely land on different answers. Card's core objection is worth sitting with even outside a factory: the method can't take you anywhere past what you already suspect. If you don't already know the real cause, five questions won't manufacture it for you.

That's exactly the trap in relationships. A couple who fight about money every month can ask "why" five times and land somewhere true — not enough time together, not enough saved reserve, a parent who went bankrupt when one of them was a kid — but only if at least one of them is willing to keep answering honestly after the second or third round, when the easy answers run out. Most people stop at round one. "We fight about money" becomes the whole diagnosis, and the actual pattern underneath — for instance, that money is the only subject where either partner feels safe expressing anger about anything — never gets named.

Here's the belief that doesn't survive contact with the research: that the opposite of love is indifference, and that a relationship in trouble is one where the warmth has drained out. Often the opposite is true. Psychologists studying love–hate relationships describe emotional ambivalence — intense love and intense hate running simultaneously — as something that tends to show up precisely where the emotional stakes are highest: in marriages, in the bond between parent and adult child, sometimes in estrangements that never quite become total indifference. One line of research out of Yale has connected persistent love–hate dynamics to low self-esteem. The people in these situations aren't underinvested. They're overloaded, feeling two contradictory things at full volume and unable to hold both without one of them curdling.

Developmental psychologists call the skill that's missing here "tolerating ambivalence" — the capacity to stay angry at someone you love without needing to stop loving them in order to justify the anger. When that capacity breaks down, people default to a defense called splitting: a person gets filed as either all-good or all-bad, because holding the mixed, accurate picture feels unbearable. It's a pattern researchers associate most closely with certain personality difficulties, and it isn't a diagnosis to hand a partner after one bad week. But the underlying difficulty it describes — struggling to hold "good" and "bad" together in the same person — is not exclusive to clinical cases; it's just usually milder and better hidden the rest of the time.

None of this is a tidy explanation for why any specific relationship ends. Relationship researchers, including the psychologist John Gottman, whose 1994 book carries the blunt title *Why Marriages Succeed and Fail*, have spent careers trying to separate the patterns that predict a breakup from the ones that don't — and even that body of work doesn't reduce to a single cause. What's consistent across it is the general shape: the presenting complaint is rarely the load-bearing one.

## PEOPLE

Picture two generalized, composite situations, built from patterns therapists describe rather than any single real case.

In the first, a couple in their thirties keep having what looks, from the outside, like the same argument about being late. One is always ten minutes behind; the other is always furious about it. Five rounds of "why" later, in an honest conversation, it turns out the lateness isn't the injury — the injury is that punctuality is the one place where "you don't respect my time" feels sayable, because saying "I don't feel important to you" out loud is much harder.

In the second, a woman in her sixties describes her relationship with her father as one she can't leave and can't stand — visiting him wrecks her for days, and skipping a visit wrecks her worse. That's the love–hate pattern in its plainer, more common form: not a diagnosis, just two real emotions that never resolved into one, running side by side for decades. Neither situation is unusual. Both are the kind of thing a five-minute conversation about "what we're really fighting about" almost never gets to, because it requires someone to keep asking past the first comfortable answer.

## DEEPER

The question sitting under "why do relationships fail" isn't really "what's the mistake." It's "what does it take to keep loving someone you're currently angry at, without either pretending the anger away or letting it cancel out the love." That's a harder, older question than any modern research field invented.

One of the oldest texts on the subject frames it not as psychology but as observation. Ecclesiastes 4:9–12 puts it plainly: "Two are better than one, because they have a good return for their labor: if either of them falls down, one can help the other up... Though one may be overpowered, two can defend themselves. A cord of three strands is not quickly broken." It isn't a technique and it isn't a promise that the fighting stops. It's a description of what a bond made of more than one strand can absorb that a single strand can't — read next to the five whys and the love-hate research, it's simply another way of saying the same thing three very different eras have separately noticed: what breaks a relationship usually isn't the presence of conflict. It's what's left holding two people together once the easy answer has been used up.

## REFLECT

The next time the same argument comes up for the third time in a month, what happens if you ask "why" about your own answer, not your partner's?

Is there a relationship in your life where you've quietly filed someone as "all bad" after one difficult stretch — and what would the more accurate, mixed picture of them actually look like?

One thing to try today: pick the recurring complaint you're most tired of repeating, and instead of repeating it, ask yourself what it would cost to say the thing underneath it out loud.

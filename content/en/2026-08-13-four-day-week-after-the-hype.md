## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** A 2025 peer-reviewed study followed 2,896 employees in 141 organisations across six countries after a work redesign and six-month four-day-week trial without reduced pay.

**What it means.** Employees reported lower burnout and better job satisfaction, mental health and physical health than workers in 12 comparison companies. The intervention was reduced time plus redesigned work, not a calendar trick alone.

**Risks and impact.** Voluntary organisations and self-reported outcomes limit how widely the result can be generalised. Compressing unchanged workloads into longer days may create a different, less helpful model.

**What can be done.** A team can run a bounded pilot with service, workload, overtime, absence and wellbeing measures, while protecting pay and agreeing in advance what would stop or modify the trial.

**What to watch.** The deciding evidence is not whether staff like Friday off, but whether service quality, hidden overtime and workload remain acceptable after novelty fades.

## FACTS

The Nature Human Behaviour study published in July 2025 analysed pre- and post-trial data from 2,896 employees at 141 organisations in Australia, Canada, Ireland, New Zealand, the United Kingdom and the United States. Twelve other companies, covering 285 employees, served as a comparison group.

Participating organisations first reorganised work to improve efficiency and collaboration. They then tested a four-day week for six months without reducing pay. The study found improvements in burnout, job satisfaction, mental health and physical health that were not observed in the comparison companies.

Reduced sleep problems, fatigue and improved self-reported work ability helped explain the wellbeing changes. Larger individual reductions in working time were associated with larger wellbeing gains. The study did not establish one identical schedule for every workplace.

Public-sector evidence is narrower but useful. Scotland tested a 32-hour week without loss of pay in two public bodies from October 2023 to November 2025. The Scottish Government reports that service delivery and key performance targets were maintained while staff wellbeing improved.

Earlier Icelandic public-sector trials involved more than 2,500 workers between 2015 and 2019, typically reducing the week to 35 or 36 hours without lower pay. Those trials helped normalise shorter negotiated hours, but they were shaped by Iceland's labour institutions and should not be treated as a universal plug-in programme.

## EVIDENCE

The best-supported conclusion is about wellbeing during a structured, income-preserving reduction in work time. The international study used a comparison group and observed a consistent pattern across several wellbeing outcomes. The Scottish pilot adds operational evidence from public services, albeit only two organisations.

The mechanism matters. Participating organisations changed work before they changed the calendar. Reducing unnecessary meetings, clarifying priorities, improving handoffs and protecting focused time can remove low-value activity. If weekly hours fall as a result, people gain recovery time instead of merely doing the same work faster.

The evidence does not prove that every employer will preserve productivity. The Nature paper focuses on employee wellbeing and self-reported work ability, not one audited productivity measure shared by 141 different organisations. Participants opted into a trial associated with four-day-week advocates, so they may have had unusually supportive leaders or work suited to redesign.

We also know less about long-term intensity, promotion, customer access and effects on people whose work is tied to physical presence. A six-month result is important but does not settle what happens after several budget cycles.

## PERSPECTIVES

The campaign frame describes a “100:80:100” exchange: full pay, 80% of the time and full output. It correctly makes pay protection explicit and pushes organisations to redesign work. Its slogan can make 100% output sound easier to measure than it is, especially in care, teaching, public service or creative work.

The managerial frame sees the shorter week as an efficiency challenge. This can eliminate waste and give teams agency. It can also become intensification if management keeps every target, removes recovery inside the workday and treats the extra day as compensation for four exhausting shifts.

The employee frame often begins with time for family, sleep, health or administration. Those benefits are real outcomes, not distractions from productivity. Yet experiences will differ. A fixed weekday off may help some workers and conflict with school, transport or caring schedules for others.

The sceptical frame asks about selection and measurement. That is necessary: willing pilot companies are not the whole economy, and self-report is vulnerable to expectation. The useful sceptic does not dismiss the findings. It asks which roles were excluded, where overtime went, whether service demand was shifted and whether gains survived after the pilot.

## CONTEXT

“Four-day week” can describe at least three arrangements. One cuts weekly hours without cutting pay. Another compresses the same hours into four longer days. A third is ordinary part-time work with lower pay. Research on one cannot automatically advertise the others.

The historical shift is from calendar debate to work design. Iceland's trials showed that public workplaces could negotiate shorter hours while maintaining services in many settings. Later company trials made the redesign process more explicit. The 2025 study then linked actual hour reductions with wellbeing rather than treating the four-day label as the active ingredient.

A credible pilot starts with a baseline. For four to six weeks, record hours actually worked, overtime, absence, unfinished work, customer wait times, error or rework rates and a short anonymous wellbeing measure. Include part-time and shift staff rather than designing only for office employees.

Next, remove work before removing time. Cancel meetings without a decision, shorten routine approvals, define response-time expectations and identify handoffs that cause rework. Choose a schedule that matches demand; the whole organisation does not need the same day off.

Run the pilot long enough to get past enthusiasm and initial confusion. Review measures weekly without using surveillance software as a substitute for outcomes. Protect pay and prevent hidden work on the fifth day. Agree on guardrails: safety, service, excessive workload and equity can trigger a redesign or pause.

Finally, publish the unflattering numbers internally. A good trial can end with “not yet” or a hybrid schedule. The purpose is to learn whether reduced working time creates sustainable capacity, not to win an ideological argument.

## PEOPLE

For an employee, the difference between 32 hours and four ten-hour days is not semantic. One may create recovery; the other may make childcare, disability or a long commute harder. A policy advertised as universal freedom can therefore distribute freedom unevenly.

Managers also face a genuine constraint. Customers do not disappear on Friday, and understaffed services cannot improve by deleting meetings alone. The most valuable trials give frontline workers authority to identify waste and give managers permission to say which demand cannot be met with current staffing.

That conversation can reveal a result more important than the schedule: whether an organisation knows what work creates value and what work survives only because nobody has been allowed to stop it.

## DEEPER

Time-saving technology has repeatedly promised leisure and delivered new expectations. Email replaced letters but multiplied messages. Faster reporting created more reports. Efficiency has no natural finish line; without a boundary, saved minutes become new capacity to fill.

The four-day week is therefore not primarily a story about Friday. It is a test of who receives the benefit of efficiency. Does saved time become another target, a lower cost, better service, or a portion of life returned to the people who made the saving possible?

That question cannot be answered by a productivity chart alone. Work supplies income, identity, relationships and public goods. It can also consume the attention needed to sustain those things outside work. A durable design respects both truths.

The most promising evidence does not say that less effort magically produces the same result. It says organisations can examine how effort is organised, then discover that hours and value are not synonyms. The difficult work is not deleting a day from the calendar. It is deciding, together, what deserves to remain.

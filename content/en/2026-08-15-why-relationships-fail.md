## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** No single event triggered this piece; it applies a manufacturing diagnostic tool, and documented research on ambivalent relationships, to a question nearly everyone eventually asks: why do relationships that started well later fail.

**What it means.** A method Toyota built to trace factory defects back to a root cause — the "Five Whys" — maps onto a pattern psychologists already describe in troubled relationships: partners keep re-fighting the same surface argument because nobody traces it back far enough.

**Risks and impact.** The stakes are ordinary and private: years spent inside a relationship that keeps rupturing and reforming without anyone ever naming why.

**What can be done.** Treat a repeated argument as a symptom to trace, not a verdict to accept. The Five Whys technique offers a simple way to do that, with a documented limitation worth knowing before you rely on it.

**What to watch.** Whether the same argument returns within weeks using different words — that repetition, not its intensity, is the signal worth investigating.

## FACTS

Picture the fight that keeps coming back. Not the same words each time — this month it was the dishes, last month it was a canceled plan, before that it was a tone of voice — but recognizably the same fight, wearing a new outfit. Anyone who has been in a long relationship knows this pattern, and most people never figure out what it is actually about.

There is a diagnostic habit for exactly this problem, though it was not built for couples. It is called the Five Whys, developed at Toyota Motor Corporation by engineer Taiichi Ohno. The method is simple: when something goes wrong, ask "why" and answer it, then ask "why" again about that answer, repeating roughly five times until the chain of causes stops pointing at a symptom and starts pointing at something that can actually be fixed.

Psychologists studying what they call "love–hate relationships" — bonds that swing between intense affection and intense conflict — describe a parallel failure. Couples argue about the visible trigger and stop there, never reaching whatever keeps regenerating it.

## EVIDENCE

What is well documented: the Five Whys technique itself, its Toyota origin, and Ohno's own description of it as "the basis of Toyota's scientific approach." It has since spread into Kaizen, lean manufacturing and Six Sigma as a standard root-cause tool. That much is not in dispute.

What is documented but more limited: the psychological research on love–hate relationships. Wikipedia's summary of the topic states that "research from Yale University suggests love–hate relationships may be the result of poor self-esteem" — but without naming the specific study, its year, or its sample. That claim should be read as a general research finding, not a precise, checkable statistic.

What is a genuine leap, and should be labeled as one: applying a manufacturing defect-tracing method to human relationships is an analogy, not a validated therapeutic technique. Nothing in the sourcing shows the Five Whys has been tested on couples specifically.

What is contested even within its home field: management researchers, including Toyota's own former global purchasing director Teruyuki Minoura, have criticized the Five Whys as too shallow a tool to reliably find true root causes.

## PERSPECTIVES

Three different ways to read the same recurring fight.

The systems-diagnosis view says the visible argument is never the real one. Money, chores and lateness are trigger points, not causes; the method's value is forcing you past the first, easy answer. Its blind spot: critics of the technique have long argued it tends to isolate a single root cause when a problem — mechanical or human — often has several running in parallel. Medical professor Alan J. Card has gone further, arguing the method should be abandoned altogether because where you stop asking "why" is arbitrary and unlikely to land on the true root cause.

The attachment-and-ambivalence view, drawn from the love–hate relationship research, says the fight keeps returning because both partners are unable to hold love and anger for the same person at once. Under strain, one feeling replaces the other entirely rather than sitting alongside it — a pattern researchers call "splitting." Its strength is explaining the whiplash quality of these fights; its blind spot is that clinical language borrowed from personality-disorder research can turn an ordinary bad week into an amateur diagnosis of a partner.

The institutional-endurance view treats long relationships less like a single bond and more like a coalition that keeps renegotiating itself. That reading has real precedent, and it survived — until, in one dramatic case, it did not.

## CONTEXT

The Five Whys works, when it works, by refusing to stop at the first plausible answer. Ohno's classic factory example: bolts are cross-threading on the line. Why? The cutting tool wasn't changed. Why? The replacement bin was empty. Why? Its contents had fallen and rolled under the shelf. Why? A shelf foot had rusted through. Five steps from "bad bolts" to "inspect the shelving" — a fix nobody would have reached by only asking once.

Applied loosely to a relationship, the same discipline resists the easy target. "We keep fighting about money" becomes: why does money feel unsafe to one of us specifically, and why has that never been said out loud, and why did it take a fight to bring it up at all. The technique's own critics matter here too: results are not repeatable — different investigators asking the same five whys about the same problem often land on different causes. Two partners tracing the same recurring fight may each arrive at an equally sincere, equally different explanation — which is itself useful information about the gap between them.

The love–hate research adds a second layer: some ambivalence is ordinary and survivable, and some is not. Wikipedia's summary notes that love–hate dynamics are linked to early childhood emotional ambivalence and, in more severe cases, to narcissistic or borderline personality patterns in which a person struggles to feel warmth and anger toward the same loved one simultaneously. The Japanese cultural term "tsundere" — alternating coldness and affection toward a love interest — shows the same swing recognized well outside clinical psychology, as does the Roman poet Catullus's line, nearly two thousand years old: "I hate and yet love. You may wonder how I manage it. I don't know, but feel it happen, and am in torment."

## PEOPLE

Ireland's Fianna Fáil party is an unlikely case study in rupture-and-repair at scale. Éamon de Valera founded it in 1926 after splitting from Sinn Féin; it took power in 1932 and held it for 61 of the next 79 years — a run built not on the absence of conflict but on repeatedly surviving it.

In 1970, the Arms Crisis nearly split the party when leader Jack Lynch dismissed ministers Charles Haughey and Neil Blaney over accusations they sought to arm the Provisional IRA. The party survived intact; the real split came in 1985, when a faction under Desmond O'Malley broke away to found the Progressive Democrats. From 1989 on, the two parties governed together in coalition — the rupture becoming the basis of a working relationship.

The renegotiating had limits. In 2011, after a financial scandal involving leader Bertie Ahern and the economic collapse under his successor Brian Cowen, the party suffered the worst defeat of a sitting government in the state's history — falling from largest party to third and losing 58 of its 78 seats. The Sunday Times called Cowen's tenure "a dismal failure." Many small ruptures were survived; this one overwhelmed the repair mechanism.

## DEEPER

So which is it — does a relationship that keeps rupturing and re-forming prove something is broken, or is rupture-and-repair simply what durable bonds are made of?

An old text on exactly this question, long before Toyota or Yale, sits in the book of Ecclesiastes. "Two are better than one," it reads, "because they have a good return for their labor: if either of them falls down, one can help the other up. But pity anyone who falls and has no one to help them up. Also, if two lie down together, they will keep warm. But how can one keep warm alone? Though one may be overpowered, two can defend themselves. A cord of three strands is not quickly broken" (Ecclesiastes 4:9–12, NIV).

Read next to a factory diagnostic and a century of Irish coalition politics, the image is not sentimental — it is structural. The passage does not promise a bond free of falling, cold, or attack. Its case for partnership is that those things happen to one person alone and to two people together, and the second version has a way to recover that the first does not. A cord holds not because its strands feel no tension, but because there is more than one strand taking the strain, and a third to catch what the other two cannot.

That is the thread running through all three sources here: the diagnostic method, the psychology of ambivalence and the political case study each describe strain as the normal condition, not the failure. What separates an enduring bond from a failing one is rarely the absence of the recurring fight. It is whether anyone ever traces it past the first, easiest answer.

## REFLECT

The next time an old argument resurfaces, what would the honest fourth or fifth "why" actually be — not the answer that lets you win, but the one you have been avoiding?

Is there a strand missing from a relationship that matters to you — a person, a habit, a boundary — whose absence makes ordinary strain feel like more than it is?

## BRIEFLY

**What happened:** A knife cuts an onion, its cells rupture and a fast sequence of reactions produces the volatile compound that makes eyes water.

**What it means:** The onion does not contain a tank of ready-made “tear gas.” Its intact cellular structure keeps enzymes and sulphur-containing precursors apart until damage brings them together.

**Risks and impact:** The familiar sting is usually brief, but folklore often replaces chemistry with claims about sulphuric acid, sweetness or one guaranteed kitchen trick.

**What can be done:** Reduce how much freshly produced vapour reaches your face: use a stable board and sharp knife, keep your face back, ventilate the workspace and consider eye protection when sensitivity matters.

**What to watch:** Plant scientists have shown that suppressing one enzyme can sharply reduce the tear factor, but doing so also redirects the onion's wider sulphur chemistry and may change flavour.

## FACTS

An intact onion is organised chemistry. Sulphur-containing flavour precursors occupy its cells alongside enzymes, but cellular compartments prevent the full reaction from running. Cutting or crushing the tissue removes those barriers. An enzyme called alliinase acts on precursor molecules and produces sulfenic acids. A second enzyme, lachrymatory-factor synthase, converts a particular intermediate into syn-propanethial-S-oxide, the volatile lachrymatory factor.

Because that product readily enters the air, some reaches the moist surface of the eyes. Sensory nerves register irritation and the lacrimal glands respond with tears that dilute and wash the surface. The reaction is defensive in effect even if the onion did not evolve with cooks in mind: damage to plant tissue rapidly creates pungent compounds.

The crucial correction is that sulphur does not mean sulphuric acid. The tear factor is a small organosulphur compound, and the pathway is more specific than the old story in which alliinase alone supposedly completed the job.

## EVIDENCE

In 2002, Shinsuke Imai and colleagues reported in *Nature* that onion preparations needed a previously unidentified enzyme to make the lachrymatory factor. They named it lachrymatory-factor synthase. The discovery changed the pathway from a presumed spontaneous rearrangement into an enzyme-guided step.

A 2017 structural study then examined the enzyme in atomic detail. The researchers described how its folded protein architecture creates a pocket able to guide the unstable sulphur intermediate toward the tear-producing molecule. Structure matters because it explains why a reaction that seems instantaneous on a chopping board can still depend on a specialised biological catalyst.

The strongest experimental test came from changing the plant. A 2008 *Plant Physiology* paper used RNA interference to suppress the LFS gene in onion plants. Lachrymatory-factor synthase activity fell by as much as 1,544-fold in the transformed plants, and wounded onions made far less tear factor. Other sulphur products rose, showing that the pathway had been diverted rather than simply switched off.

## PERSPECTIVES

For the cook, tears feel like a nuisance attached to a simple ingredient. For the plant biochemist, they reveal a compartmented defence system that assembles reactive products only after damage. For a breeder, the same pathway is a design trade-off: lowering one volatile can change the network of compounds contributing to aroma, taste and storage behaviour.

That trade-off helps explain why “tearless” cannot automatically mean “the same onion without the crying.” The 2008 silencing experiment altered the secondary sulphur profile. Later low-tear onions can be useful products, but flavour must be measured rather than assumed. Sweetness and pungency are also different sensations. An onion can contain appreciable sugars and still generate a strong sulphur response when cut.

Kitchen advice deserves the same pressure test. A method should have a plausible way to reduce tissue damage, slow production or keep vapour away from the eyes. A candle, a piece of bread in the mouth or an elaborate cutting ritual may feel effective in one kitchen without establishing a reliable mechanism.

There is also a sensory perspective: the same family of reactions that inconveniences the eyes helps give raw onion its immediacy. Removing tears is not an obvious improvement if the accompanying aroma changes. Breeders and cooks therefore care about the whole flavour profile, not one irritating molecule in isolation.

## CONTEXT

Onions, garlic, leeks and related *Allium* plants are valued precisely because damaged tissue creates potent sulphur chemistry. Garlic channels its precursors toward compounds such as allicin; onion chemistry favours the lachrymatory factor and a different aroma. What tastes inert inside a whole bulb becomes vivid when cells are broken.

The 2002 discovery is a useful lesson in how scientific explanations improve. The earlier account was not absurd: alliinase really is essential, sulfenic acids really are produced and the tear factor really is unstable. What changed was the missing step. Researchers found that another enzyme directs the final transformation. A good correction keeps the valid parts of the earlier model while replacing its unsupported shortcut.

It also explains why chopping style matters only at the margins. More crushing generally means more disrupted cells and more opportunity for chemistry. Yet onion variety, temperature, freshness, blade movement, ventilation, working distance and individual sensitivity all vary. No single household trial cleanly isolates them.

Even the word “onion” hides variation. Bulbs bred for storage, sweetness, colour or regional growing conditions can carry different precursor profiles. Age and storage alter water content and tissue firmness. Two cooks using the same knife can therefore encounter different amounts of airborne irritant without either one's technique proving a universal rule.

## DEEPER

The onion's trick is timing. Reactive chemistry would be costly and potentially damaging if it ran everywhere in living tissue. Compartmentalisation stores the ingredients in safer forms and activates them only where a bite, cut or crush breaks the boundary. The knife does not merely reveal flavour; it completes the conditions for making it.

That pattern appears across biology. Mustard sharpness, horseradish heat and many plant aromas emerge when physical damage lets enzymes meet stored precursors. Food preparation is therefore not only mixing and heating. It is controlled tissue destruction, with size, pressure and timing changing which molecules are made and how quickly they escape.

The tears are the most visible measurement available to a cook, but they are a poor laboratory instrument. A strong response shows that enough irritant reached a particular person's eyes under particular conditions. It does not rank flavour, nutritional value or overall onion quality. The useful insight is narrower and better: the sensation records a reaction that began seconds earlier at the cut surface.

This is why the 2008 gene-silencing result is more informative than a roomful of kitchen anecdotes. Researchers changed a specific biological step and measured both the intended reduction and the chemistry that moved elsewhere. Causation became visible because the intervention had a target and the consequences were compared, not because the outcome felt dramatic.

## PRACTICAL TAKEAWAY

Work from the route the molecule must travel rather than from folklore:

1. Use a sharp knife and a stable board so slices are deliberate rather than crushed or repeatedly sawn.
2. Keep your face out of the plume rising from the cut surface and move air away from you with ordinary kitchen ventilation.
3. Chill the onion if convenient; lower temperature can slow molecular motion and enzyme activity, although it will not make every onion tear-free.
4. For a large batch, a well-ventilated food processor or close-fitting eye protection reduces direct exposure more reliably than a ritual with bread or flame.
5. Wash hands and tools before touching your eyes. If irritation is unusually strong or persists, stop the task and follow appropriate local health advice.

## REFLECT

An onion's tears begin as an architectural failure at microscopic scale: walls fall, ingredients meet and a volatile signal escapes. Which other familiar flavours appear only because chopping, grinding or chewing completes the chemistry? And when a kitchen trick seems to work, what part of the route—from cell damage to airborne exposure—could it actually have changed?

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Cross-cultural research found recurring features in songs used for infant care, and American babies relaxed to unfamiliar lullabies in languages they had never heard.

**What it means.** Human listeners may be sensitive to musical cues associated with soothing, but singing to a baby is still a cultural practice learned from other people.

**Risks and impact.** Calling lullabies “universal” can flatten cultural differences and ignore the Northern Aché, among whom decades of observation found no infant-directed song.

**What can be done.** Families can treat lullabies as living inheritance rather than performance: a familiar voice, repeated gently, matters more than perfect pitch.

**What to watch.** Future research needs broader observation and collaboration with communities to distinguish a widespread tendency from a behaviour present in every society.

## FACTS

In a 2020 experiment, 144 American infants aged two to fourteen months heard recordings from other societies. Each lullaby was paired with another song from the same society, such as a dance or healing song. During the unfamiliar lullabies, measures including heart rate, pupil size and skin conductance shifted in directions consistent with relaxation. The effect did not depend on understanding the language.

That result followed a large cross-cultural project published in *Science* in 2019. Researchers examined ethnographic descriptions and recordings from societies around the world. Music appeared widely, and songs serving similar purposes—dance, healing, love or infant care—showed patterns listeners could partly recognise across cultural boundaries.

Then came an important exception. A 2025 *Current Biology* report drew on 43 years of field knowledge among the Northern Aché of Paraguay. Researchers reported no observed dancing or infant-directed song. Northern Aché adults did sing, but songs centred on hunting, current events, conflict and deceased relatives. Parents soothed infants in other ways, including playful speech, facial expression and laughter.

## EVIDENCE

The infant experiment supports a specific claim: babies in one country showed average physiological and behavioural responses to eight unfamiliar lullabies compared with matched non-lullabies. It does not prove that any slow song will soothe every baby, nor that lullabies are biologically programmed as a parental behaviour.

The global song study found statistical regularities across a deliberately broad collection. Lullabies tended to be simpler and slower than dance songs, and listeners could infer song function above chance. Yet archives reflect what collectors preserved, and categories translated across societies are never perfectly neutral.

The Northern Aché evidence is unusually valuable because it rests on long-term observation and language knowledge. It challenges the strongest meaning of “universal”: a practice found in every known society. The authors suggest the group may have lost infant-directed song and dance during cultural and demographic disruption, but that historical explanation remains an inference. Researchers also noted that practices may have changed after fieldwork ended in 2020.

Taken together, the studies support human receptiveness to certain musical signals and the power of cultural transmission. They do not support a simple contest between nature and culture.

## PERSPECTIVES

The biological framing begins with the baby. Slow tempo, smooth contours and a caregiver’s attention may provide cues of safety before language is understood. It explains why unfamiliar songs produced measurable responses. Its danger is sliding from “infants can respond” to “all adults spontaneously invent the same practice.” A receiver’s sensitivity does not automatically create the cultural form it receives.

The cultural framing begins with imitation. People sing the tunes, words and vocal habits available around them. Lullabies can preserve dialect, migration history, religious imagery and family names. This explains enormous variation—and why a practice can disappear. Its danger is treating every cross-cultural resemblance as coincidence when experiments show that listeners detect shared functional cues.

A third perspective sees lullaby singing as a relationship rather than a musical object. The song coordinates breathing, gaze, repetition and attention between two people. That frame accommodates both biology and learning, but it is harder to test because “the lullaby” includes a voice, body, room and history, not merely an audio file.

The strongest account leaves the seam visible. Human infants bring capacities and needs; cultures supply repertoires of action. Neither half performs the song alone.

## CONTEXT

The word “lullaby” tempts outsiders to imagine a single genre: quiet, sweet and sung by a mother at bedtime. Real traditions are less tidy. Infant-directed songs may carry warnings, grief, household work, spiritual language or hopes for the child. They can be sung by fathers, grandparents, siblings and other caregivers, during work as well as sleep.

Researchers also use “universal” in several ways. It can mean present in every society, common across most societies, independently invented many times, or built from features that unfamiliar listeners can recognise. Evidence for one meaning does not automatically establish the others. The Northern Aché report is powerful because it forces those definitions apart.

There is a practical consequence for preserving culture. A song does not survive because it is useful in theory. Someone must sing it where another person can hear, remember and alter it. A recording can document a melody, but transmission usually includes when it is sung, who may sing it, how a child is held and what the words mean locally.

For families, this removes an unnecessary standard. Soothing song is not a vocal examination. Repetition and familiarity can make a modest tune meaningful. If singing does not settle a particular infant, that is not evidence of failure; babies differ, and distress can have needs music cannot answer. A lullaby is one caring practice among many.

## PEOPLE

The Northern Aché case deserves care because a community is not a missing square in somebody else’s theory. Kim Hill’s decades of fieldwork included learning the language and observing ordinary life at close range. Manvir Singh, whose earlier work supported broad claims about music, took the exception seriously when Hill raised it.

That exchange is a useful picture of research correcting itself. The new report did not portray Northern Aché parents as less attentive. It documented other ways they engaged unsettled babies: playful speech, smiles, laughter and expressive faces. The human need was present; the familiar musical solution was not observed.

Nor should the possible loss of practices be reduced to a romantic story about isolation. The researchers discuss population bottlenecks, epidemics, settlement on reservations and the forcible removal of children as possible disruptions to cultural transmission. Some details cannot now be reconstructed with certainty. What remains clear is that knowledge carried between generations can be resilient and still be vulnerable.

## DEEPER

Culture often looks natural from inside it. A melody learned before words feels as if it has always existed. That feeling can be so strong that we mistake inheritance for instinct.

The lullaby offers a gentler way to see the distinction. A baby need not understand the lyrics to respond to pace, repetition and attention. But an adult needs some model—remembered, borrowed or improvised—to turn those ingredients into a song. Biology opens a door; culture decides what passes through it.

This is why traditions do not sit safely in the past. They survive as repeated acts in the present. Every performance is both preservation and change: a name substituted, a verse forgotten, a melody bent toward the singer’s voice. Purity is not the price of continuity. Participation is.

The Northern Aché evidence adds humility. Even practices that appear almost everywhere are not inevitable. They can be lost, replaced or never observed. That makes cultural transmission more remarkable, not less. Across generations, people keep teaching one another small technologies of attention: how to greet, mourn, cook, soothe and remember.

A lullaby may calm a child for only a few minutes. Its longer work is to show how a human skill travels—from one unguarded voice into another person’s memory, waiting for a night when it will be needed again.

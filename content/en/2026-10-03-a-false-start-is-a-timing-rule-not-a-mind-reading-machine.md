## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** In elite sprinting, one thousandth of a second can place an athlete on opposite sides of a rule. A start information system flags a possible false start when the measured response is below 0.100 seconds.

**What it means.** The threshold is a practical boundary, not a device for reading intent. It converts force detected at the blocks into an officiating signal. That is different from proving that a runner anticipated the gun.

**Risks and impact.** A crisp number can make a measurement look more certain than the chain that produced it. Sensor design, force thresholds, signal delivery and the official's judgment all matter.

**What can be done.** Read a start time as a rule-governed measurement: ask what was detected, by which system and how the rules use the result.

**What to watch.** Research continues to test whether 100 milliseconds is the right boundary and whether start systems measure responses consistently enough for such close decisions.

## FACTS

World Athletics' 2026 rules require athletes in relevant races to remain in their final set position until the report of the gun or approved starting apparatus. At major competitions, an approved start information system connects to the starting blocks. A reaction time below 0.100 seconds produces an acoustic signal for the starter indicating a possible false start.

The word “possible” matters. The equipment does not disqualify an athlete by itself. The starter considers the system's information and what happened at the line. The rules distinguish a starting motion from every twitch, loss of balance or disturbance. The official decision belongs to the officiating process.

The recorded “reaction time” is also narrower than everyday reaction. It is the interval between the start signal and a detected change in force at the blocks. It is not the instant the sound reaches the ear, the brain registers it, a muscle first activates or the body visibly leaves the blocks. Those events occur in a sequence, and different instruments can choose different points in that sequence.

## EVIDENCE

The 100-millisecond boundary was built around an assumed minimum auditory reaction time. World Athletics' own sprint-start research page says that premise was the reason for commissioning a study of neuromuscular responses. In that small project, seven national-level Finnish sprinters were tested with instrumented blocks and muscle recordings.

A peer-reviewed study indexed by PubMed examined nine athletes and found evidence that responses below 100 milliseconds can occur. Its authors separated electrical muscle activity from the later force detected at a footplate. That difference is not a loophole; it shows why the definition of “response” matters.

A 2020 critical review surveyed start information systems and concluded that technology, athlete characteristics and event-detection methods can affect the reaction time reported in competition. It also noted that the first detectable biomechanical event may be force through the arms, while existing systems commonly infer response from force at the foot blocks.

More recently, a 2025 statistical analysis prompted by Devon Allen's 0.099-second disqualification at the 2022 World Championships compared elite competition data across venues. The authors found systematic differences between competitions and modelled sub-0.100 responses as rare but physiologically plausible. That paper is a preprint, not the final word. It strengthens the case for examining the measurement, not for ignoring the rule.

## PERSPECTIVES

### The official's view

A race needs a decision that can be applied quickly and consistently. Without a boundary, arguments about anticipation could become subjective and endless. The electronic system gives the starter evidence that the naked eye cannot reliably supply.

### The athlete's view

A difference of one millisecond can erase years of preparation. If two approved systems identify the start of force differently, “same rule” may not mean identical measurement. Athletes therefore care about calibration and protocol, not only the number on the results screen.

### The scientist's view

Human response is continuous, but rules are categorical. There is no natural cliff at exactly 99 versus 100 milliseconds. A threshold can still be defensible if its purpose, error rate and measurement method are explicit.

### The spectator's view

Television graphics encourage a simple story: quick means brilliant, too quick means cheating. The evidence supports a more careful account. A false start is a rule violation; it need not be a moral diagnosis.

## CONTEXT

False-start rules have changed because incentives matter. Earlier systems allowed one false start to the field or disqualified an athlete only after a later offence. Research on championship data found that athletes adjusted their response times when the penalty structure changed. A start is therefore both a sensory task and a strategic environment.

The technology tries to make that environment fair. The gun signal can be distributed through speakers behind each athlete so lane position does not create a meaningful sound delay. Instrumented blocks can detect force before an official could see motion. Yet every measurement system still has definitions, thresholds and tolerances.

This is common in sport. Goal-line technology, photo finishes and electronic touches in swimming do not remove judgment from competition. They move judgment into the design of instruments and rules. Precision is valuable precisely because the institution has agreed what the number will mean.

## DEEPER

The false-start line is a useful example of measurement humility. A stopwatch display may show three decimal places, but decimal places describe resolution, not automatically truth. To interpret the figure, one must know where time zero came from, what event stopped the interval and how stable those detections are.

The same discipline helps outside sport. Credit scores, medical cut-offs and performance targets also turn continua into categories. The category may be necessary for action, while cases near the boundary remain more alike than the labels suggest. The right response is neither blind faith in thresholds nor casual rejection of them. It is to ask whether the measurement and the decision are fit for the stated purpose.

For sprinting, that means keeping three questions separate. Did the system record a response below the threshold? Did the athlete violate the competition rule? What can be inferred about anticipation or intent? The first is a measurement, the second an official judgment, and the third often uncertain.

## PRACTICAL IMPACT

When a close false-start decision appears, look for the official reaction time, the system used and any explanation from officials. Avoid translating “below 0.100” into “the runner guessed” unless independent evidence supports that claim. The cleanest language is usually the most accurate: the athlete was disqualified under the start rule.

## READER OUTCOME

You should be able to explain why a 0.099-second reading can lead to disqualification without claiming that the number directly reveals intention or that all start systems measure the same biological event.

## REFLECT

A rule needs a boundary; a human process rarely supplies one naturally. Where else do precise cut-offs create decisive categories from measurements that contain method, noise and uncertainty?

The deeper skill is to hold two ideas together. Sport cannot function without enforceable lines, and an enforceable line does not become a law of physiology merely because a display gives it three decimals.

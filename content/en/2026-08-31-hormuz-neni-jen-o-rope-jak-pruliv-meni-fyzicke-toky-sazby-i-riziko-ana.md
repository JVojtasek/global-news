With Hormuz, watching the oil price is not enough; what matters is whether physical flows, insurance and tanker rates, the number of escorts and signs of fatigue in maritime logistics are improving at the same time.

Oil right now is not only in barrels but also in ship maintenance calendars and sailors' shifts. According to a Bloomberg summary, the US conflict with Iran is, after six months, at a stage where oil flows from the Persian Gulf have recovered to only about 40 % of pre-war levels, the Strait of Hormuz remains restricted and the US Navy is running into strain on personnel, equipment and finances.

An important detail: Bloomberg also says that a new deal concerning Venezuelan oil fields is unlikely to bring quick relief to global supply. In other words, the market is getting oil back slowly, while the military escort of tankers is wearing out quickly.

Most commentary gets stuck on the line: Hormuz restricted, oil pricier. But the more interesting part is the second line of the receipt: the price of energy security is measured not only by the barrel but also by the endurance of the fleet.

The Strait of Hormuz is the narrow neck between the Persian Gulf and the world market. When it is restricted, it is not enough to say: someone elsewhere will produce more. There also has to be a safe route, insurance, tankers, crews, military escort and the political will to keep this traffic going for weeks or months. Here oil resembles a restaurant with a single lift to the kitchen: even if the cooks keep cooking, the guests do not get fed if the lift runs only now and then and the repairman has not slept for the third night running.

The fresh point: the geopolitical premium in the oil price does not have to disappear just because production is rising somewhere. It disappears only when the whole logistics chain is restored. And Bloomberg's figure of around 40 % of pre-war flows suggests the market is not in normal mode but in improvisation mode. Venezuela in this story is not a magic spare battery; according to Bloomberg, it has more of a long charging cable.

The best indicator therefore may not be the oil price itself but a small dashboard of fatigue: how many convoys must be escorted, how many ships are in service compared with normal rotation, whether maintenance intervals are being extended, how war-risk insurance rates are rising, how tanker freight rates are moving, and whether reports appear of munitions resupply or budget requests for operations. These are the things that tell you whether the system is just more expensive or already getting out of breath.

The historical parallel is the Tanker War of the 1980s during the Iran–Iraq conflict: the biggest lesson was not only in the oil price but in the fact that securing sea lanes consumes time, ships, munitions, maintenance and political capital. The market often prices the shock but underestimates the fatigue of whoever keeps order.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The direct impact falls mainly on oil and energy. A higher risk premium can help oil and gas producers such as Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), ConocoPhillips (COP) or the European integrated companies Shell (SHEL) and BP (BP), if higher prices outweigh operational and political risks. Oil services, for example SLB (SLB) or Halliburton (HAL), may benefit from debates about increasing production away from threatened routes, but that is more a thematic exposure than a direct impact from Hormuz.

On the other side are fuel consumers: airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL), United Airlines (UAL) and American Airlines (AAL), logistics such as FedEx (FDX) or UPS (UPS), chemicals such as Dow (DOW) and, in general, companies for which oil gets into costs like sand into shoes.

A special basket is defence and naval infrastructure. Companies such as Huntington Ingalls Industries (HII), General Dynamics (GD), Lockheed Martin (LMT) or RTX (RTX) are only illustrative examples: prolonged pressure on military readiness can intensify the debate about budgets, servicing, munitions and shipbuilding capacity. But it is not an automatic equation of the conflict-equals-rising-stocks kind; budgets have political brakes and margins can suffer from costs.

A better framework than 'oil up/down' is three clocks: oil flow, fleet fatigue, replacement supply. If flows hold around 40 % and Hormuz remains restricted, the market may keep attaching a security premium to oil. If flows move well above half of pre-war levels, the number of escorted convoys falls, war-risk insurance rates ease and ships return to a more normal maintenance rotation, part of the tension may fade. And if someone promises a quick replacement from Venezuela, it is worth asking: are these barrels today, or a political postcard from the future?

A practical filter for similar news: do not read only the headline about the strait, but look for a combination of five indicators — physical flows, insurance rates, tanker rates, the number of extraordinary escorts and mentions of maintenance or capacity replenishment. One of these items may be noise; but when three worsen at once, the market is usually dealing not just with the price of a commodity but with the capacity of the whole security machine.

A 'risk premium' is like ordering a taxi to the airport during a snowstorm. The trip itself may still be just as long, but the price jumps because the driver risks delays, detours and chaos. Oil is similar: a barrel does not have to be physically scarcer everywhere in the world, but when the route out of the Persian Gulf is uncertain, the market pays extra for its nerves. For stocks, this means pressure in favour of energy and against big fuel consumers; for the ordinary person, it can over time feed into the prices of petrol, air tickets and the transport of goods.

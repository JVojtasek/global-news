With European yields, watching rates rise is not enough; the key is whether the BTP–Bund, OAT–Bund and Bonos–Bund spreads and bank credit premiums are widening, because that is exactly where an ordinary rise in the cost of money can turn into a broader problem of trust.

When the bond market gets a fright, shares usually just cough; in Europe, though, the whole electrical wiring can start to cough.

According to the excerpt provided for id=359850, rising funding costs are making American investors nervous, but in Europe they are causing even greater panic. The point is not just that bond yields are rising; the problem is that in Europe, more expensive money tips more quickly into questions about state budgets, banks and corporate refinancing.

America is mainly dealing with the price of money. Europe is dealing with the price of money plus the question of whether all the sockets in the house run off the same fuse. That is a fundamental difference.

With US bonds, the market typically watches how higher yields change equity valuations: the higher the risk-free yield, the tougher the competition for expensively valued shares. In Europe, however, fragmentation is added on top: one monetary policy from the European Central Bank, but many countries with different levels of debt, growth and political risk. That is why it matters to watch not just the absolute level of yields, but also the spread between safer and riskier government bonds within the eurozone. When these spreads widen, it is not just accounting cosmetics; it is a sign that the market is starting to distinguish more sharply between different parts of Europe.

A practical "stress gauge" is simple: 10-year Italian BTPs against the German Bund, French OATs against the Bund and Spanish Bonos against the Bund. As a rough guide, not a mechanical rule: for BTP–Bund, a range up to roughly 150 basis points is often seen as a normal risk premium, the 150–250 point area already forces the market to think about budgets and politics, and above roughly 250–300 points people have historically started talking about fragmentation stress in the eurozone. With OAT–Bund, the market is more sensitive, because France belongs closer to the core: a move towards the 70–100 basis point area usually already says that the "safer part of Europe" is not as uniform as it looked. With Spanish Bonos, the comparison is what is most interesting: if both Italy and Spain are widening, it is more likely broader pressure on the periphery; if it is mainly Italy pulling away, the market may be punishing specifically Italian fiscal or political risk.

The second layer is the banks: bank CDS, meaning the price of insurance against a bank failing to repay its debt, and the iTraxx Senior Financials index, which summarises the credit risk of large European financial institutions. When sovereign spreads widen and bank insurance gets more expensive at the same time, an old European bogeyman appears: the state props up the banks, the banks hold government bonds, and everyone pretends to be everyone else's support until the table starts to wobble. Third, swap spreads are worth watching, meaning the difference between the swap rate and the yield on a government bond. Sharp moves here can point to a liquidity problem, demand for "safe" collateral, or trust in government paper.

The counterintuitive point: higher yields are not always bad news for everyone. For some financial firms, they may mean a better return on newly invested money. But in Europe, the catch is that the extra yield may come wrapped in worse credit risk. It is like getting a higher interest rate on a savings account at a bank whose small print everyone is suddenly starting to read nervously.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

Long-dated bonds are on the minus side: when the yield rises, the price of older bonds with a lower coupon usually falls. The most sensitive are instruments with long duration, meaning a long time for the money to come back. With European government bonds, though, the crucial thing is whether everything is falling in a similar way, or whether spreads such as BTP–Bund, OAT–Bund and Bonos–Bund are pulling apart.

Debt-financed real estate and infrastructure tend to come under pressure: European property owners such as Vonovia (VNA.DE) or Unibail-Rodamco-Westfield (URW.AS) have to work out at what cost they will roll over loans and how higher yields will feed into the value of their buildings. For utilities such as Enel (ENEL.MI) or Iberdrola (IBE.MC), the key is to watch the ratio of debt to operating profit, the cost of new debt, and whether regulated returns are enough to keep up with more expensive financing. If sovereign spreads in the countries where these companies operate widen at the same time, the market may start demanding a higher premium on corporate bonds too.

Banks such as BNP Paribas (BNP.PA), Deutsche Bank (DBK.DE) or UniCredit (UCG.MI) are a mixed case. Higher rates can help the interest margin if banks do not raise rates for depositors just as quickly. At the same time, though, there is a risk of weaker loan demand, worse repayment and losses on bond portfolios. The main things to watch are deposit sensitivity, loan quality, the maturity of the bonds held, bank CDS and iTraxx Senior Financials. If sovereign spreads and bank credit premiums worsen at the same time, it is no longer just about higher rates, but about trust in the financial plumbing.

Insurers such as Allianz (ALV.DE) or AXA (CS.PA) may benefit from investing new premiums at a higher yield. But here too: the market repricing of older bonds can hurt in the short term. The difference is whether the higher yield comes from a calmer repricing of rates, or from a nervous rise in risk premiums.

With news like this, the question "are yields rising?" is not enough. A better checklist: are only safe yields rising, or risk premiums too? For BTP–Bund, as a rough guide, it is important to see the difference between the normal range up to roughly 150 basis points, the tenser 150–250 point area, and the zone above roughly 250–300 points, where the market has historically started worrying about more serious fragmentation. For OAT–Bund, watch whether France is holding as a calm core, or whether the spread is moving towards the tens of points that already resemble a political and fiscal repricing. For banks, add iTraxx Senior Financials and bank CDS. And then ask the boring but profitably important question: is new debt getting more expensive faster than companies' revenues and profits are growing?

Picture the spread between BTPs and the Bund as the difference in rent between two flats in the same city: one is in a building with a new roof and a solid lift, the other in a building where the lights sometimes flicker. When the difference in rent grows, it is not just that housing has got more expensive; people have started thinking harder about which building they trust. For the market, this means investors are less likely to lump Europe together, shares of banks and indebted companies may be more sensitive, and for ordinary people it can spill over into more expensive loans, more cautious banks and a slower economy.

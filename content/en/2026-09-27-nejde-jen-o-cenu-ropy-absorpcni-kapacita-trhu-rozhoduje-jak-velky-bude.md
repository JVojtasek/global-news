With energy shocks, watch not only the oil price but above all the market's absorption capacity: inventories, days of cover, OPEC+ spare capacity, the futures curve, crack spreads and companies' ability to pass costs on.

Oil sometimes behaves less like a commodity and more like a spare tyre: you notice it most at the moment you find out it is flatter than you expected.

The report points out that oil and gas markets face higher risk because global strategic oil reserves are thinning and tensions around Iran remain unresolved. The important shift is in expectations: the market is counting less on a quick return of supply and prices to a calmer regime.

It is not just that oil may be more expensive. It is that the market is losing its "shock absorber" — and that can be tracked more concretely than through a headline about geopolitics.

Strategic oil reserves are not a magic well from which governments can pour cheap oil onto the market forever. They work more like an airbag: they help in a crash, but after use they have to be refilled. If inventories are lower, every further geopolitical snag hits the market harder — because traders price not only the current barrel, but also how many fire extinguishers are left on the wall.

In practice, it is not just about the level of Brent or WTI. The market's "absorption capacity" has several gauges on the dashboard: the state of strategic reserves, commercial inventories in OECD countries, the number of days of demand cover, OPEC+ spare production capacity, the shape of the futures curve, refiners' crack spreads and also strain in transport — tanker rates, for example, or the volume of oil waiting at sea. Each of these indicators measures something different: how much we have in the tank, how much we can quickly add in production, whether the market is paying a premium for immediate delivery and whether refiners can turn a more expensive input into more expensive fuel.

This is the counterintuitive part: even without an immediate supply outage, the price can contain a higher risk premium. Tension around Iran need not immediately mean a physical shortage of oil; it may be enough that refiners, carriers and traders start demanding a bigger safety cushion in prices, insurance, inventories and contracts. The market then looks less like a supermarket with an empty aisle and more like a restaurant that has its ingredients — except the head chef is suddenly ordering twice as cautiously because the supplier is unreliable.

The historical point after the large reserve releases of recent years is simple: a reserve can dampen a shock today, but refilling it later itself becomes future demand. The airbag saves you in the crash; the repair bill just moves to later.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

Higher oil typically helps oil and gas producers, especially those with low production costs and less price hedging. Examples of the impact: integrated oil companies such as Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), Shell (SHEL) or BP (BP), and more standalone producers such as ConocoPhillips (COP) or EOG Resources (EOG). The mechanism is simple: if they sell output at higher realised prices and costs do not rise as fast, the margin widens.

Alongside them, energy services companies such as SLB (SLB) and Halliburton (HAL) may benefit if higher prices lead producers to step up activity. In gas and LNG, companies such as Cheniere Energy (LNG) may be sensitive, but the impact depends on contracts, regional prices and capacity, not just on a headline about oil.

Refiners such as Phillips 66 (PSX), Valero Energy (VLO) or Marathon Petroleum (MPC) have a mixed picture. More expensive oil is their input cost; what decides is the difference between the input price and the price of petrol, diesel and other products, that is, the crack spread. In other words: dearer flour does not help a baker who cannot raise the price of bread rolls.

On the other side stand airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL) and United Airlines (UAL), logistics such as FedEx (FDX) and UPS (UPS), chemicals such as Dow (DOW) or BASF (BAS.DE) and some consumer companies. Higher energy is either a direct cost or a brake on demand. For bonds, the main damage is the second round: more expensive energy can keep inflation higher, which makes rapid rate cuts harder and weighs on long-term bond prices.

With news like this, following "oil up = energy up" is not enough. A better framework has three steps: 1) who has direct exposure to the commodity price, 2) who has price hedging and long contracts, 3) who can pass higher costs on to the customer. Add to that the measures of absorption capacity: strategic and commercial inventories, days of cover, OPEC+ spare capacity, the futures curve, crack spreads and strain in tanker transport. In financial statements, look for the realised selling price, the volume of hedging, unit production costs, capital expenditure and management's comments on demand. For carriers and industry, watch fuel costs and the ability to pass them into prices.

Strategic oil reserves are like cans of petrol in the cellar of an entire country. In a crisis you can pull them out and keep driving for a while. But if they are no longer as full, the next problem hurts more: oil can get more expensive faster, companies that use a lot of fuel face cost pressure, and ordinary people may feel it through more expensive transport, energy and goods that had to travel somewhere by lorry, ship or plane.

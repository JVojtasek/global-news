In shipping, the key is not just geopolitical tension, but above all whether higher rates last longer than the shortage of ships before new capacity arrives around 2028.

Shipping right now resembles a motorway in a storm: the cars are no faster, but every detour raises the price of the ride. The report says that for crude oil tankers there is a real geopolitical premium and the market remains tight, but newly ordered ships may gradually close this window towards 2028. In dry bulk, meanwhile, fundamentals are improving, especially for the largest Capesize vessels.

The biggest mistake in shipping is to think that "war = everything in transport goes up". In reality, war does not create one market; it breaks the map into several different game plans: crude tankers deal with route risk and insurance, dry bulk deals mainly with tonne-miles for iron ore and coal, and shipowners' shares deal with whether high rates can be turned into cash before the new ships arrive.

That is the counterintuitive part: in shipping, it is often not "more world" that makes money, but "a less efficient world". When a ship has to take a longer route, waits longer in port or faces more expensive insurance, capacity disappears even though the number of ships on paper stays the same. It is as if a city did not have fewer taxis, but half of them had to go round three extra roundabouts because of road closures. The app then shows a higher fare without residents suddenly starting to go to the opera en masse.

The key detail is the year 2028. If the orderbook of new ships starts delivering capacity, today's shortage may turn into yesterday's story. So the market is not just asking "is there a war?", but above all: how many ships are available today, how many are on order, how old is the fleet, how expensive is it to operate, and are longer routes really soaking up capacity.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

In the short term, the environment may help owners of crude tankers if the geopolitical premium feeds into spot rates and daily earnings after costs, known as TCE. Examples of companies investors watch in such an environment are Frontline (FRO), DHT Holdings (DHT), International Seaways (INSW) or Teekay Tankers (TNK). What decides is not the company name, but the metrics: the share of ships on the spot market, the cash breakeven, debt, fleet age and sensitivity to fuel prices.

In dry bulk, Capesize ships, the giants for iron ore and coal, are at the centre. This is exactly where the report mentions better fundamentals, and it sounds positive on companies such as Seanergy Maritime (SHIP) and Star Bulk Carriers (SBLK). A similarly watched name is Golden Ocean (GOGL). Here you need to look at demand for iron ore, commodity exports, waiting times in ports, steelmakers' inventories, and also how many days the ships are actually employed.

The same development may hurt commodity importers and refineries if higher transport costs shrink their margins. And watch out: from 2028 the tables may turn against shipowners who ordered capacity aggressively. In good times, new ships are like ordering more umbrellas during a downpour — great, until it stops raining.

With news like this, it pays to check four things: spot rates versus long-term contracts, the orderbook relative to the existing fleet, real tonne-miles instead of political headlines, and companies' financial leverage. The positive scenario rests on longer routes and risk premiums lasting long enough for shipowners to generate cash. The negative scenario is simpler: the geopolitical episode fades, routes normalise, China disappoints on ore demand, or new capacity arrives sooner than the market expects.

The "orderbook" is the list of ships that shipowners have ordered from shipyards but that are not yet sailing. Imagine a pizzeria that is short of couriers right now, so delivery gets more expensive. But the owner has already ordered ten new scooters that will arrive in a few months. Delivery may be expensive today, but once the scooters arrive, the shortage disappears. For the market, this means: shipping shares can benefit from the tension only until new capacity starts pushing freight rates down.

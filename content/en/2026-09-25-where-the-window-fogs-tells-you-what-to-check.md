## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** A window turned cloudy, but “condensation” is only the name of the process, not the diagnosis.

**What it means.** Water appears when moist air meets glass cold enough to reach that air’s dew point. The important clue is which air touched which surface.

**Risks and impact.** Brief outdoor dew may be harmless. Repeated indoor water can wet frames and feed mould. Haze trapped between sealed panes points to a glazing problem that household ventilation cannot reach.

**What can be done.** Identify the wet surface, note when it appears, measure room humidity and check ventilation, heating, leaks and seals before buying a remedy.

**What to watch.** Escalate if water persists, mould returns, timber softens, stains spread after rain or moisture remains between panes. Those patterns move the problem beyond a morning wipe-down.

## FACTS

Condensation begins at the dew point: the temperature at which water vapour becomes liquid droplets. NOAA explains that relative humidity depends on temperature. The same amount of water vapour produces a higher relative humidity in cooler air, and saturation is reached when the air can no longer retain all that vapour.

A cold window cools the thin layer of air touching it. If that layer reaches its dew point, water forms on the glass. The Australian Government’s YourHome guide gives a useful example: at 20°C and 60% relative humidity, the dew point is about 12°C. Any indoor surface at or below that temperature can collect condensation even though the middle of the room feels comfortable.

The glass is therefore a witness, not necessarily the culprit. Three variables meet there: how much moisture is in the nearby air, how cold the surface is and how freely air moves around it. Cooking, showers, drying clothes and breathing add vapour. Outdoor temperature, glazing and insulation shape surface temperature. Curtains, furniture and closed vents can restrict circulation.

## EVIDENCE

Location divides the common cases.

**On the room side**, droplets usually mean indoor air has met a cold interior surface. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency calls condensation a possible sign of high humidity and recommends a humidity gauge as a simple measurement. One cold morning is not proof of a building defect; a repeating wet sill, musty smell or mould deserves attention.

**On the outdoor face**, the mechanism is reversed. Humid outside air has met an exterior pane below its dew point, much like dew on grass. This often clears after the glass warms. It does not by itself show that indoor ventilation is poor, and wiping the inside cannot change it.

**Between panes**, the moisture is inside the glazing cavity. A factory-sealed double- or triple-glazed unit is meant to keep that space isolated and dry. Persistent internal haze therefore needs assessment of the sealed unit or its seals. FENSA’s homeowner guidance treats cavity condensation separately and directs householders toward an approved installer for work inside the assembly.

## PERSPECTIVES

For a resident, the first temptation is to purchase a dehumidifier or replacement window. Both may be reasonable in the right case, but neither is a universal answer. A dehumidifier does little for outdoor dew or a failed insulated-glass seal. New glazing may warm the inner surface, yet indoor moisture still needs a route out.

For a landlord or building manager, “just open the window” can become an unfair reflex. The moisture load comes from occupants, but the building determines whether normal life can be ventilated and heated without cold bridges and chronic wet surfaces. YourHome stresses that climate, construction and use all affect risk.

For an installer, the pane location prevents an expensive category error. Surface water calls for a moisture-and-temperature investigation. Water inside a sealed cavity calls for inspection of the glazing unit. Staining around the frame after rain may indicate penetration rather than condensation at all.

For a health-conscious household, the concern is not the droplet itself but persistent dampness. EPA notes that mould gradually damages what it grows on. The Energy Saving Trust distinguishes occasional condensation that clears quickly from moisture that accumulates, damages finishes or supports mould.

## CONTEXT

Homes once lost heat—and moisture—through leaky fabric almost by accident. Better draught sealing and insulation reduce energy loss, but airtightness without planned ventilation can also reduce drying. YourHome describes this as a balance: heat, air and water-vapour flows have to be controlled together.

That history explains an apparent paradox. A newer, more efficient window can show dew outside because its outer pane is no longer warmed as much by escaping indoor heat. Meanwhile, the same home can develop indoor condensation after air leaks are sealed if extractor fans, background vents or occupant habits do not remove moisture. The two surfaces are reporting different air masses.

Condensation also differs from penetrating and rising damp. The Energy Saving Trust says a concentrated patch that worsens with rain suggests water entering from outside, while a tide mark low on a wall points toward rising damp. Window droplets are conspicuous, but hidden leaks and cold corners may matter more.

The word “humidity” can also mislead when detached from temperature. Fifty per cent relative humidity does not describe a fixed quantity of water across every room and season. It describes how close the air is to saturation at its current temperature. That is why a cooler edge, metal spacer or poorly insulated reveal may become wet while the centre of the room—and even the centre of the pane—stays dry.

## PEOPLE

The problem is easier to diagnose when people are not treated as the problem. A family must cook, wash and breathe; a tenant may not control the heating system, window specification or extractor fan. Advice should begin with observable conditions and responsibilities, not blame.

Some households also cannot ventilate or heat as freely as generic checklists assume. Outdoor pollution, noise, security, disability and energy cost change what is practical. Mechanical extraction, repaired vents, insulation work or professional moisture assessment may be more realistic than keeping windows open.

Responsibility also depends on tenure. A resident can use an extractor and report a pattern; an owner or manager may need to repair a failed fan, defective seal, leak or thermal bridge. A dated log helps both sides distinguish a behaviour change from work the building itself requires.

## DEEPER

Use a five-minute “which surface?” audit before choosing a fix.

1. **Locate the water.** Touch only accessible surfaces. Is it on the room face, outside face or visibly trapped between panes? Check more than one window.
2. **Record the timing.** Note weather, room, cooking or showering, heating and whether the moisture clears after ventilation or sunlight. Photograph repeated patterns, not just the worst moment.
3. **Measure the room.** Put an inexpensive hygrometer away from the glass and log temperature and relative humidity at the same times. A reading is context, not a universal pass/fail line: colder surfaces can condense at otherwise ordinary room humidity.
4. **Test the low-cost causes.** Use extractor fans that vent outdoors, clear trickle vents, avoid blocking air movement with heavy curtains, dry standing water and check whether clothes are being dried indoors without extraction.
5. **Follow the evidence.** If indoor condensation falls, moisture and ventilation were important. If fog remains between panes, seek a glazing assessment. If stains track rain, plaster crumbles, timber softens or mould returns, investigate leaks, insulation gaps and building fabric rather than endlessly wiping glass.

The practical rule is simple: change the air when the water is on your side; inspect the assembly when it is trapped inside; wait for the weather when harmless dew is outside. Persistent damage overrides every shortcut.

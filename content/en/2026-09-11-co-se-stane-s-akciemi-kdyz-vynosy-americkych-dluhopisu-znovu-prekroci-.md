A rally can come even without great news about the economy, but if US Treasury yields break through the 5% area again, the relief in stocks can quickly fade.

When bond yields knock on 5%, the stock market doesn't behave like an exchange, but like a dog at the vet: it puts on a brave face, but flinches at every rustle of paper. According to Thursday's MarketWatch commentary, the S&P 500 at the close had logged four losing days in a row and was 2.7% below last month's record, while a global bond sell-off pushed US benchmark yields toward the 5% mark. The headline promises a "face-ripper rally" — a sharp rebound that hurts mainly those who stood against the market — but the excerpt shows the main backdrop: not stocks, but bonds.

The most interesting thing isn't the four-day losing streak itself. On the stock market, that's about as shocking as a queue at a trdelník stand in Old Town Square. What's interesting is that a possible rally wouldn't necessarily be proof of market health, but relief that the bond market has stopped forcing every other asset class to reprice.

The standard explanation goes: stocks compete with bonds for investors' money. When the 10-year US yield heads toward 5%, an investor asks: Why should I pay high multiples for future earnings when a government bond offers me a decent nominal yield without corporate risk? That's the duel of earnings yield vs. Treasury yield — the yield from corporate earnings against the yield of a government bond.

But that's only the first floor. The second floor is portfolio mechanics: when bond prices fall sharply, the "safe" part of the portfolio suddenly doesn't behave like an airbag, but like a passenger who yanks the steering wheel in a corner. Portfolio managers then often cut risk not just in bonds, but across the market — trimming stocks, less liquid positions and the most expensive growth stories. That's why the market can jump even without great news about the economy: it's enough for the forced selling of everything with long duration to stop.

So the key isn't just 5% as a round number. Three things matter: the level of the yield, the speed of the move and the composition of the move. In other words: is the problem the high yield itself, or that it got there too quickly? Is the real yield rising, i.e. the yield after subtracting inflation, which directly raises the bar for valuations? Or is the market demanding a higher reward for uncertainty, the so-called term premium? For stocks, it makes a difference whether bonds are saying "the economy is strong" or "we want higher compensation because we're afraid".

### Who it helps and who it hurts

If yields hit a ceiling at 5% and start to retreat, growth stocks usually breathe a sigh of relief fastest: chipmakers such as NVIDIA (NVDA), the foundry TSMC (TSM) or software companies such as Microsoft (MSFT). Their valuations rest on earnings far in the future, and at a higher discount rate those are worth less today. The impact can be checked through gross margins, data center revenue growth, production utilization and TSMC's capex outlook.

Conversely, long-rising yields hurt sectors that look like bond substitutes: utilities such as NextEra Energy (NEE), telecoms, or real estate trusts such as Prologis (PLD). For these, watch interest costs, debt maturities and the ability to pass costs through to rents or tariffs.

Banks are double-edged. JPMorgan Chase (JPM) or Bank of America (BAC) can benefit from higher rates through interest income, but at the same time they're squeezed by pricier deposits and the repricing of bond portfolios. In the accounts, look for net interest margin, deposit costs and unrealized losses in the securities portfolio.

With news like this, don't just ask "will stocks rise?", but "who's at the wheel: earnings or yields?" A practical checklist: 1) is the US 10-year yield holding below 5%, or breaking through that threshold, 2) was the move in yields slow or sharp, 3) is more than just a handful of mega-caps rising, 4) is the ratio of advancing to declining stocks in the S&P 500 improving, 5) is the pressure not spreading into credit spreads — the premiums companies pay over government bonds, 6) are companies confirming margins and outlooks in their quarterly results.

A rally driven solely by relief in bonds can be fast, spectacular and treacherous. If yields go back up, the same spring that launched stocks can pull them back down again.

The discount rate is the financial version of the question: How much is money I'll only receive later worth to me? Imagine a friend promises you dinner in a year. When everything is cheap and calm today, that sounds nice. But if in the meantime rent, gasoline and beer all get more expensive, that future dinner suddenly doesn't seem so valuable. The market works similarly: the higher bond yields are, the less investors value companies' distant earnings. In practice, this can push down mainly expensive growth stocks and make financing more expensive for companies and households.

The biggest risk is mistaking summit rhetoric for a finished deal, and a geopolitical threat for zero impact, because markets often price risk before the event.

Picture the world economy as a restaurant kitchen at the lunch rush: one cook promises new deals, a second stands in the doorway with a barrel of oil, and a third manager wants to bring AI into everything — but they all need the same power outlet.

President Trump hinted that he expects more deals at the summit with Chinese President Xi Jinping, while Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian warned that Iran will not tolerate freedom of navigation through the Strait of Hormuz if sanctions and the American blockade continue. Meanwhile, corporate leaders keep wrestling with how fast, and at what price, to bring artificial intelligence into decision-making and operations.

The point is not just business optimism versus geopolitical risk. It is two bottlenecks at once: a physical one, through which oil and LNG flow, and a digital one, through which chips, computing capacity and export rules flow.

The market often reads the US–China summit as a story about tariffs and equity sentiment. But next to Hormuz, it also becomes a story about inflation and margins. According to the US EIA, roughly a fifth of global petroleum liquids consumption passes through the Strait of Hormuz over the long term, along with a significant share of Qatar's LNG. That is no minor pipe in the basement; it is the main water line of a skyscraper.

The counterintuitive point: good news for AI is not automatically good news for the whole technology sector if it makes energy more expensive at the same time. Data centers are not little clouds in the sky but electricity factories dressed up as software. If tension in Hormuz raises the risk premium in oil and LNG, the effect can spill over into electricity prices, cooling, backup power and long-term energy contracts. The AI boom is then not only a question of chips, but also a question of the power bill.

The second filter: summit headlines often run ahead of implementation. For the market, a handshake photo carries a different weight than a concrete text on tariffs, export licenses, rare earths, agricultural purchases or restrictions on advanced chips. The difference is like that between the menu on a restaurant's door and the food on the plate.

### Who It Helps and Who It Hurts

In energy, oil and gas producers such as Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), Shell (SHEL) or BP (BP) may benefit from a higher risk premium, mainly through the price of realized barrels and upstream margins. Sensitivity, however, depends on hedging, the tax regime and the share of production versus refining.

Refiners such as Valero Energy (VLO) or Marathon Petroleum (MPC) are a more complicated story: pricier oil is a cost, but if the crack spread widens, that is, the gap between the price of fuels and crude oil, margins can hold up. Airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL), United Airlines (UAL) and American Airlines (AAL), by contrast, watch the price of jet fuel; what matters is how much they have hedged and how quickly they can pass costs on to ticket prices.

In shipping, tankers such as Frontline (FRO) or Teekay Tankers (TNK) may be sensitive, because tension raises the importance of freight rates, insurance and routes. For LNG, the key is to watch the gap between European TTF and Asian JKM: if Asia outbids Europe, the cost of rerouting cargoes rises and Europe may again feel price pressure.

Technology has a dual exposure. Chipmakers such as NVIDIA (NVDA), AMD (AMD), Broadcom (AVGO), TSMC (TSM) and ASML (ASML) react to trade rules, export licenses and demand for AI infrastructure. Hyperscalers such as Microsoft (MSFT), Alphabet (GOOGL), Amazon (AMZN) and Meta Platforms (META) are working out whether AI revenue growth will justify capital expenditure, chips and energy.

With news like this, it makes sense to separate three layers: 1) diplomacy — is there a written schedule of tariffs, licenses or purchases, or just optimistic rhetoric; 2) energy — are Brent, time spreads, tanker rates, insurance and the TTF-versus-JKM gap moving; 3) the AI economy — is capital expenditure growing faster than operating cash flow, and are companies documenting concrete AI revenue, or just saying AI more often on earnings calls. The biggest risk is mistaking a political headline for finished cash flow.

The Strait of Hormuz is like the only bridge from a big housing estate to the city center. When someone says the bridge might be closed, even people who are not driving across it today start worrying about traffic jams, detours and costlier transport. On the market, that means oil, LNG, airfares, freight and company costs can get more expensive before anything physically happens — because the market starts paying extra for the risk.

With energy news the key is to distinguish oil, refining and diesel: the same geopolitical event can help refiners, hurt carriers and make everyday goods more expensive for consumers.

In this report diesel behaves not like a fuel but like a diplomat with a jerry can in hand. According to the excerpt, Donald Trump wants to pressure Volodymyr Zelensky into having Kyiv agree to an energy ceasefire with Moscow, while also claiming that because of the war Russia has "lost control" of its oil business. According to the report, the US retail price of diesel rose above 6.50 USD per gallon for the first time, with fuel supplies disrupted both by the war in Ukraine and by the conflict with Iran.

The most interesting thing isn't oil itself. It's diesel — the economy's dull workhorse. Gasoline is the voter's emotion at the pump, but diesel is globalization's accounting pencil: trucks, ships, agriculture, mining, construction. When diesel gets more expensive, it isn't just about "pricier fill-ups" but about making the movement of things more expensive.

Trump's phrasing about Russia's "loss of control" may be less an energy diagnosis and more a negotiating lever. In practice the market needn't care whether the sentence is politically accurate; it cares whether pressure is building to change fuel flows. With oil people often talk about barrels. With diesel the refining bottleneck matters more: whether crude can be turned into the right fuel at the right place in time.

A memorable framework: oil is flour, diesel is the finished bread roll. When the problem is in the bakery, it isn't enough to say "there's plenty of flour." If the bakeries can't keep up, rolls get more expensive. And the economy is hungry in the morning.

Mini-math: an extra 1 USD per gallon means 42 USD per barrel of product, because a barrel holds 42 gallons. That's a huge difference for segments that buy fuel every day, but not always equally good for refiners — what matters is whether the gap between the input price and the output price widens, not just the price tag at the pump.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

Watch out for a trap: higher revenue isn't the same as higher free cash flow. At a refiner, rising prices can inflate working capital. Illustration: if 2 million barrels of inventory require 20 million USD more cash when prices rise by 10 USD/barrel, that can temporarily eat up even an improvement in EBITDA. The warning sign is a situation where EBITDA grows but operating cash flow lags because of inventories and receivables.

With reports like this it isn't enough to read "oil up/down." A better framework is to split the chain into four floors: production, refining, transport, end consumer. Each floor has different economics. The producer sells the barrel, the refiner earns on the price spread, the carrier deals with the fuel bill, and the household pays indirectly in the price of goods.

The biggest mistake is to throw "energy" into one sack. An energy ceasefire could reduce part of the risk premium, but if the problem remains in refining capacity or trade routes, diesel can stay expensive even with calmer oil.

Picture an "energy ceasefire" as an agreement between neighbours that they'll stop shutting off each other's water. But even if they stop arguing, the pipes can still be clogged. For the market this means: fuel prices may fall if the fear of outages eases, but they needn't fall right away if the problem is in refineries, transport or inventories. For an ordinary person's wallet, diesel matters even without a diesel car in the garage — it hauls food, parcels and building materials, so its price can hide in receipts all around you.

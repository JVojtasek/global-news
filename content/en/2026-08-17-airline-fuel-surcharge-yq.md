## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** ANA published its fuel surcharge for tickets issued between 1 September and 31 October 2026: 55,000 yen one way to Europe and North America, down from a record 65,000. In Japan the figure is filed and needs government approval.

**What it means.** That charge is not a tax. It is airline revenue with its own ticket code, YQ or YR, sitting in the part of the receipt most passengers read as government money.

**Risks and impact.** Delta publishes a ceiling of up to 650 dollars each way for carrier-imposed surcharges. On a miles booking, the fare is what your miles cover and this box is what your card pays.

**What can be done.** Open the fare breakdown before you pay and read the surcharge line separately from the tax lines. In the European Union that breakdown is compulsory.

**What to watch.** A United States proposal from 1 July 2026 would let airlines display those component lines as prominently as the total price.

## FACTS

Japan is the outlier. Airlines that levy a fuel surcharge on international tickets sold there file it with the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism, and publish the arithmetic behind it.

JAL prints the whole ladder. Take the two-month average price of Singapore kerosene, multiply by the average yen-dollar rate for the same two months, and read off the resulting yen-per-barrel figure against 23 lettered bands. Below 6,000 yen a barrel, the surcharge is zero. The band decides the amount, and it is fixed for the following two months.

You can watch it work. For tickets issued in February and March 2026, JAL cited kerosene at 89.86 dollars a barrel across October and November 2025 at 153.01 yen: 13,750 yen a barrel, band H, 29,000 yen one way to North America or Europe.

Five months later the same sum gave a different answer. Kerosene averaged 178.21 dollars a barrel across April and May 2026 at 158.85 yen, or 28,308 yen a barrel. That is band W, which is 74,000 yen. Airlines charged band T instead, 65,000 yen, because the Japanese government subsidised the difference as an emergency measure tied to events in the Middle East.

Then fuel fell. ANA's table, updated on 14 August 2026, shows 55,000 yen for tickets issued in September and October, again reduced by the subsidy. Outside Japan the same sector is 352 dollars, 308 euros or 265 pounds.

## EVIDENCE

Two things here are documented rather than argued.

The Japanese formula is public, and so is every input. The band tables, kerosene averages, exchange rates and resulting yen figures all appear on the carriers' own sites, and each announcement adds that the level is subject to government approval.

The charge is legally distinct from tax everywhere it appears. European Union law has required since 2008 that the final price be broken into the fare, taxes, airport charges, and other charges or surcharges such as those for security or fuel. In 2017 the Court of Justice held that carriers must specify those amounts separately and may not fold them, even partly, into the fare. Delta's own published schedule lists carrier-imposed surcharges under codes YQ and YR, up to 650 dollars each way.

One thing cannot be checked at all, which is the point. No large carrier outside Japan publishes a formula linking its surcharge to a fuel price. So there is no public way to test whether a given YQ tracks fuel, moves with demand, or simply stays where it was last put. Trade explanations circulate about how the split affects agency commissions and revenue sharing between airlines; we could not verify them from public documents, so they are not here.

## PERSPECTIVES

**It is an honest unbundling.** Fuel is volatile, the argument runs, and a separate line lets a carrier pass through a cost it does not control. That explains the surcharge's birth. It explains its persistence less well: a pass-through that only ever needed to move with fuel would have a published formula, and outside Japan it does not.

**It keeps the headline number down.** A search result shows a fare. The surcharge appears later, in a box shared with airport charges and government taxes. Nobody has to say the word tax for the box to do that work. What this understates is that the total, in Europe and the United States alike, must still be the whole price before you pay.

**Disclosure is the fix.** The European Union has mandated the four-line breakdown for eighteen years and won a court ruling to enforce it. The surcharge is still there. Disclosure changed what you can see, not what you are charged — worth knowing before expecting much from the next transparency rule.

**The last time anyone audited it, they found coordination.** Between August 2004 and January 2006, according to the United Kingdom competition authority, British Airways and Virgin Atlantic coordinated long-haul surcharge pricing by exchanging commercially sensitive information. Virgin reported it and was not fined, under the leniency policy. British Airways paid 58.5 million pounds in April 2012. One episode, twenty years old, in one market — and the only time the mechanism has been examined from the inside.

## CONTEXT

The surcharge is younger than most passengers assume. The United States Department of Justice, announcing British Airways' guilty plea and 300 million dollar fine in August 2007, put its growth plainly: the round-trip passenger fuel surcharge was around 10 dollars a ticket in 2004 and nearly 110 dollars by 2006. Oil rose over that period. So did the number of airlines charging for it.

Two decades on, the rules govern presentation, not amount. Both regimes say how the components must be shown. Neither says a charge called a fuel surcharge must bear any relation to fuel.

The American rules are moving in both directions at once. On 2 July 2026 the Department of Transportation issued a final rule restoring the 2011 text of its full-fare standard. The day before, it proposed something larger: letting airlines show the component charges as prominently as the total rather than smaller, and rescinding nine older guidance documents. Comments closed on 31 July 2026.

One of the nine is worth naming. Guidance issued on 17 May 2012 tells carriers that award travel cannot be advertised as free if the passenger must pay mandatory charges, and that those charges must appear as prominently as the mileage price. Its own example, New York to London, put government fees and taxes at 216 dollars, more than a fifth of an economy ticket.

## DEEPER

A price is a demand. An explanation is a story. The receipt on an airline ticket is unusual because it hands you both, and the story is doing more work than the numbers.

Consider what the word fuel accomplishes. It names a cost the airline does not set, cannot refuse and did not want. It moves the blame outward, to refineries and wars and currency markets, and it does this in a single syllable next to a figure. Nobody argues with weather.

The trouble comes when the story stops matching the number. A line item created for a reason acquires a second life once the reason recedes, because withdrawing it is a confession. Raise a fare and you are a business. Remove a fuel surcharge and you have admitted it was, for some interval, not really about fuel. There is no press release for that.

Which is why the Japanese case is interesting, and not because Japanese carriers are more scrupulous. They publish because a ministry makes them, and once the arithmetic is public it runs both ways. Twenty-nine thousand yen in February, sixty-five thousand in July, fifty-five thousand in September. A number that can rise in public can also fall in public, and the second half never happens on its own.

There is a complication in that story too. In July the formula said 74,000 yen and passengers paid 65,000, because the state covered the gap. Even the transparent version was political as well as arithmetic. Making a price legible does not make it neutral. It only means that when someone decides, you can see who.

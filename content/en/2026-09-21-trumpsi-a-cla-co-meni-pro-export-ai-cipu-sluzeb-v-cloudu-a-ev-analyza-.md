With AI and EV geopolitics, it is not enough to read the headline about a deal; the key is whether the rules reach chip performance, licences, servicing, cloud computing, and above all corporate cash flow via capex, inventories and margins.

This is not a diplomatic dinner, it is a technical fitness check of two superpowers. According to the excerpt, Donald Trump and Xi Jinping are set to meet in Washington before the looming end of the trade truce. On the table are mainly AI, export rules for semiconductors, and the possibility that Chinese electric cars would be built directly in the United States.

It is not just about tariffs. The real question is: who gets to make intelligence, and where? A semiconductor is not an ordinary component; it is a passport to the world of AI. An electric vehicle is not just a car; it is a computer on wheels, carrying a battery, software and data.

An unexpected parallel: in the 1980s the United States feared Japanese cars. The result was not that Japanese competition disappeared, but that it started building factories in America. The battle shifted from the ports to the dealerships. If a similar model emerged with Chinese EVs, it would not end the tension, only change the field: less tariff war, more competition over margins, supply chains, and politically palatable jobs.

The important point is that export restrictions are not a single switch on a wall. They are more like a cockpit dashboard: one knob caps the performance of AI chips, another demands a licence for a specific customer, another bans sales of manufacturing equipment for advanced chips, and a fourth can restrict servicing, spare parts and software. Then there is a separate drawer: access to compute capacity via the cloud. A firm does not need to physically buy a chip to rent its performance in a data centre outside China. That is why the market is not just asking whether tariffs will be higher or lower, but whether the rules will land on direct chip sales, licences, machine servicing, or on compute power as a service.

The mechanics in numbers, purely as a model and not as an estimate for any specific firm: free cash flow, meaning the cash left after investment, can be simplified as EBITDA minus capex minus the growth in working capital. A reality anchor: top-tier chip designers tend to run high operating margins thanks to software and intellectual property, whereas carmakers and new EV factories operate with much lower margins and heavy capex. That is why the same political move hurts everyone differently.

The point: the headline talks about diplomacy, but the earnings statement will be settled by accounting. The biggest impact is often not in revenue, but in inventories, capex, and whether margins can absorb politics.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

A plus can go to firms tied to US factory construction, automation, and electrical infrastructure: industrial equipment such as Caterpillar (CAT), automation such as Rockwell Automation (ROK), electrification and distribution such as Eaton (ETN). If local production of Chinese EVs really gains political space, the construction and grid ecosystem wins too, not necessarily just the carmakers.

A mixed impact lands on chipmakers such as NVIDIA (NVDA), AMD (AMD) and Broadcom (AVGO). A softer tone would support AI demand, while tougher export rules would curb part of their foreign sales. The difference is between flagship AI accelerators, weaker tweaked versions for regulated markets, and selling compute via the cloud. For chip-equipment suppliers such as Applied Materials (AMAT), Lam Research (LRCX), KLA (KLAC), and ASML (ASML), the difference is between new machines and servicing: machines are more cyclical, servicing tends to be more resilient, but a ban on spare parts or software updates would be a harsher hit than merely slowing new orders.

Pressure could land on carmakers selling in the US, such as Tesla (TSLA), General Motors (GM) and Ford (F), if locally built Chinese EVs raised price competition. For batteries and materials, for example Albemarle (ALB), the impact is double-edged: higher volumes help, a price war and overcapacity hurt. For utilities and energy, it depends on the model: regulated utilities such as American Electric Power (AEP) need approved rate-base growth, meaning assets recognised by the regulator; merchant power producers such as Vistra (VST) are more sensitive to market electricity prices.

With stories like this, the biggest trap is treating the meeting as a binary event: a deal equals a rally, a quarrel equals a sell-off. A better frame has three layers: 1) export and licensing rules, meaning whether they target chip performance, the end customer, servicing, or cloud compute, 2) production localisation and capex, 3) working capital, mainly inventories and receivables. The warning indicator is not just weaker revenue growth; worse is the combination of rising inventories, large investment, and a declining margin.

Chip export rules are like a bouncer at a club where AI is playing. Some get in, some only into the cheaper room, some are allowed to buy a ticket only after approval, and some are forbidden from borrowing the VIP lounge even through a friend. For the market, that means even a great firm can have a temporarily smaller addressable market. For stocks, it raises volatility. For an ordinary wallet it can show up indirectly: in more expensive technology, slower innovation, or conversely in new jobs, if production moves closer to the customer.

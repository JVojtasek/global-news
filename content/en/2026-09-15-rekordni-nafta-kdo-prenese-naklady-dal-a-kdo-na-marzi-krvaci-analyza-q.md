With record diesel prices, watch above all who can pass the cost on; the rise in fuel prices alone doesn't yet tell you who in the market wins and whose margins bleed.

Diesel is economic caffeine: when it gets pricier, trucks, farms and warehouses don't sleep — only margins develop a nervous twitch. The average price of US diesel jumped to a new record of 6.27 USD per gallon. The market read it as more pressure on inflation and therefore as a reason why the Fed may keep a tighter course on rates.

This isn't just about "expensive oil." Diesel is special because it isn't primarily the fuel for a weekend trip, but the price of moving the physical world: food, parcels, building materials, grain, spare parts. Gasoline hurts voters at the pump; diesel hurts almost every price tag in the store.

The counterintuitive point: record diesel can be worse for stocks than a rise in oil alone, because it creates a double squeeze. First, it raises costs for companies that have to haul or make something. Second, it can push the Fed toward tougher rates, which lowers the valuations of stocks sensitive to future profits. But the Fed can't produce a gallon of diesel — it can only cool demand. It's a bit like dealing with an overheated kitchen by turning off the heating in the whole house. It helps the temperature, but the guests at the table start grumbling.

A mini calculation: a truck averaging around 6.5 miles per gallon at a price of 6.27 USD pays roughly 0.96 USD per mile for fuel. If diesel cost 5 USD, it would be about 0.77 USD per mile. The difference is almost 20 cents per mile; on a 1,000-mile route, that's roughly 200 USD extra for fuel alone. And somebody ultimately has to swallow it: the carrier, the customer, or the consumer.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It helps refiners in particular, if they can buy inputs and sell finished products at a higher margin. Examples are Valero Energy (VLO), Marathon Petroleum (MPC) or Phillips 66 (PSX). For them, watch above all refining margins, processing volumes, outages and the share of distillates, meaning fuels such as diesel and heating oil.

Integrated energy companies such as Exxon Mobil (XOM) and Chevron (CVX) may also look relatively better, but the mechanism is different: part of the profit comes from oil production, part from refining. Not every "energy" company earns the same way; an oil producer and a refiner aren't the same animal, even if both smell of the gas station.

It hurts transportation and logistics: FedEx (FDX), UPS (UPS), J.B. Hunt Transport Services (JBHT) or Old Dominion Freight Line (ODFL) are working out how much of the cost they can pass on through fuel surcharges and how much will eat their operating margin. Retailers such as Walmart (WMT), Target (TGT) or Amazon (AMZN) feel it through pricier shipping of goods and pressure on prices. Rate-sensitive companies — growth tech stocks or the real estate sector — suffer indirectly too, because more expensive money raises the bar for valuing future profits. Bonds typically don't fare well when the market expects higher rates: prices of existing bonds tend to fall, because newly issued paper has to offer a more attractive yield.

With news like this, it isn't enough to look at the "record price" headline. A better checklist: 1) is the problem in oil, or in refining capacity? 2) are companies' fuel costs rising faster than revenue? 3) do they have fuel surcharges, or do they carry the cost themselves? 4) does management mention weaker demand due to transport prices on the analyst call? 5) for refiners, look in quarterly results for throughput, crack spread, planned outages and inventories; for carriers, fuel expense, operating ratio and fuel surcharge revenue in the MD&A or segment tables.

The risk: a record commodity price often looks like a simple "energy up" story, but the market usually punishes those who lack the ability to pass the cost on. The difference between winner and loser isn't who uses diesel, but who can invoice it to someone else.

A fuel surcharge is like when you order a pizza with a friend and the courier says: "Gas is expensive, delivery costs more." The transport company is trying to send part of the bill to the customer. The impact? If the customer pays extra, the carrier's margin survives better. If not, the company earns less — and when that happens across the economy, goods get more expensive, the Fed gets more nervous, and the shares of companies with little ability to raise prices tend to come under pressure.

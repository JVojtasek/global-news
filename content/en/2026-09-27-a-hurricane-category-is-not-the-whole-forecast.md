## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Hurricane Polo was a Category 3 storm at 18:00 UTC on September 27, about 460 kilometres west-southwest of Cabo San Lucas. The US National Hurricane Center said it was approaching Baja California Sur and expected to cross the peninsula on Monday night.

**What it means.** A falling category does not make the approaching storm harmless. The Saffir-Simpson number describes maximum sustained wind; it does not combine rain, storm surge, destructive waves or the way steep ground concentrates runoff.

**Risks and impact.** Hurricane warnings cover stretches of both coasts. Authorities have suspended school and government work for Monday and Tuesday, stopped maritime activity and prepared temporary shelters.

**What can be done.** The useful public signal is the local warning and forecast hazard, not the category alone. People in affected areas should follow the instructions of Mexican civil-protection and meteorological authorities.

**What to watch.** Through Tuesday, watch where the centre crosses, how much rain falls and whether warnings expand on the mainland side of the Gulf of California.

That made it a Category 3 hurricane. The centre forecast a turn north and then northeast, an approach to Baja California Sur on Monday, a crossing Monday night and an approach to northwestern mainland Mexico early Tuesday.

The advisory put hurricane warnings on parts of the peninsula's west and east coasts, with additional hurricane and tropical-storm watches extending north and across part of mainland Mexico. It forecast 100 to 200 millimetres of rain across portions of Baja California Sur, with isolated totals around 300 millimetres. It also warned of significant coastal flooding where winds push water onshore, accompanied by large and destructive waves.

Reuters independently reported the approach and preparations, including evacuations, port closures, school cancellations and beach restrictions. Baja California Sur's government said maritime activity was suspended, schools and public offices would be closed Monday and Tuesday, and 169 temporary-shelter spaces could be activated according to conditions.

The primary record is the National Hurricane Center advisory, which gives the measured position, aircraft-observed intensity, warning areas and hazard forecast. The sources do not make the future certain. The National Hurricane Center said fluctuations in strength were possible and forecast Polo to remain a strong hurricane when it reached the peninsula. Reuters cited a private forecast that expected Category 1 strength at landfall. Those statements are not interchangeable: they reflect different forecast moments or models, and intensity can change before the centre crosses the coast.

The stable conclusion is therefore about exposure, not a precise category at landfall. A powerful cyclone is approaching a populated peninsula under hurricane warnings, and several hazards extend beyond the eye and beyond the wind number.

## CONTEXT

The Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale groups storms by their maximum sustained wind. That makes the scale useful for communicating potential wind damage, but it deliberately excludes rainfall, storm surge and tornadoes. A compact number is easy to repeat; the full forecast is harder because it is a bundle of maps, time windows and probabilities.

That difference matters especially on a long, narrow peninsula. Polo can approach one coast, cross elevated terrain and then expose communities along the Gulf of California or mainland Mexico to a changed wind field. Rain falling on steep ground can move downhill long after the strongest wind at the coast has passed. Waves can arrive before the centre and remain dangerous after it moves away.

Warnings also begin before landfall because the relevant deadline is not the moment the eye crosses a map. The National Hurricane Center typically issues a hurricane warning about 36 hours before tropical-storm-force winds are expected, since those winds can make outdoor preparation difficult or dangerous. That is why public measures may look early compared with a landfall headline.

## DEEPER

Risk communication often compresses several questions into one label. “Category 3” answers how fast the strongest sustained winds are. It does not answer how much rain a particular valley will receive, whether water will be pushed onto a specific shore, how long power may be interrupted or whether a road crosses a flood channel.

The same problem appears outside weather. A credit rating does not describe every way a company can fail; an average temperature does not show who faces the hottest hour; a single benchmark does not capture every capability of an AI system. A summary measure helps only when its job description remains visible.

Polo offers a practical reading habit: pair the headline number with the hazard that matters locally. Wind category belongs beside warning boundaries. Rain totals belong beside terrain. Surge belongs beside coastline and wind direction. Timing belongs beside the moment when travel or outdoor work becomes unsafe. The fuller picture is less tidy, but it is closer to the decision authorities and households actually face.

## IMPACT

For residents and visitors in Baja California Sur, effects may include closed ports and beaches, cancelled classes, disrupted travel, power loss, flooding and dangerous surf. These will not be uniform across the peninsula, and a weaker centre can still leave severe local damage where rain or water concentrates.

The practical takeaway is to use the latest official local warning as the action signal. Category changes are context; warning areas, expected arrival times and civil-protection instructions define where and when precautions apply.

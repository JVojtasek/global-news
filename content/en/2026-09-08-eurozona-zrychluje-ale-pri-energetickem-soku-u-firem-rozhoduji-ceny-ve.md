Stronger GDP is not enough on its own: in an energy shock, the key is to watch whether companies' costs are rising faster than their ability to pass them into prices.

European GDP now looks like a runner who sped up just before a hill — a nice split time, but the course is just starting to climb. According to an excerpt from report id=360371, the eurozone economy grew faster in the quarter to June than previously estimated. The same excerpt, however, adds a conditional warning: the bloc's resilience may be tested if the war with Iran continues to block transit through the Strait of Hormuz.

This report is not just about growth being good and expensive energy being bad. More interesting is that both can coexist in the short term — and that is exactly what tends to confuse the market.

GDP is the rear-view mirror. It shows how far the economy has already travelled. Hormuz, by contrast, is the windscreen: it shows what the economy may run into. If the energy shock drags on, the eurozone faces not just higher inflation, but a so-called terms-of-trade shock — a deterioration in the terms of trade. In plain words: Europe has to pay for imported energy with a larger share of its income, corporate profits and spending power. This is not a classic demand party, where prices rise because everyone is spending as if it were payday. It is more like a heating bill that lands on the table in the middle of a lively dinner.

More useful than guessing whether "growth" is stronger than "oil" is to watch the margin sandwich: on top, energy input costs; in the middle, companies' ability to raise prices; at the bottom, the final margin. When only the top slice gets more expensive and the customer refuses to pay a higher price for the menu, profit flattens faster than toast in an office toaster.

The counterintuitive point: better GDP can be a double-edged sword for markets. On one hand, it reduces fear of recession. On the other, it can make it harder for the central bank to ease monetary policy quickly if energy is pushing up inflationary pressure. The result is not a simple "growth = hooray", but a mix: the real economy is still holding up, company margins may take a hit and the market starts repricing who has energy as an input and who has it as a product.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The plus side is intuitive: oil and gas producers such as Shell (SHEL), TotalEnergies (TTE) or Equinor (EQNR) may benefit from higher energy prices if the tension feeds into oil, gas or refining margins. For LPG, the impact is less straightforward: carriers and players tied to liquefied petroleum gases, for example Dorian LPG (LPG) or BW LPG (BWLPG), may see higher freight rates and rerouted flows, but at the same time carry the risk of more expensive fuel and operational complications.

The minus side: airlines such as Lufthansa (LHA.DE), Air France-KLM (AF.PA) or IAG (IAG.L) react sensitively to fuel, because kerosene is a large cost item and not everything can be passed into ticket prices right away. Chemicals and energy-intensive industry, for example BASF (BAS.DE), watch gas and electricity prices as pressure on margins. Utilities such as RWE (RWE.DE), Enel (ENEL.MI) or Iberdrola (IBE.MC) are a mixed case: in some places higher wholesale prices help, elsewhere regulation and political pressure hold back the passing on of costs.

With news like this, reading only the GDP headline is not enough. A better framework is a four-point checklist: 1) is the energy move a one-day spike, or does it last weeks, 2) does it feed into oil, gas and electricity, or just one market, 3) are companies talking about squeezed margins, or about the ability to raise prices, 4) does the central bank treat it as a temporary shock, or as a broader inflation problem. The biggest trap is confusing resilience with immunity: the economy can grow and yet the profitability of some sectors can deteriorate.

A terms-of-trade shock is like running a pizzeria where flour, cheese and electricity all suddenly get more expensive because of a problem somewhere far away. You are still selling pizza, so revenue may look decent, but you keep less from each pizza. For the market, this means good GDP need not automatically mean good company profits; for ordinary people, it may mean more expensive energy, transport and some goods in shops.

Read a higher share of stocks at family offices only after checking the size of the change, the sample methodology and whether it is a real flow of capital or just a mechanical shift in weights after assets were revalued.

When a billionaire's piggy bank starts smelling more of the stock exchange than of concrete, it is not just optimism — often it is a clean-up in a cellar where the light has finally been switched on. According to the CNBC Family Office Portfolio Tracker, family offices increased their share of stocks in the second quarter. At the same time they cut back on real estate and private investments, while private credit funds marked part of their assets down.

An important brake: from this information we know the direction of the change, but without published percentage points, sample details and methodology we cannot automatically say how large a flow of money actually took place. A higher weight of stocks in a portfolio can also arise mechanically — stocks get more expensive, illiquid assets are revalued lower, and the percentages move without a family office dramatically "buying the market".

At first glance it sounds like classic risk-on: the rich are buying stocks, the market has a green light. But the more interesting reading is different: it is not necessarily about more courage, but about a greater hunger for price clarity.

The CNBC Family Office Portfolio Tracker is useful as a data probe into the behaviour of some family offices, not as a complete census of the whole market. Family offices are also extremely diverse: one may be a global investment machine with a team of analysts, another more of a discreet family wealth manager with a few large holdings. That is why it is key to read the tracker as an indicator of direction within the tracked sample, not as an exact map of all the world's wealthy families.

Stocks are uncomfortably honest. Every day they tell you on the screen what you are worth. Real estate funds, private equity and private credit are more polite guests: prices often arrive with a delay, via models and estimates. But when write-downs start appearing in private credit, the elegance disappears. Suddenly it turns out that part of the portfolio was not a stable rock, but a sofa with a blanket thrown over a stain.

That is the essence: a rotation into stocks may be less a bet on endless growth and more a move from opaque assets to liquid assets. Liquidity means an asset can be sold relatively quickly at the market price. For family offices this matters because they manage long-term capital, but at the same time they do not want to find out only six months later that their private investment was valued too optimistically.

A historical parallel? After periods when illiquid assets are revalued, the market often first punishes not risk as such, but the uncertainty around valuation. In other words: investors may not mind an expensive asset. What they mind is an asset where nobody really knows whether it is expensive, cheap, or just nicely painted in Excel.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The plus side belongs mainly to publicly traded stocks and the infrastructure around them. If capital is moving into liquid markets, large and easily tradable stocks can benefit, for example tech leaders such as Microsoft (MSFT), NVIDIA (NVDA), Apple (AAPL) or Alphabet (GOOGL) — not as universal tips, but as typical examples of stocks that big money can enter and exit quickly.

Secondary beneficiaries may be asset managers and exchange infrastructure: BlackRock (BLK) thanks to public funds, Intercontinental Exchange (ICE) or CME Group (CME) thanks to market activity. The mechanism is simple: more allocation to public markets means more trading, more portfolio management and a greater emphasis on products with a daily price.

Real estate and illiquid private markets are in a weaker position. In real estate, commercial property managers such as CBRE Group (CBRE) or Jones Lang LaSalle (JLL) may come under pressure, as may real estate funds and REIT segments such as offices or warehouses, for example Prologis (PLD) as an illustrative name in logistics property. For alternative managers such as Blackstone (BX), KKR (KKR), Apollo Global Management (APO) or Ares Management (ARES), the impact is mixed: in the long run they know how to make money in private markets, but in the short term write-downs and more cautious clients complicate fundraising and sentiment around valuations.

With news like this, it is good not just to ask: Are the rich optimistic? A better question is: What are they moving away from?

- Is this buying of stocks, or just a fall in the value of illiquid assets that changed the percentages in the portfolio?

- Are the changes given in percentage points, or only verbally as "more" and "less"?

- Do we know how large the tracker's sample is, which family offices it captures and whether it is a representative picture of the broader market, or just a readily observable part of it?

- Is the broad market being bought, or mainly a few mega-cap stocks?

- Are the write-downs in private credit isolated, or are they recurring across funds?

- Are stock fundamentals, that is, profits and margins, improving, or is capital simply flowing into the more liquid part of the market?

- In real estate, watch occupancy, debt refinancing and valuation rates; that is often where it becomes clear whether it was temporary pain or a change of regime.

A private credit markdown means a fund admits that a loan or a bundle of loans is worth less than it previously appeared. Imagine you have a collection of expensive wines at home and for years you have told yourself that each bottle is worth a fortune. Then an expert comes, tastes two, chokes and says: this will not be Bordeaux, more like expensive vinegar. The impact? Investors start trusting assets with a daily price tag, that is, stocks, more, and things whose price is revealed only in an unpleasant conversation, less. For an ordinary wallet this can mean greater swings on the stock market and more cautious financing of real estate or riskier companies.

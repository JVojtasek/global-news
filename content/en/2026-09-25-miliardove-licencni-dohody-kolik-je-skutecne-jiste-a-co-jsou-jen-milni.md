With billion-dollar licensing deals, the key is to separate guaranteed upfront cash from conditional milestones. That is often where the gap between the headline and reality hides.

When the market starts yawning after a presentation, a pharmaceutical company sometimes does not pull out a new slide. It pulls out a chequebook.

Novo Nordisk has signed a global licensing deal with Sweden's BioArctic AB (BIOA B.ST) for exidavnemab, an antibody against aggregated alpha-synuclein for Parkinson's disease, which has completed phase 1 and is being prepared for phase 2. The deal is worth up to USD 1.2 billion: USD 100 million is an immediate upfront payment, and up to USD 1.1 billion comes as development, regulatory and commercial milestones. There are also meant to be tiered royalties, but the percentages were not quantified in the published terms.

This is not just a biotech fairy tale about a small company landing a big partner. Above all, it is a repair job on the Novo Nordisk (NVO; NOVO-B.CO) story after a capital markets day that did not give investors a clear enough answer to the question: what comes after the GLP-1 euphoria?

The market often reads licensing deals as a sudden discovery of gold. A more accurate picture is different: Novo has not bought a finished mine, but the right to dig on someone else's land, and it has paid a deposit on the shovel. What is truly certain is USD 100 million. The rest of the billion-dollar headline unlocks only if the programme makes it through the clinic, the regulators and possibly the market. That is the crucial difference between money in the bank and money on an advertising billboard.

An unexpected angle: for BioArctic, USD 100 million is a far bigger strategic event than it is for Novo. For a small biotech, an upfront payment can improve its negotiating position, the funding of development and the credibility of its pipeline. For Novo, it is more a cheaper way to buy an optional future beyond obesity and diabetes, especially in neurodegeneration, where one success can change how the whole company is seen, but most attempts have historically ended in an expensive lesson in humility.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

A plus for BioArctic AB (BIOA B.ST): concrete cash of USD 100 million, validation of exidavnemab and a stronger partner for the next clinical phase. That is why it makes sense that the shares reacted sharply; a small company is more sensitive to such a change in its story than a pharmaceutical giant.

A mild plus for Novo Nordisk (NVO; NOVO-B.CO): the company is showing that it does not want to be merely a synonym for GLP-1 drugs for diabetes and obesity. In the short term, though, this deal does nothing for revenue from Wegovy or Ozempic; a phase 2 Parkinson's programme is a long way from possible commercialisation.

The read-across for neurodegeneration specialists such as BioArctic itself, Eisai (4523.T; ESALY) or Biogen (BIIB) is positive for sentiment: big pharma is once again paying for risky brain programmes. Conversely, it puts no direct pressure on obesity drug makers such as Eli Lilly (LLY); the deal is more an extension of the map than an attack on their core business.

With news like this, look at three lines: how much is upfront cash, how much is conditional milestones, and what clinical phase the asset is in. A billion-dollar headline for a programme that is ready for phase 2 is often more of an option contract than an almost certain future income.

Milestone payments are like telling a painter: here is a deposit for painting the kitchen, and you get the rest once you finish the flat, it passes inspection and the visitors are delighted. For the market this means: a small company's shares can jump on the deposit and a big partner's trust alone, but an ordinary investor should not treat the whole billion-dollar package as cash in hand. The effect reaches people's wallets only indirectly, through share values, confidence in the sector and, over the very long term, the chance of a new medicine.

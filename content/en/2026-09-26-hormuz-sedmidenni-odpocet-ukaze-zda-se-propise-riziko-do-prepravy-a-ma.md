With Hormuz it is not enough to watch the headline about a deal. The key is whether physical flows resume and whether the risk premium actually feeds into shipping, insurance and company margins.

Imagine someone locking the only exit from a giant car park full of tankers, and then offering to perhaps return the key within a week.

According to the report, Iran is offering the US a deal that would allow the Strait of Hormuz to reopen within seven days. A US official told the BBC that constructive talks are taking place through intermediaries. For now this matters mainly as a hint of de-escalation, not as a finished return to normal.

At Hormuz the market is not pricing only oil. It is pricing insurance against ordinary traffic turning into geopolitical roulette.

The biggest mistake is to think: the strait opens, oil simply falls, done. Hormuz is a narrow neck for a large part of oil and LNG exports from the Persian Gulf; it is not an ordinary road where you can bypass a queue on a side lane. When a chance of reopening within seven days appears, part of the crisis premium disappears, but not the whole risk. Why? Because the tanker, the insurer, the refinery and the trader are all watching different clocks.

The tanker is concerned with the safety of the route today. The insurer is concerned with the war-risk surcharge on the next voyage. The refinery is concerned with what it already paid for its inventories. The trader is concerned with whether the deal falls apart before the ship gets through. Seven days is a political calendar, not a logistical magic trick.

In accounting terms this is beautifully counterintuitive: for producers, a change in price feeds quickly into revenue and operating cash flow, because they sell the commodity. For refiners, the same event shows up in the cost of crude input, the value of inventories and above all in the gap between product prices and crude, that is, the refining margin. That is why one piece of geopolitical news can be negative for part of upstream and positive for part of downstream at the same time. One barrel, two income statements, three headaches.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It may help refiners such as Valero Energy (VLO), Marathon Petroleum (MPC) or Phillips 66 (PSX) if the price of crude input falls faster than the prices of petrol, diesel and jet fuel. It is not automatic: the refining margin, inventories and price hedging decide.

Integrated giants such as Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), Shell (SHEL) or BP (BP) face a mixed effect. Their production arm may lose part of the geopolitical premium in the oil price, while their refining and trading divisions may benefit from better availability of crude and less stress in logistics.

It may hurt tanker companies such as Frontline (FRO) or Teekay Tankers (TNK) if war-risk surcharges, insurance costs and exceptionally tight shipping rates come down. Conversely, airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL), United Airlines (UAL) or American Airlines (AAL) may feel relief through fuel costs, but with a delay and depending on their fuel hedging.

With news like this, watch four things: 1) whether both sides confirm the deal, not only the intermediaries; 2) whether tanker transits and loadings actually resume; 3) whether shipping and war-risk insurance costs fall; 4) how companies explain margins, inventories, hedging and working capital in their results. The headline moves emotions, but the income statement will show who actually paid the bill.

The Strait of Hormuz is like the only narrow gate out of a giant festival. When it closes, everyone starts paying more: for security, for transport, for waiting and for their nerves. When someone promises to open the gate within a week, the panic eases, but nobody throws away the receipts straight away. For the market this means less fear of an oil price shock, and for shares different effects depending on who extracts, ships, processes or burns oil as a cost.

With airlines facing expensive fuel, watching oil is not enough: what decides is the jet fuel spread against Brent, the percentage and price of hedging, unhedged consumption, capacity cuts, yield per passenger and the debt calendar.

Imagine a restaurant that sold its Christmas menu in advance — and then found out that butter costs as much as champagne. That is exactly what part of the airline business looks like today: Ryanair is warning of unhedged jet fuel at up to 140 USD a barrel and is cutting winter capacity to limit the pain.

Ryanair cut winter capacity because part of its fuel consumption is not hedged against price rises, and jet fuel may, according to the company, cost up to 140 USD a barrel. This is not just an accounting nuisance: expensive fuel immediately feeds into decisions about how many flights are worth operating at all.

An important clarification: the 140 USD a barrel on its own is only an upper price anchor for the unhedged part. For the real impact on Ryanair, the key is to know three numbers, which have to be read directly from company commentary and reports: what share of winter consumption is hedged, at what price, and how large a part remains open.

An airline is not just a carrier. It is a factory of "badly timed bets": it often sells tickets in advance, but part of its costs — mainly fuel — can stay open until the moment of the flight. When fuel is cheap or hedged, the model works like a well-oiled conveyor belt. When it is not, every poorly filled winter flight becomes a financial sandwich with a razor blade inside.

The most interesting thing is not the price of fuel itself. What is interesting is the difference between jet fuel and Brent, i.e., put simply, "the surcharge for finished aviation fuel over crude oil". If, for example, Brent cost 90 USD and jet fuel 140 USD, the airline is dealing not just with expensive oil, but with a premium of 50 USD a barrel. If Brent were 120 USD, the same 140 USD for jet fuel means a completely different story: the pain is still there, but a smaller part of the problem lies in the refining premium.

At Ryanair, the capacity cut is telling precisely because the low-cost model rests on high aircraft utilisation and tight control of unit costs. Cutting capacity can therefore be a rational defence of margin, not just panic. In winter, demand is weaker, so the airline can remove the worst routes — typically those with lower load factors, weaker ticket prices or high consumption per seat. Fewer flights can paradoxically help keep a higher average price where people have to fly.

That is the counterintuitive lesson: expensive fuel does not automatically mean "all airlines down equally". What decides is a mix of three things: how much fuel is hedged, how strong demand is on specific routes, and whether the company can pass the cost into prices without driving passengers onto the train, into the car or onto the sofa.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The negative pressure is aimed mainly at carriers with lower fuel hedging and price-sensitive customers. Examples: low-cost airlines such as Ryanair (RYAAY), easyJet (EZJ.L) or Wizz Air (WIZZ.L), but also network carriers such as International Airlines Group (IAG.L), Lufthansa (LHA.DE) or Air France-KLM (AF.PA). With them, the market is mainly concerned with margin per seat, load factor and the ability to raise prices without losing volume.

Airports and infrastructure see a mixed effect: Aena (AENA.MC), Fraport (FRA.DE) or Vinci (DG.PA) may feel fewer aircraft movements and passengers, but if the cuts hit only weak winter routes, the effect need not be dramatic. Aircraft makers such as Airbus (AIR.PA) and Boeing (BA) are affected rather indirectly: expensive fuel increases the value of more fuel-efficient aircraft, but weaker airlines may postpone fleet growth.

On the opposite side stand energy and refiners. Producers and integrated companies such as Shell (SHEL), BP (BP) or TotalEnergies (TTE) can benefit from higher energy prices in general. For refiners, what matters is the difference between the price of oil and the price of products such as jet fuel — i.e. the margin for turning the raw material into fuel. Not every rise in the oil price is the same for them; the key is whether the premium for finished fuel is rising too.

With news like this, do not watch only the oil price. A better checklist: 1) the price of jet fuel against Brent crude — a widening of the difference by single dollars a barrel is noise, by tens of dollars it already changes unit costs; 2) the share of hedged fuel consumption — for Ryanair, the price of up to 140 USD needs to be supplemented with the hedging percentage, the hedge price and the period; 3) the open part of consumption times the difference against the hedged price — that is the practical bridge into margin; 4) capacity in available seat kilometres — cutting weak winter routes is a different story from a blanket reduction of the whole network; 5) the trend in average yield per passenger — if it grows faster than the fuel cost, the pressure is dampened; 6) debt and the refinancing calendar — expensive fuel plus expensive debt is a double bill for weaker carriers.

"Fuel hedging" is like a household fixing the price of electricity. When prices jump, the neighbour without a fixed price swears at the bill, while you are at peace — at least until the end of the contract. For airlines, this means two companies can fly the same route at the same ticket prices, but one makes money and the other bleeds, because it refuels at a worse price. For the market this matters: expensive fuel does not punish the whole industry evenly, but reveals who had the better financial umbrella before the storm.

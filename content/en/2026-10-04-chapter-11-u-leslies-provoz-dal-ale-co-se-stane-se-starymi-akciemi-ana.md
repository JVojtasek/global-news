In a Chapter 11, always separate the survival of the business from the fate of the old shares: what matters is the order of claims, DIP financing, the reorganisation plan and who gets the new value after the debt is rewritten.

Bankruptcy sometimes looks not like a locked door but like a "renovation in progress, open as usual" sign taped to the till. Leslie’s Inc. (LESL), a US retailer of pool chemicals and equipment, has filed for Chapter 11 protection, plans to close 76 stores, cut roughly $685 million of debt and keep operating in the meantime.

The essence: this is not a classic liquidation but a court-supervised slimming of the company. For shareholders and creditors it is a hard event; for customers, in the short term, it may look like nothing more than fewer branches on the map.

Leslie’s is not just a story about pools. It is a small barometer of America's "backyard economy" after the Covid boom: people back then upgraded homes, patios and pools, but higher financing costs and a more cautious consumer are turning a pool upgrade from "definitely this year" into "maybe next season".

There are so far mainly two hard numbers in this event: 76 stores closing and about $685 million of debt to be removed or rewritten in the reorganisation. And that is exactly the point: closing branches is an operational cut, while $685 million is a balance-sheet operation. Without the latest figures on revenue, same-store sales (sales in comparable stores), margins, EBITDA, interest costs, debt maturities and cash, it is impossible to say fairly how much of the pain comes from the market and how much from excessively heavy debt.

The counter-intuitive point: pool chemicals are partly a repeat purchase, yet a company can still end up in Chapter 11. The problem need not be that customers stopped buying chlorine. It may be the combination of expensive bricks-and-mortar branches, weaker big-ticket purchases such as pumps or equipment, and debt that behaves like a rucksack full of wet towels. You are still swimming, but every climb out of the water is agony.

And here is the investment-education point: with specialty retail, watching revenue is not enough. You need to distinguish three pockets of money: everyday consumption, deferrable upgrades and debt service. If the first pocket holds, but the second weakens and the third swells, the shares can suffer even while the shop is still selling.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The hardest impact falls on Leslie’s Inc. (LESL) as an equity story. Chapter 11 has its own waterfall of claims: first comes new financing during the bankruptcy, often called DIP financing, then secured creditors, then unsecured creditors, and existing shareholders stand at the very back by the door, where sometimes not even a towel is left. DIP financing is in effect an oxygen tank for the business: money for inventory, wages and day-to-day running, but it usually ranks very high in the order of repayment.

The plan to cut debt by roughly $685 million is positive for the chance that the business survives, but it does not automatically mean good news for the old equity structure. In a reorganisation plan, debt is often swapped for new shares, so original shareholders can be heavily diluted or wiped out entirely. That is precisely the difference between the sentence "the company carries on" and the sentence "the old share has value".

Pool equipment and chemical suppliers face a mixed impact. Companies such as Hayward Holdings (HAYW) or Pentair (PNR), tied to pool technology and water systems, may benefit in the short term from Leslie’s wanting to stay operationally open. At the same time, 76 closed stores mean fewer physical points of sale and potentially tougher negotiations over prices and payment terms.

The wholesale distributor Pool Corp (POOL) is an interesting point of comparison: if Leslie’s troubles reflect a weaker pool cycle, that is unpleasant for the whole ecosystem. If it is mainly about badly structured debt and branch network, stronger competitors and distributors may gain share locally.

Home improvement chains Home Depot (HD) and Lowe’s (LOW) may pick up some purchases such as pool chemicals or small accessories in areas where branches close. But this is not the core of their business, so the impact is likely to be marginal and local.

And GameStop (GME)? More of a sentiment warning light than an operational parallel. Both companies are linked to specialty bricks-and-mortar retail, but they sell completely different products and have different capital structures. The comparison makes sense only as a question: how does the market value shops that must defend their relevance against e-commerce and weaker footfall?

With news like this, it pays to ask not just "how many stores is it closing" but "what is the company closing: loss-making locations, or future growth?" Checklist: how much debt disappears, who gets the new shares, whether there is DIP financing, where it ranks in repayment, whether suppliers keep delivering, whether the problem concerns demand for the product or mainly the balance sheet, and whether closing branches strengthens margins or shrinks the brand's reach.

Chapter 11 is like a family that cannot manage its repayments but, instead of eviction, gets the chance to sit down at a table with the court and creditors: some obligations are rewritten, something is sold, spending is cut and life goes on. What matters is the seating order: the bank and the new rescuers eat first, the old shareholder waits last. For the market this means two things at once: the company may survive and keep serving customers, but the old shares may be badly damaged. For an ordinary person it may in practice mean fewer branches and perhaps a longer trip for pool chemicals, but not necessarily the immediate end of the brand.

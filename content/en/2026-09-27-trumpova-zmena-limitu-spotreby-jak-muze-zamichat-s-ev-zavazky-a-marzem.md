Looser rules may help carmakers, but the real effect will show only in the mix of cars sold, fines or credits, margins, inventories, capex and the use of EV capacity.

Carmakers have just been given political permission to drive more slowly. But careful: factories, battery contracts and customer habits do not turn like the steering wheel of a golf cart.

Trump said on Truth Social that he had approved new fuel economy standards meant to roll back the stricter rules of the Biden era. At the core are the US fleet standards known as CAFE, the rules for the average fuel consumption of newly sold cars by model year. This is not about cars already sitting in garages, but about what mix of vehicles a carmaker may sell in the coming years without regulatory costs.

The biggest mistake with news like this is to read it simply as "bad for EVs, good for gasoline." In reality it is mainly a change in the value of flexibility.

The mechanics are less exciting than the political headline but more important for the shares: a carmaker has an average fuel economy target for each fleet. If it misses it, the shortfall is multiplied by the number of vehicles sold and can end in a fine; if it beats the target, it generates credits that help cover weaker parts of the portfolio or have value for other players under the rules of the programme. That is why weaker rules do not just mean "fewer electric cars," but above all cheaper room to manoeuvre between pickups, SUVs, hybrids and EVs.

The car industry is not a phone app where you change the icon today and have a new product tomorrow. Platform development, supplier contracts, battery capacity and dealer inventory run on long tracks. Next to that, the political cycle is like a driver who every four years switches the navigation from "shortest" to "fastest" and is surprised the truck behind him did not turn 90 degrees.

So the less obvious effect is not only in demand for electric cars. It is that companies with modular production, able to build combustion, hybrid and electric versions on a similar platform, gain more strategic value than companies tied to a single technology bet. Looser standards may ease the pressure for fast EV volumes, but they will also reveal who built an EV business because of real customer demand and who did it mainly because of the regulatory whip.

The QMA framework: we watch five things, namely policy, margins, capital spending, customer behaviour and the supply chain. Here policy changes the pace, but margins and capex will show the truth. If less strict rules improve cash flow on traditional models, management may postpone part of its EV investment. But if it already has signed battery commitments or factories under construction, regulatory relief need not mean immediate relief for the wallet.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

A plus for traditional carmakers with a large share of pickups, SUVs and combustion models, for example General Motors (GM), Ford (F) or Stellantis (STLA). The mechanism is simple: less regulatory pressure can reduce the need to offset sales of less efficient cars with a higher share of EVs, hybrids or purchases of regulatory credits. But they are not the same: Ford has big exposure to pickups and commercial vehicles, GM combines pickups/SUVs with large EV ambitions, and Stellantis relies heavily on the Jeep and Ram brands. It makes sense to compare them through the mix of cars sold, North American margin, dealer inventories, planned capex and comments on fines or credits in their filings. But it is not a clean win: if they already have EV programmes under way, there is a risk of lower capacity utilisation.

Mixed for hybrid players and parts suppliers, for example Toyota (TM), Honda (HMC), Denso (DNZOY) or BorgWarner (BWA). Hybrids could have benefited from stricter rules as a practical bridge between gasoline and full EVs. Looser rules reduce that pressure, but customers may still want hybrids for fuel economy and range. For Toyota the key is the difference between the regulatory advantage of hybrids and genuine customer demand; for parts suppliers it depends on whether they earn money on combustion powertrains, hybrids or purely electric components.

A minus for pure EV makers and charging infrastructure, such as Tesla (TSLA), Rivian (RIVN), Lucid (LCID), ChargePoint (CHPT) or EVgo (EVGO). It is not that demand for EVs suddenly disappears. But the political pull that could have sped up adoption and pushed competitors toward larger electric volumes is weakening. With Tesla the market also usually watches revenue from regulatory credits, because their value depends on how costly it is for others to miss the rules. For Rivian and Lucid the more sensitive question is volumes and factory utilisation; for charging companies it is the number of EVs actually on the road, not just political targets.

A mild plus for fuels and refiners: Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), Valero (VLO), Marathon Petroleum (MPC), and indirectly tanker companies such as DHT Holdings (DHT). If the vehicle fleet stays less efficient for longer, that supports gasoline demand. But the effect is slow, because the car fleet takes years to turn over.

With news like this, do not watch only the headline. In company filings and management calls, look for: the share of EVs and hybrids in sales, average prices and margins on pickups/SUVs, comments on regulatory credits, any fines or reserves, utilisation of battery plants, dealer inventories, changes in capex, and gross margin excluding one-off items. The biggest risk is mistaking political relief for an immediate economic saving.

The fleet fuel economy average is like a school rule that the whole class must have a better average grade in PE. If you have a few athletes, you can afford classmates who run like a fridge on wheels. When the rule is relaxed, carmakers do not have to push their most efficient cars as hard to balance thirstier models. For the market this means less pressure for fast EV growth, possible support for more profitable combustion models, and for an ordinary person potentially a wider choice of conventional cars, but also a slower decline in fuel consumption across the whole vehicle fleet.

With AI energy, do not read a backlog as revenue already earned; first check the financing, the grid connection, the quality of the contracts and the risk of share dilution.

An AI data centre can sell out the ball before the hall is even built. Former bitcoin miners such as IREN (IREN), TeraWulf (WULF), CleanSpark (CLSK), MARA (MARA) and Riot Platforms (RIOT) are trying to rebuild their story from crypto mining into AI computing capacity and energy infrastructure. According to the report, they have large contracted volumes relative to their current revenues, yet none of these companies generates positive free cash flow; the build-out rests on GPU-backed debt, customer prepayments and share issuance.

The biggest mistake with AI electricity is confusing "demand" with "built capacity". Wall Street may paint steeper growth in data centre consumption than the IEA does, but the physical world keeps slower office hours: grid connections, transformers, turbines, permits, construction work, cooling and, above all, financing.

Here is the unexpected point: these former bitcoin miners are not primarily a technology story. They are companies with one scarce input — access to electricity, land and infrastructure. In the AI fever, they are trying to sell what used to feed their mining machines: megawatts. But a megawatt on paper is not the same as a megawatt in a rack full of GPUs.

The historical parallel is not the dot-com software bubble, but rather the fibre-optic cable of the turn of the millennium. Internet demand really did explode in the end, but many investors confused the right long-term direction with the wrong short-term timing and weak balance sheets. AI electricity can be a real megatrend, and some companies can still hit a wall if they build faster than their cash, the grid and their customer contracts allow.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The plus side is cleanest for companies selling shovels in the AI gold rush, not for those that have to finance the whole mine. If the build-out really gets going, the winners include suppliers of data centre power and cooling such as Vertiv (VRT), electrification components from Eaton (ETN), grid and service capacity such as Quanta Services (PWR), and makers of turbines and power equipment such as GE Vernova (GEV) and Siemens Energy (ENR.DE).

The second layer is computing hardware suppliers: chipmakers such as NVIDIA (NVDA) and server builds from Dell Technologies (DELL) or Super Micro Computer (SMCI). With them, though, it is important to watch whether orders rest on firm customer payments, or on ever more expensive capital and optimistic projections.

The pivoting miners themselves — IREN, WULF, CLSK, MARA and RIOT — face a mixed to risky impact. A big backlog relative to today's revenue looks beautiful in a presentation, but if a company is burning cash and funding growth with shares, revenue growth can arrive together with dilution of existing shareholders. It may also help power generators and independent producers such as Constellation Energy (CEG) or Vistra (VST) if they sign firm contracts, but overshooting demand estimates can lead to expensive construction that pays back more slowly.

With news like this, a better trader does not read the headline backlog number but its quality. Checklist: 1) is the contract take-or-pay, meaning the customer pays even if it does not use the capacity? 2) does it include prepayments, or only future promises? 3) does the project have a confirmed grid connection, not just land? 4) how much of the build-out is covered by operating cash, and how much by new debt or shares? 5) does the company use GPUs as collateral — and what happens if the value of used hardware falls?

The risk is not that AI will not need electricity. The risk is that the market pays today for capacity that will arrive physically, regulatorily or financially much later.

A backlog is like the booking list of a new restaurant. It looks great when your calendar is full of weddings, but if you do not yet have a kitchen, cooks, electricity or money for an oven, the bookings alone will not feed anyone. For the market, this means: shares can react sharply to large contracted volumes, but what an investor's wallet ultimately cares about is whether they turn into real revenue without painful borrowing and share dilution.

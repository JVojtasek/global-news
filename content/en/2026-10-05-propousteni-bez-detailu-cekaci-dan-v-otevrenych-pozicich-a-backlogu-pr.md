With layoffs announced without details, watch not only the cost saving but the waiting tax: open positions, project backlog, restructuring costs and, above all, whether revenue keeps growing after the cuts.

The worst thing about layoffs is sometimes not the parachute but the corporate fog: you know the storm is coming, you just do not know whether it will be over your desk. The report describes how companies from Nike to Meta announce broader cuts in advance, but often do not tell employees exactly who, where and when will be affected. The hard number in this report is in fact zero: zero details on headcounts, locations and dates.

The market often reads layoffs as a simple equation: fewer people equals lower costs equals better margins. But this equation has a hidden item that spreadsheets handle badly: the waiting tax.

When a company announces cuts without details, it creates not just a savings programme. It creates an organisational traffic jam. People postpone projects, managers are afraid to hire, employees look for work pre-emptively, and customers of more sensitive services may perceive uncertainty in support. It is like a chef in a restaurant announcing that he may close half the kitchen, but not saying when. The guests still sit at their tables, but their orders suddenly sound more cautious.

To keep the waiting tax from being just a nice term, it can be operationalised with four indicators: 1) whether management comments mention more postponed projects and a weaker backlog, that is, the stock of ordered work; 2) whether open positions are disappearing and a de facto hiring freeze is emerging; 3) whether restructuring costs and severance are rising, which can mask the real saving in the short term; 4) whether revenue growth holds after the cuts, or margins only look better thanks to a lower cost base.

There is also an unconventional macroeconomic effect. Layoffs announced today need not show up in official labour statistics right away, especially when they are gradual cuts, voluntary departures or the cancelling of open positions. In the US there is also the WARN Act, which for selected mass layoffs usually requires 60 days' advance notice, but thresholds and rules differ by situation and state. That is why the market can see the cloud before the statistical rain.

Within QMA this is a clash of five things: company profitability may look better in the short term, employee and consumer sentiment worse, seasonality of purchases more cautious, valuations more sensitive to every word from management, and crowd behaviour more nervous. In other words, the same news can be a relief for one stock and a warning light for another.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It may help large platforms and companies where wages are a significant part of operating costs. For communications and technology firms such as Meta Platforms (META), Alphabet (GOOGL) or Salesforce (CRM), the market usually watches whether cuts really lower the ratio of costs to revenue, or merely shift the pain into one-off severance and restructuring costs.

A mini-example of this trap was shown by Zoom Video Communications (ZM). In February 2023 it announced a headcount reduction of roughly 15%, about 1,300 people. In the short term investors could read this as discipline after pandemic-era hiring, but according to company results for fiscal 2024, revenue grew by only 3.1%, after 7.1% the year before. The lesson is not that cuts are bad. The lesson is that a cost saving alone does not answer whether demand is returning.

Nike (NKE) has a mixed impact. If it cuts mainly for efficiency, it may protect margins. If it cuts because demand for trainers and sportswear is softening, that is less pleasant news for the consumer cycle. Likewise, companies such as GameStop (GME) are rather indirectly sensitive to household mood: a person waiting to see whether they will lose their job will more often postpone a dispensable purchase than the rent.

It may hurt recruitment and staffing firms such as Robert Half (RHI) or platforms tied to the labour market such as ZipRecruiter (ZIP). When companies slow hiring, the volume of ads, commissions and demand for services falls. Beware also of software charged per user: for firms such as Workday (WDAY), Zoom (ZM) or Atlassian (TEAM), a lower number of employees at customers can hold back licence expansion even though the products themselves remain good.

With news like this it is better not to watch only the headline "we are laying people off". A more useful checklist: 1) are headcounts, countries and dates known, or only fog; 2) is it cost discipline or weaker demand; 3) do one-off severance and restructuring costs appear; 4) are hiring and the number of open positions falling; 5) is the project backlog shrinking, or are customers taking longer to decide; 6) does management talk about margins or about revenue. The biggest trap: the market sometimes celebrates savings before it knows whether the cut is also into future growth.

Restructuring is a corporate term for rebuilding a company. Imagine a household that says: from next month we will save, but does not yet tell anyone whether the holiday, the car or dining out is being cancelled. Everyone at home starts spending more carefully today. For the market this means the same thing: the shares of some companies may benefit from the prospect of lower costs, but the wider economy may feel a chill, because employees and households postpone purchases.

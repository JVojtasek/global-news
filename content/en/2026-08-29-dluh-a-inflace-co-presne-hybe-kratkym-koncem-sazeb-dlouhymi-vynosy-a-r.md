Inflation is a political restaurant bill where voters mainly remember the price of dessert — and according to the news provided, the bill is now catching up with Trump. The source says that in 2024 the president promised an economic course correction, but almost two years on, high debt and rising prices are complicating that battle, and with the midterm elections ahead.

This news is not just about the president's popularity. For the market, what matters more is that two things are meeting that are politically hard to put out at the same time: high public debt and voters' sensitivity to prices.

Debt calls for discipline, prices call for relief. But before elections there tends to be a temptation to do the exact opposite of discipline: give households relief, support demand, promise lower taxes or shift costs elsewhere. The source does not spell out specific measures in the excerpt, so it is not possible to claim what exactly the government will do. But the framework is clear: if the market comes to believe that policy will be less fiscally careful before the elections, it may start demanding a higher reward for holding long-term bonds.

And here is an often overlooked detail: it is not only about whether the central bank cuts rates or not. For long bonds, the so-called term premium also plays a role, that is, the surcharge for the uncertainty of a longer future. It is like a friend promising to pay you back tomorrow — fine. But when he says in ten years, and meanwhile plans an expensive kitchen renovation, you want more certainty or higher compensation. The bond market thinks similarly, except that instead of a friend it is assessing a state.

A newer layer is diagnostics: a long yield is not one thermometer, but an aquarium with three fish — expected Fed rates, expected inflation and, precisely, the surcharge for uncertainty. When it is mainly the ten-year yield that swims upward while the short end of the curve does not as much, it is not automatically the same story as a hawkish central bank. It may rather be the bill for debt and uncertainty about future inflation.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The negative side is mainly assets dependent on cheap financing. Real estate and REITs, that is, listed property landlords, typically suffer when financing becomes more expensive and safe bonds offer a more attractive alternative. For them, sensitivity is not measured just by a feeling from the chart, but in practice through four indicators: how much debt matures soon, what share of the debt carries a floating rate, what the ratio of net debt to operating profit is, and whether the rental yield of the properties still provides a sufficient cushion over the yield on government bonds. Illustrative examples of sensitive companies: American Tower (AMT), Prologis (PLD) or Realty Income (O). In the residential market, higher mortgage rates would be a burden for homebuilders such as D.R. Horton (DHI) or Lennar (LEN), again only as an example of the mechanism, not as a claim about their current moves. There it pays mainly to distinguish cancelled orders, buyer incentives, the size of the backlog and gross margins.

Banks such as JPMorgan Chase (JPM) or Bank of America (BAC) tend to see a mixed impact. Higher rates can help income from loans, but if at the same time clients' ability to repay worsens or the economy cools, the advantage quickly turns into accounting sudoku. In practice it is not just “rates up = banks better”; what matters is how quickly banks have to raise deposit rates, how credit quality develops and whether falling bond prices are putting pressure on capital.

Companies with pricing power may be relatively more resilient — that is, those that can pass price increases on to customers without an immediate loss of demand. Large consumer goods brands such as Procter & Gamble (PG) or Coca-Cola (KO) are typically cited as illustrative examples. Here too the source does not say they are winners of the news; it is an explanation of why the market sorts them differently in an inflationary environment from highly indebted sectors. Real pricing power shows less in a press release and more in whether a company's margins hold without sold volumes dropping sharply.

With political-inflation news like this, it helps to distinguish three layers: first, short rates, which reflect expectations of the central bank; second, long yields, which also contain fear of debt and future inflation; third, market breadth, that is, whether the pressure is felt only by real estate and bonds, or is already spilling over into banks, consumer stocks and smaller companies. A better checklist than “inflation bad, stocks down” is: what the two-year yield is doing, what the ten-year yield is doing, whether real yields after adjusting for inflation are rising, and whether the pressure appears mainly in companies with high debt or across the board. The risk is mistaking a political headline for a simple market conclusion: the same news can be negative for long bonds because of debt, but neutral for stocks in the short term if company earnings hold up.

The term premium is a surcharge for an uncertain future. Imagine lending money to your neighbour: for a week, no questions asked; for ten years, you want more, because anything can happen — a divorce, a new motorbike, expensive energy. With a state it is similar. When investors sense that debt is growing and prices are misbehaving, they may want a higher yield from long-term bonds. The practical impact: more expensive money can feed into mortgages, property valuations and the stocks of companies that rely heavily on cheap financing.

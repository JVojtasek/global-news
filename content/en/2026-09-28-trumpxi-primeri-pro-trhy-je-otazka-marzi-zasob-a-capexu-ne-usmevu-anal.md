At US–China summits, watch the politicians' smiles less and watch more closely whether uncertainty starts to fade from margins, inventories, capex and company comments on licences and tariffs.

A truce between the US and China is like boxed sushi: while it is fresh, everyone looks pleased — but if it is not eaten quickly, you start to smell the problem. According to the report, Donald Trump and Xi Jinping met in Washington and expect two more meetings this year. What matters is not the leaders' photo opportunity itself, but whether it produces tangible results: less uncertainty around tariffs, export restrictions and supply chains.

The market often overrates "big meetings" and underrates the dull paperwork that follows. A summit is theatre, but the real film is cut in the annexes: the list of exemptions, licences, controls, customs categories and permitted trade channels.

The fresh angle is this: US–China calm today is not a binary question of "deal or no deal". It is more of an operating mode, like an airport in a storm. Planes do not fly according to whether the pilot is smiling, but according to how many runways are open, what the visibility is and whether the control tower changes its instructions every ten minutes.

For companies, the most valuable thing is not a "peaceful atmosphere" but predictability. If management does not know whether a particular component will, in three months, face a tariff, need a licence or be off the table altogether, it holds more inventory, spreads production across more countries and signs more expensive backup contracts. That then shows up in the financial statements as pressure on gross margin, working capital and capital spending. In other words, geopolitics does not reach share prices only through headlines, but through accounting lines.

In the QMA view, this is a mix of the "macro", "earnings" and "crowd behaviour" pillars. The crowd celebrates dialogue. Earnings, however, need less friction in logistics, licensing and pricing. And macro wants to know whether tariffs and restrictions will remain a tax on uncertainty.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

It mainly helps companies for which uncertainty is itself a cost. Examples are chipmakers and chip designers such as NVIDIA (NVDA), Qualcomm (QCOM) or Broadcom (AVGO): their economics depend on access to customers, manufacturing partners and licensing rules. For TSMC (TSM), a different mechanism matters — not simply "Chinese demand", but the stability of global orders and cross-border technology flows.

Companies with complex supply chains could also feel a positive effect: Apple (AAPL), Tesla (TSLA), industrial companies such as Caterpillar (CAT) or aerospace group Boeing (BA). For them, the question is not just sales, but also where parts come from, how long inventory sits and whether production has to be expensively rerouted.

On the other hand, it could hurt anyone who has already bet on chaos as a business advantage. Companies profiting from relocating production, duplicating suppliers or more expensive local capacity could lose part of their premium story if tensions genuinely ease. And if the summit brings no concrete steps, the risk will keep hanging mainly over technology, communication services and industrials.

With news like this, it does not pay to watch only the politicians' tone. A better framework is "where will it show up in the financial statements":

- revenue by region: how much companies earn in China or the US,

- gross margin: whether tariffs, rerouted production and logistics are eating into profitability,

- inventories and working capital: whether companies are stockpiling components just in case,

- capital expenditure: whether they are building parallel production footprints,

- comments on licences and export restrictions: whether management speaks concretely or stays vague.

Beware of summit euphoria: without concrete measures, it is more a calendar event than a change in the economy.

"Tangible results" means not that two leaders shake hands, but that the cook finally gets the recipe and the shopping list. For the market, it is the difference between "maybe there will be dinner" and "we know what is cooking, at what cost and when". Shares of companies that depend on US–China trade react mainly to the second version, because only that allows them to plan prices, production and inventory. For ordinary people, this may show up over time in electronics prices, the availability of goods and the mood in markets — not instantly or by magic, but through companies' costs.

With geopolitical deals in Africa, do not watch only the total value of announced agreements, but above all the breakdown by sector, country, project stage, financing and the actual role of specific companies.

From the perspective of the great powers, Africa is changing from a map of opportunities into a map of insurance policies: whoever has access to ports, mines, grids and loans has leverage beyond Africa too. The US administration says it has helped close 37 commercial deals in Africa worth 25.67 billion dollars, while Washington is framing the Chinese presence more sharply as a combination of export dominance, state subsidies and security influence. The essence is not just diplomacy; it is a contest over who will build, finance and control the critical nodes of further growth.

The biggest mistake is to read this news as a classic USA versus China: whoever gives more money wins. In reality, the game is about something more boring, and for that very reason more important: the bankability of projects. That is, whether a political announcement turns into a financeable construction project, contract, power plant, mine or logistics corridor.

An important revision compared with the usual QMA framework of announcement versus reality: here it is not only that a memorandum is not an order. It is that the package of 37 deals worth 25.67 billion dollars is, without a detailed breakdown, analytically semi-transparent. Ideally, an investor would need to see for each deal the country, sector, project stage, financing institution, role of the American company, timing, and whether it is a binding contract, financial close, or merely a politically signed intention. Without that, the aggregate number is a bit like a hypermarket receipt showing only the total: you know someone spent a lot, but you do not know whether it was on a fridge, bread rolls or an inflatable unicorn.

This matters in practice. If most of the volume consists of a few capital-heavy infrastructure projects, the impact on suppliers' revenue may come slowly and in stages. If, on the other hand, a larger part is in technology, energy equipment or machinery, it may feed through into orders faster. And if these are mainly framework agreements, the market may be celebrating a wedding while the groom has not even hired a suit yet.

China has the advantage in Africa of a long presence, state banks, construction companies and the ability to deliver a railway, road or port as a package: loan, contractor, workforce, technology. The USA more often comes with private capital, export support, security partnerships and pressure on standards. That sounds less cinematic than a geopolitical duel, but for markets the difference is fundamental: the Chinese model often triggers order volume faster, while the American model may be slower, but if it goes through, it tends to be more attractive for companies with higher margins, technology and a compliance apparatus.

QMA framework: this is not just about the geopolitics pillar. It also touches corporate profits, capital expenditure, the commodity cycle and crowd behaviour. The crowd often buys the first headline about Africa as the new growth continent. A better analyst asks: how many of those 37 items are memoranda, how many binding contracts and how many already have financing? Is it a photo of politicians, or an order for turbines, excavators and cables?

### Who it helps and who it hurts

Potential winners are industrial companies that can deliver infrastructure, energy, mining equipment and project management: Caterpillar (CAT), Deere (DE), GE Vernova (GEV), Fluor (FLR), AECOM (ACM) or European groups such as Siemens (SIE.DE), Schneider Electric (SU.PA) and ABB (ABBN.SW). If American pressure translates into concrete contracts, it is not only about building roads; it is about electrification, grids, data centres, mining operations and ports.

The difference is in timing. Machinery, servicing and technology components may show up in orders sooner. Large infrastructure needs permits, financing, land, local politics and route security. That is why, with a package like these 37 deals, it would be key to know whether energy and industrial supplies dominate, or multi-year corridors and ports. The same dollar amount can mean a completely different revenue path for shares.

On the commodity side, it may help companies linked to copper, cobalt, lithium, uranium or iron ore, because infrastructure lowers the costs of mining and export. Examples of the impact can be found among global miners such as BHP (BHP), Rio Tinto (RIO), Freeport-McMoRan (FCX) or among Chinese players such as Zijin Mining (2899.HK) and CMOC Group (3993.HK), which already operate in African raw-material chains.

On the other hand, low-margin Chinese exporters may come under pressure if African governments start protecting local industry more or diversifying suppliers. Local African manufacturers also face risk: cheap subsidised imports may make their inputs cheaper in the short term, but crush domestic capacity in the long term. For Chinese infrastructure companies such as China Communications Construction (1800.HK) or CRRC (1766.HK), the impact is mixed: greater political scrutiny may slow new contracts, but their pricing and execution strength remains high.

With news like this, it pays not to count press conferences, but to follow the project conversion funnel:

- Announced deal versus legally binding contract.

- Financial close: who is actually providing the loan or capital?

- Breakdown by country: one more stable market is not the same as a project in an area with security risk.

- Breakdown by sector: energy, mining, logistics, digital infrastructure and consulting have different margins and timing.

- Share of American companies: is the company the main contractor, a subcontractor, an adviser, or just part of a consortium?

- Local permits, licences, elections and the risk of a change of government.

- Type of asset: a port and a power plant have a different impact from a consulting agreement.

- Time horizon: machinery and services may come within quarters, large infrastructure more likely in years.

- Route security: a project in a risky region can be economically beautiful and logistically hell.

The main warning: a geopolitical headline can raise expectations faster than an excavator breaks ground. The market sometimes prices a treasure map even though no one has found a shovel yet.

Project bankability means: it looks credible enough for someone to lend real money to it. It is like a friend saying they will open a café. The idea is nice, but the bank will ask: is there a lease, a budget, suppliers, customers and permits? For the market, the impact is simple: announcements move the mood, but only a financeable project creates orders for companies, work for suppliers and potentially pressure on commodity and infrastructure prices.

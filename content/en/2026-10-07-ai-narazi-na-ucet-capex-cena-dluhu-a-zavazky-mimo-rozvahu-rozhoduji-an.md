With AI investment, watch less of the talk about revolution and more of the cash left after capex, the cost of debt and off-balance-sheet commitments — that is where you can tell whether the boom is just digesting or hitting a wall.

When even the richest companies on the planet start to look like a household that bought a pool, a sauna and an electric-car charger all at once, the market sits up. The underlying report argues that the boom in AI infrastructure investment is running into a financing problem: hyperscalers such as Meta (META), Amazon (AMZN), Microsoft (MSFT), Alphabet (GOOGL) and Oracle (ORCL) face pressure on free cash flow, more expensive debt and off-balance-sheet commitments. The point is not "AI is over", but "the pace of the build-out may stop being set by demand for chips and start being set by the balance sheet".

The most interesting thing about this report is not the bubble. That word is used so often it already has its own exercise bike in the gym of financial clichés. More interesting is the shift in the bottleneck: for a long time the market worried about whether there were enough GPUs. Now the question is becoming: is there enough cheap capital, electricity, data centre space and accounting tolerance?

The AI cycle looks like software, but financially it is starting to behave like a mix of utilities, real estate and an airline fleet. Every further leap in performance is not just a line of code; it is a server hall, cooling, networking gear, backup power, long contracts and depreciation. That is the counter-intuitive part: the more AI looks like "digital magic", the more it resembles concrete, copper and an electricity bill.

Here comes the practical lesson. Free cash flow, the cash left after investment, is like oxygen in a marathon for these companies. Cloud revenue may be growing, but if capital spending is running even faster, the athlete is speeding up while breathing through a straw. And off-balance-sheet commitments are like a wedding banquet you have already ordered: it is not all on your card statement yet, but the chef is already cutting the cake and knows whom to send the bill to.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The downside is most visible among AI infrastructure suppliers. Chipmakers such as NVIDIA (NVDA) and AMD (AMD) are sensitive to the pace of accelerator orders; Broadcom (AVGO) to networking and custom chips for data centres. TSMC (TSM) and ASML (ASML) are not hit immediately in the same way, because the semiconductor chain plans far ahead, but if big customers start talking about a slower capacity ramp-up, the market usually prices it in ahead of time.

Companies around data centres see a mixed impact: Vertiv (VRT), Eaton (ETN) or Schneider Electric (SU.PA) benefit from demand for cooling, power and switchgear, but their valuations may be sensitive to any hint of project delays. Data centre REITs such as Digital Realty (DLR) and Equinix (EQIX) may keep profiting from a shortage of suitable sites and electricity, but long contracts are not the same as endless growth.

Among the hyperscalers the picture varies. Microsoft (MSFT), Alphabet (GOOGL), Amazon (AMZN) and Meta (META) have strong businesses that can carry the investment for longer, but the market will watch more closely whether AI spending actually brings new revenue and margins. Oracle (ORCL) is more sensitive to the cost of debt and how quickly its cloud expansion pays back, because an aggressive infrastructure build-out can hurt its balance sheet sooner.

Asset-light software companies such as Adobe (ADBE) or Salesforce (CRM) may look relatively better if they can monetise AI features without giant physical infrastructure of their own. That is not a tip, just logic: less concrete in the business means less hunger for capital.

A better trader does not follow only the headlines about the "AI boom" with a story like this, but five checkpoints: 1) is capex growing faster than cloud revenue, 2) is free cash flow after investment deteriorating, 3) are leases and off-balance-sheet purchase commitments piling up, 4) is debt getting more expensive for these companies, 5) are chip and infrastructure suppliers talking about order delays. If spending slows and revenue holds, it is healthier digestion. If spending balloons, cash disappears and debt gets dearer, the "AI wall" is getting closer.

Off-balance-sheet commitments are like ordering a kitchen renovation, paying only the deposit and insisting at home that the bill does not exist yet. But it does exist — it just has not arrived all at once. For the market, this means investors look not only at today's debt but also at future bills that are already in motion. For shares of AI suppliers, the risk is that customers slow their orders. For an ordinary wallet, it can mean bigger swings in technology indices within pension and investment portfolios, because big tech companies carry a heavy weight in them.

With news like this, watching the oil price is not enough; what decides is whether inventories and the futures curve confirm physical strain, and whether expectations for Fed rates are being lastingly repriced at the same time.

Oil today is not just fuel; it is a tax collector in a leather jacket who turns up at the petrol pump, in company income statements and in a central banker's head. Wall Street fell on Tuesday: the Dow Jones Industrial Average shed 617 points, or 1.16%, to 52,797.10, while the S&P 500 lost 0.58% to 7,673.94. The trigger was a combination of higher oil on renewed tension in the Middle East and stronger bets that the Fed could raise rates next week.

The most interesting thing is not the 600-point drop in the Dow itself. It sounds dramatic, but in percentage terms it is 1.16%; moreover, the Dow is a price-weighted index, so more expensive shares move it more than cheaper ones, regardless of company size. That is like judging a household budget by the price of the biggest item on the receipt rather than the whole shop.

The less visible problem is a collision of three clocks. The first clock ticks in minutes: the price of oil and gasoline futures reacts to the headline. The second ticks in weeks: the physical market has to show whether inventories, supplies and routes are really changing. The third ticks to the Fed's calendar: if the shock arrives just before a meeting, the market reprices not only oil, but also whether central bankers will get less room to be softer.

This is a different framework from a simple "oil up = inflation up". The key is whether a more expensive barrel turns into more expensive money. In practice: the jump in Brent or WTI on its own is the first chapter, but the second chapter is the reaction of the short end of the yield curve — mainly two-year US yields — and of the prices of 30-day Fed funds futures traded on the CME. When oil jumps but the expected Fed rate for the nearest meeting quickly returns, the market is saying: "It was noisy fireworks." But when the rate repricing holds into the next session as well and the two-year yield joins in, it is already pressure on equity valuations, not just more expensive filling up.

Here is QMA's added filter: do not deal only with the oil price, but with the path of the shock and its translation into rates. A short shock is a headline and a price jump. A structural problem only arises when physical flows change: ship insurance gets more expensive, routes get longer, cargoes are delayed, inventories fall and the oil futures curve tightens into backwardation, a situation where near-term deliveries cost more than distant ones because the market wants oil now. That is the difference between one spilled coffee and the café running out of a grinder.

Publicly trackable anchors: weekly inventories, Cushing stocks, refinery utilisation and petroleum product supplies are published by the U.S. Energy Information Administration in the Weekly Petroleum Status Report; CPI inflation is released by the Bureau of Labor Statistics; the Fed publishes its decisions and rate projections after the FOMC meeting; exchange prices of Fed funds futures and oil futures are available through the relevant exchange contracts.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The plus side is clearer for energy producers and services companies: integrated oil companies such as Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), Shell (SHEL) and BP (BP) may benefit from a higher realised oil price, provided the rise does not destroy demand. Services players such as SLB (SLB) or Halliburton (HAL) tend to be sensitive to producers' investment budgets.

The minus side is more varied. Airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL) and United Airlines (UAL) deal with fuel as one of their key cost items; the impact depends on hedging, that is, prices locked in in advance. Logistics such as FedEx (FDX) and UPS (UPS) can pass some costs on through fuel surcharges, but not always immediately. Chemicals and materials, for example Dow (DOW) or LyondellBasell (LYB), run into inputs tied to the energy complex. Long-duration assets also suffer from rates: utilities such as NextEra Energy (NEE) and real estate companies such as Realty Income (O) or Prologis (PLD), because higher bond yields increase competition for their dividends and make financing more expensive. Growth technology such as NVIDIA (NVDA), Microsoft (MSFT) or Apple (AAPL) may feel similar rate pain, because a large part of their value rests on profits expected in the future.

Banks such as JPMorgan Chase (JPM) or Bank of America (BAC) are a mixed case: higher rates can help interest margins, but an economic slowdown raises the risk of worse loans. For LPG, it is better to watch the gap between the commodity price, freight rates and contracts; an example is the carrier Dorian LPG (LPG), where it is not only the price of the molecule that decides, but also the price of its journey.

A checklist for news like this: 1) for Fed funds futures, watch the implied rate for the nearest meeting before and after the headline; more important than a minute-long jump is whether the repricing survives into the next trading day and moves in the same direction as the two-year US yield, 2) for oil, do not compare only the Brent or WTI price, but the gap between the nearest and the sixth to twelfth monthly contract; a larger backwardation that lasts several days shows the market is paying a premium for immediate availability, 3) in the EIA data, read crude inventories, Cushing, gasoline, distillates, refinery utilisation and product supplied, an approximate gauge of domestic demand, all together; one weekly deviation is noise, two reports in the same direction are worth attention, 4) separate oil from gasoline: if only the barrel rises, but not gasoline futures and retail fuel prices, the pass-through to CPI may be weaker, 5) in equities, watch whether it is mainly carriers, real estate, utilities and growth technology that fall, or whether it is a broad sell-off. Treat it as a regime change only when at least two or three points are confirmed at once; a single headline is often just a market siren, not a fire.

Backwardation is like a queue in the heat for the last bottled water: water right now is worth more than water next week. For the market, this means immediate availability of oil matters more than the distant future. It usually makes life harder for stocks, because companies pay more for energy, the Fed may be more cautious with rates and ordinary people may feel it through more expensive filling up, flights or transport of goods.

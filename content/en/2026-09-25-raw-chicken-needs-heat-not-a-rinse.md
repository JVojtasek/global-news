## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Public-health agencies advise against washing raw chicken because rinsing does not make it safe and can spread contamination by splashing.

**What it means.** The sink adds movement, not protection. Safety comes from keeping raw juices away from ready-to-eat food, cleaning hands and tools, and cooking the meat thoroughly.

**Risks and impact.** Invisible droplets can reach the sink, tap, worktop, clothes or nearby food. The risk is greatest when contamination reaches something that will not be cooked, such as salad.

**What can be done.** Move poultry from package to pan without rinsing, use a separate board, wash hands for 20 seconds, clean touched surfaces and check that the thickest part reaches 165°F (74°C).

**What to watch.** Follow the path of raw juice, not merely the appearance of the chicken. If the sink or surrounding area was exposed, clean and sanitise it before preparing food that will be eaten raw.

## FACTS

Raw chicken can carry Campylobacter, Salmonella and Clostridium perfringens, according to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. It may look and smell ordinary. Water does not reliably remove these germs, and rinsing creates another route by which they can travel.

CDC guidance, dated April 29, 2024, says raw chicken is ready to cook and does not need washing. It recommends keeping chicken and its juices away from ready-to-eat food, washing hands with soap and water for at least 20 seconds, using a separate cutting board and checking an internal temperature of 165°F, or about 74°C, with a food thermometer.

The agency also cites USDA consumer research in which one in seven people who cleaned a sink after washing chicken still had germs in the sink. That result concerns the kitchen environment, not whether every chicken carries the same organism or quantity.

The UK Food Standards Agency gives the same central advice. Its Campylobacter guidance says washing poultry can splash contamination onto hands, work surfaces, clothes and equipment. It recommends never washing poultry, preventing raw-to-ready food contact, chilling raw meat in a leak-proof container and cooking it thoroughly.

## EVIDENCE

The strongest evidence here combines microbiology, fluid mechanics and observed household behaviour. Health agencies know raw poultry can be contaminated and that adequate heat kills relevant bacteria. The remaining question is what running water does before cooking.

A peer-reviewed experiment published in *Physics of Fluids* on March 24, 2022 placed raw chicken under flowing water and used large agar plates, bacterial culture, DNA sequencing and high-speed imaging. The researchers confirmed that bacteria could move from the chicken surface in splashed droplets. A greater distance between faucet and meat produced more unstable flow and more splashing; the soft surface could form a small divot that redirected water.

The experiment's restraint matters. It did not identify specific pathogens in the transferred droplets. It found bacteria, including genera that contain pathogenic members, and demonstrated a mechanism by which harmful organisms could spread when present. That supports risk, not certainty about each splash.

The CDC and FSA recommendations are broader because households cannot identify contamination by sight. Both focus on a barrier system: separation prevents transfer, cleaning removes contamination from hands and surfaces, chilling limits growth, and cooking supplies the lethal step. Rinsing adds no comparable control.

## PERSPECTIVES

For some cooks, washing poultry is not a casual mistake. It is a cleanliness practice learned from family, sometimes tied to removing loose material, odour or residue before seasoning. A safety message that mocks the habit will miss the reason it persists. The useful distinction is between cleaning for appearance and controlling microbes. Water may change the surface. It cannot certify the meat as safe.

The strict public-health frame says simply: never wash it. That is the clearest rule because it removes an unnecessary splash event. The CDC now also gives harm-reduction instructions for people who nevertheless rinse—use a gentle stream, then immediately clean and sanitise the sink and wash hands. That is not evidence that rinsing is beneficial. It recognises that behaviour does not always change after one warning.

A third frame focuses only on final temperature: if heat kills the germs, preparation does not matter. This overlooks the salad, tap handle or towel that will never enter the oven. Cooking controls contamination on the chicken. It cannot travel backward through time and cook the cucumber cut on the same unwashed board.

The sensible hierarchy is therefore not purity but control. Avoid the step that spreads raw juice. Keep the raw zone small. Clean what touched it. Then verify the temperature rather than judging doneness by colour alone.

## CONTEXT

Kitchen safety is a problem of routes. A pathogen needs a source, a way to travel and a person who consumes enough of it to become ill. Raw poultry can be the source. Hands, utensils, boards, towels and droplets can become the route. Ready-to-eat food is a particularly efficient destination because it has no later heat step.

This explains why a sparkling-looking sink is not proof of safety. Microbes are too small to see, and a damp cloth can carry them beyond the original spill. It also explains why soaking chicken in lemon juice, vinegar or salt water is not a substitute for cooking. A household marinade may affect flavour or surface texture; it is not a validated kill step for the whole piece.

A practical workflow starts before the package opens. Prepare salad or fruit first and move it away. Set out the pan, board, knife, thermometer and a place for packaging. Open the chicken over the board, transfer it without rinsing, discard the packaging and wash hands. Anything touched with raw hands—including spice jars and the tap—belongs on the cleanup list.

After cooking, insert the thermometer into the thickest part without touching bone and look for 165°F or 74°C. Wash the board, knife, dishes and worktop with hot soapy water. If raw juice reached the sink, clean and then sanitise it according to the product instructions. Use a fresh cloth or paper towel rather than spreading the cleanup water around.

This sequence is less theatrical than rinsing. That is its advantage. Each action blocks a known route instead of trying to make danger visible.

## PEOPLE

The person cooking is often working quickly, perhaps with a child asking a question and several dishes competing for the same small counter. “Do not cross-contaminate” sounds abstract in that room. A clean-to-dirty order is easier to use: ready-to-eat food first, raw poultry last, then cleanup.

Households also differ. One kitchen has a deep double sink and a dishwasher; another has one basin beside the only preparation space. The smaller the space, the more useful it is to keep the raw zone deliberate. A tray or board can define it. The goal is not anxiety about every surface. It is fewer contacts to remember.

People at higher risk of severe foodborne illness—including young children, older adults, pregnant people and those with weakened immune systems—have less room for a casual lapse. But the same workflow serves everyone. It replaces a ritual that looks cleansing with barriers that can actually be checked.

## DEEPER

Rinsing chicken feels persuasive because water is one of our oldest signs of cleanliness. Mud runs away. A cloudy glass becomes clear. The eye receives evidence that something happened. Heat inside a piece of meat is quieter. A thermometer supplies a number instead of a cleansing scene.

Safety often asks us to prefer an invisible mechanism over a visible ritual. Seat belts do not make a road look safer. Ventilation does not announce which particle left the room. A food thermometer cannot make dinner feel cared for in the way a familiar preparation habit can. Yet mechanism matters when appearance and risk part company.

That does not make tradition foolish. Many household practices began under different conditions and carried several meanings at once: removing feathers or debris, managing smell, marking the start of preparation, doing food “properly”. Respecting that history allows a practice to change without treating the people who learned it as the problem.

The deeper skill is tracing consequences beyond the object in front of us. The chicken will be cooked. The lettuce beside it will not. The hand will touch the tap. The towel will touch the next hand. Good safety thinking follows the path, then places one effective barrier at each turn.

A rinse concentrates attention on the meat. A safer kitchen widens the frame.

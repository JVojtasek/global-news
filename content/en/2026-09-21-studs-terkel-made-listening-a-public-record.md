## BRIEFLY

**Who.** Louis “Studs” Terkel was a Chicago broadcaster, actor, activist and author whose long radio career became a vast record of twentieth-century voices.

**What he did.** On WFMT and in books such as *Division Street: America*, *Working* and *The Good War*, he placed celebrated guests beside waitresses, labourers, organisers, office workers and people rarely asked to narrate public life.

**Why it matters.** Terkel did not make listening passive. He used curiosity, follow-up and silence to let a speaker complicate the premise of the question.

**The limit.** A recorded memory is a perspective, not an automatically verified fact. Interviewer choice, editing, context and the archive's gaps still shape what survives.

**A practical takeaway.** Begin with a concrete question, follow one revealing word instead of rushing to the next prompt, and distinguish what a person experienced from what outside records can establish.

## FACTS

Terkel began a morning programme at Chicago fine-arts station WFMT in 1952. The station suited his mixture of music, politics, culture and conversation. His guests included artists and political figures, but the category that made his work distinctive was less glamorous: people explaining how work, race, depression, war and city life felt from inside an ordinary day.

The radio practice migrated into books. *Division Street: America* appeared in 1967 from conversations with Chicagoans across occupations and social positions. *Hard Times* followed in 1970, *Working* in 1974 and *American Dreams: Lost and Found* in 1980. *The Good War*, an oral history of the Second World War, won the 1985 Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction.

The scale matters. The WFMT archive describes thousands of programmes across a broadcast career that ran until 1997. The Chicago History Museum holds the tapes associated with his oral-history books, while WFMT and the museum manage the radio archive with digitisation support from the Library of Congress. What began as ephemeral broadcasting became searchable historical material.

## EVIDENCE

The archive's biography offers a concise account of the method: Terkel asked questions and then listened. It says he drew out things speakers did not know they had in them. That sounds romantic until one hears the recordings. His prompts are often plain, but he notices an unexpected phrase, hesitation or contradiction and stays there long enough for another layer to emerge.

His 1973 conversation with Rosa Parks is one example. The historical outline is familiar, yet the recording preserves pacing, emphasis and Parks's own choices about what to foreground. TIME's presentation of the audio reminds readers that a voice carries evidence a quotation alone cannot: not secret access to perfect memory, but cadence, agency and the relationship between question and response.

The archive also documents institutional mediation. WFMT manages the platform with the Chicago History Museum; the Library of Congress supplied digitisation; grants from the National Endowment for the Humanities supported development. Interactive transcripts widen access, while licensing still governs reuse. Preservation is not simply pressing “record.” It requires selection, description, rights work, technology and continuing stewardship.

## PERSPECTIVES

Terkel is often described as a master interviewer. The label can imply a clever extractor who obtains the quote he wanted. His own practice points elsewhere. Conversation was collaborative and sometimes unruly. A guest could reverse the direction, resist a premise or ask him questions. His value was not invisibility; his opinions and enthusiasm were audible. The craft was making that presence generative rather than dominant.

Another frame calls the books “voices of the people,” as though a microphone removed hierarchy. It did not. Terkel chose whom to approach, which exchange to pursue and what to publish. Editors arranged spoken language into readable form. Some communities and experiences remained outside the archive. Respect for a voice should include honesty about the gate through which it entered the record.

A third perspective treats testimony as either sacred truth or unreliable anecdote. Oral history is more useful than either extreme. A person may misremember a date while describing the emotional and social texture of an event with great precision. A historian can preserve that account, compare it with other testimony and records, and note disagreement instead of forcing every source into one voice.

## CONTEXT

Radio was an intimate medium before podcasts made long conversation seem newly invented. A listener heard breath, interruption, uncertainty and laughter in real time. Terkel used that intimacy across subjects usually divided into separate programmes: opera singers, civil-rights organisers, novelists and steelworkers belonged to the same civic conversation.

His politics shaped both access and exclusion. Blacklisting during the anti-communist period made work difficult, according to the archive's biography. WFMT later gave him a durable home. That history matters because the ideal of open conversation was not abstract for him; the boundaries of permissible speech had affected his livelihood.

The oral-history books also arrived when official narratives often reduced large events to leaders, dates and outcomes. *Hard Times* asked how the Depression was remembered by people who lived through it. *Working* asked what labour did to identity, pride and boredom. *The Good War* complicated its admiring title with memories that did not align neatly. The method changed the unit of history from the event alone to the person carrying it.

Digitisation changed the audience again. A tape once accessible to a specialist in Chicago can now be heard with a transcript and searched by topic. Yet search creates its own temptation: jump to the matching sentence and miss the ten minutes that gave it meaning. An archive makes fragments easy to find; good listening restores the surrounding conversation.

## DEEPER

The deeper lesson is that a question is not only a request for information. It allocates dignity and attention. Asking a hotel cleaner, nurse, executive or unemployed worker what their day means says that interpretation does not belong only to experts. The answer may still be partial or self-serving, but it has been admitted into evidence.

### A practical listening sequence

Start with something a person can see or remember: “What happened when you arrived?” is easier to inhabit than “What does society misunderstand?” When a charged noun appears—*pride*, *waste*, *afraid*, *fair*—ask what it means in that person's example. Let a pause remain. Then check the boundary: “Is that something you saw yourself, heard from someone else, or concluded later?”

For any account you may publish, explain purpose and obtain consent appropriate to the setting. Preserve the original recording when possible, note edits and keep fact-checking separate from policing emotion. A speaker owns their experience; neither speaker nor interviewer owns every factual inference that can be drawn from it.

Terkel's achievement was not that he discovered a magic question. He built a habit of attention, repeated over decades, and institutions later did the unglamorous work that kept it audible. The record survives because curiosity was paired with tape, description, rights and care.

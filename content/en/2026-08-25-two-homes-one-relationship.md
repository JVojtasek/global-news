## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** More older couples are maintaining committed relationships while living in separate homes, an arrangement researchers call living apart together, or LAT.

**What it means.** Two homes can preserve routines, property and independence without making the relationship casual. The arrangement works by replacing many automatic assumptions of cohabitation with deliberate coordination.

**Risks and impact.** Ambiguity becomes costly when illness, money, adult children, emergency access or unequal travel and care enter the picture. Emotional closeness does not automatically create legal standing.

**What can be done.** Couples can discuss schedules, expenses, illness, family boundaries, keys and emergency contacts before a crisis decides for them.

**What to watch.** The useful test is not whether two addresses look unconventional, but whether both partners can describe the same commitments and revisit them as health, housing and family needs change.

## FACTS

At ages 90 and 94, Luci Dannar and James Pastoret had a routine that looked both intimate and carefully bounded. As reported by The Washington Post, they lived about 300 steps apart in the same Missouri retirement community. He came for supper; each returned to private time. They had also toured assisted-living facilities and discussed what they might do if either needed more care.

That detail matters. “Living apart together” is sometimes presented as romance without laundry: keep the pleasant evenings and avoid arguments about towels. For older couples, the choice can carry more weight. A home may represent financial security, a familiar neighbourhood, proximity to children, memories of a previous spouse, or freedom hard won after years of caregiving.

UCL researchers reported in 2024 that around four per cent of people over 60 in the UK were in a steady relationship across separate households. Their longitudinal work, using UK household data from 2011 to 2023, found associations between entering LAT relationships and improved mental wellbeing compared with remaining single. The improvement was slightly smaller than that associated with marriage or cohabitation. These are population patterns, not a promise for an individual couple.

## EVIDENCE

The evidence supports a more modest conclusion than either fans or critics may want. Separate homes can provide companionship alongside autonomy. They may also make separation less disruptive because property and households were never merged. But observational data cannot show that the address arrangement itself caused better wellbeing. People who can afford two homes, remain healthy enough to travel between them, and freely choose the arrangement may differ from those constrained by housing, work or care.

Qualitative research adds texture. Older adults describe wanting to retain routines, finances, friendships and personal space. Some women, in particular, have already spent long periods caring for children, parents or an ill spouse and do not want cohabitation to silently recreate a gendered domestic job. That is a serious preference, not an evasion.

Caregiving research also complicates the tidy picture. Studies of older LAT couples found both commitment and uncertainty. Partners may intend to help during illness while still protecting independence. In practice, illness can transform a relationship that was organised around visits into one requiring transport, medication collection, advocacy and daily presence. The two-home model does not abolish dependence; it delays the moment when its terms must be negotiated.

## PERSPECTIVES

One perspective says commitment should be visible through a shared household. Cohabitation makes ordinary care easier: noticing a change in health, sharing costs, responding at night, handling chores. Separate homes can hide unequal effort. One partner may always travel, host, cook or adapt while both describe the arrangement as independent.

The opposing perspective notes that a shared address is not proof of mutual care. Cohabiting couples can be lonely, controlling or financially entangled. Two homes can protect dignity and allow a relationship to be chosen repeatedly rather than maintained by inertia. For people with established property, children and histories, not merging everything may be prudent.

Neither view wins in the abstract. LAT can be a chosen long-term form, a temporary stage, or a constraint imposed by jobs, housing or family duties. The honest question is whether the arrangement serves both people under current conditions—and whether it can change when those conditions do.

## CONTEXT

Cohabitation supplies defaults. Who has a key? The person who lives there. Who notices that someone did not get up? The person in the next room. Who pays the electricity bill? Usually an established household system answers. Two homes remove those defaults.

Law removes fewer uncertainties than many couples assume. Rules differ by country, and an unmarried partner may not automatically have authority to receive medical information, make decisions, enter a home, inherit property or organise a funeral. This article cannot settle those legal questions. It can identify them early enough for a couple to seek locally qualified advice where necessary.

Family recognition matters too. Adult children may see a later-life partner as central, peripheral or threatening. In one case described by researchers in The Washington Post, a woman learned that her partner had entered a nursing home only after she could no longer reach him. A relationship can be emotionally real and institutionally invisible at the same time.

## PEOPLE

Dannar and Pastoret did something easy to overlook: they discussed a future that neither wanted to romanticise. Their preferred arrangement included separate apartments while it worked and the possibility of sharing assisted accommodation—with separate bedrooms—if care required it. The value was not the specific solution. It was their willingness to name a trigger for changing the plan.

That is the human skill beneath LAT. Autonomy is not silence. It is the ability to make a choice openly, hear what it costs the other person, and revise it without treating revision as failure.

## DEEPER

A useful conversation has six parts.

**Time.** Which days are usually shared? Who travels? How much solitude does each person need, and how will cancelled plans be handled?

**Money.** Which costs remain separate, which are shared, and what happens when one person has less income or a home needs major repair?

**Illness.** What kind of help is freely offered? What would require outside care? At what point would the current arrangement be reconsidered?

**Family.** How will adult children be introduced to the commitment? Who should be contacted in an emergency, and who may not make decisions for the couple?

**Access.** Who has keys, alarm codes and essential contact information? Are permissions and legal documents appropriate for the jurisdiction and the couple’s wishes?

**Review.** Put a date on the calendar. A plan made for two healthy drivers may fail after surgery, bereavement or loss of mobility. Reconsidering it is maintenance, not betrayal.

## REFLECT

Two homes can create breathing room. They can also create a corridor full of unspoken assumptions. The arrangement is strongest when independence is not used to avoid the difficult subjects that shared walls would eventually force into view.

A committed relationship does not need to look conventional to be serious. But seriousness has practical evidence: the ability to answer who comes, who pays, who knows, who may enter, and what changes when one person can no longer cross those 300 steps.

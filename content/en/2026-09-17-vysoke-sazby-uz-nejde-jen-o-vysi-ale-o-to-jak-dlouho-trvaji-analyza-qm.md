With higher rates, it isn't just about the increase itself, but about how long time stays expensive — and whether pressure on valuations spills over into credit stress.

The biggest victim of higher rates isn't a stock or a bond — it's investors' patience. The source says the Fed is raising interest rates because the fight against inflation is dragging on; alongside that, it mentions political opposition to a large arms sale to Israel and BlackRock's plan to bring the 401(k) closer to a pension model. For markets, the main event is the Fed: higher rates change the price of money across stocks, bonds, and credit.

The usual explanation goes: "higher rates hurt growth stocks". More accurate is: higher rates tax the future. The longer inflation persists and the Fed has to stay tough, the more the market punishes companies whose big profits lie somewhere beyond the horizon.

That's why the same news can hurt technology names more than, say, boring insurers. With a growth company, the investor is often paying for a story: "today we invest, tomorrow we dominate the market". But with higher rates, "tomorrow" is worth less, because money today already earns something on its own. Patience stops being free.

An unexpected connection is BlackRock and a more pension-like 401(k). In the era of zero rates, bonds looked like a bland diet bread roll: safe, but they excited hardly anyone. When rates rise, pension-like products — those focused on regular future income, not just a number in an account — make more economic sense again. So higher rates aren't just "bad for stocks"; they rearrange the whole financial ecosystem, from speculating on growth toward valuing cash, coupons, and cash flow.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The minus side is clearest for long-term bonds: when yields rise, the prices of previously issued bonds fall. That's why government and corporate bonds with longer maturities may come under pressure.

"Long-duration" stocks tend to be under pressure too — companies where a large part of the value rests on future profits. Examples of the impact: chipmakers such as NVIDIA (NVDA), AMD (AMD), or Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSM), and software companies such as Salesforce (CRM) or Snowflake (SNOW). That doesn't automatically mean a bad business; it means tougher valuation math.

Banks such as JPMorgan Chase (JPM), Bank of America (BAC), or Wells Fargo (WFC) face a mixed impact. Higher rates can lift income from loans, but at the same time they make funding more expensive, increase pressure on deposits, and can worsen credit quality if the economy slows.

Insurers and asset managers, for example MetLife (MET), Prudential Financial (PRU), or BlackRock (BLK), may find relative support if higher bond yields make products based on future income more attractive. Defense companies such as Lockheed Martin (LMT), RTX (RTX), or Northrop Grumman (NOC) may come into play through the politics around arms sales to Israel, but that is a separate geopolitical channel, not a direct effect of the Fed.

With news like this, it's better not to focus only on "the Fed raised rates", but on three layers: how much more it can raise, how long it will keep rates high, and whether the pressure is spilling into credit. If stocks fall but credit spreads — the premium of corporate bonds over government bonds — stay calm, it's more of a valuation repricing. If spreads widen significantly, the market is no longer dealing only with more expensive money, but with fear of funding problems.

Technically speaking: a rebound in stocks carries more weight when short-term yields stop rising at the same time and credit spreads stop widening. If the index jumps but two-year yields keep climbing and credit stress intensifies, it may be just an appealing bounce into a strong headwind.

A "discount rate" is like a friend promising you dinner — either today, or sometime in three years. When money is cheap, waiting doesn't matter much. But when you can earn decent interest elsewhere in the meantime, dinner in three years doesn't look so tempting. For the market, this means that distant future profits are worth less, stocks with big promises may be more vulnerable, and for the ordinary person, mortgages and loans get more expensive, while savings may earn more than before.

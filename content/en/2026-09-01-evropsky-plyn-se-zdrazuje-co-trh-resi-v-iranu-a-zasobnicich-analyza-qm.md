With gas, watching the price is not enough: the key is whether storage is nearing its pre-winter comfort level, whether LNG ships from places such as Qatar keep flowing without pricier transport, and whether companies can pass higher energy costs into prices without losing margin.

Gas in Europe is once again behaving like a taxi in the rain: there are not necessarily dramatically fewer cars, but suddenly everyone is paying for the certainty of getting home. European natural gas prices extended their rise because escalation between the US and Iran heightened supply worries, and according to the report, Europe's storage is 65.09 % full. That is not an empty fridge, but it is not a pantry after the big pre-winter shop either.

The most interesting thing about this news is not the rise in the gas price itself. It is the fact that the market is reacting to Iran even though Iran is not a major direct supplier of gas to Europe. The point is the Strait of Hormuz: a narrow sea passage between Iran and Oman through which, according to the US EIA, a significant share of global LNG trade passes, especially Qatari cargoes from the Ras Laffan terminal. And Qatar is not just "another supplier" — it is among the world's largest LNG exporters, and its ships head to both Asia and Europe.

After Russian pipeline flows were curtailed, Europe swapped the old model of more stable contracts for greater dependence on LNG, that is, liquefied gas carried by ship. And ships love not just calm seas but also cheap insurance, available crews, normal tanker rates and the certainty that a cargo will not get stuck in a geopolitical traffic jam. When the risk premium around Hormuz rises, it does not have to mean that gas physically disappears. It is enough that shipping gets more expensive, shipowners ask for more, insurers tighten their terms and traders start redirecting cargoes to wherever they get the best price. Europe then competes not only for gas molecules but for a place in the tanker queue.

The counterintuitive part: tension in the gas market does not have to arise from molecules physically disappearing. Often it is enough for traders to start repricing the risk that they might disappear in future. It is like a supermarket announcing that there may be no butter. The shelf is still full, but the first three people take five blocks each, the fourth starts panicking, and the price in everyone's head jumps before the price tag does.

Through the QMA lens, this is mainly a lesson about the market's fifth pillar: crowd behaviour. Storage at 65.09 % says the system has a cushion. But context matters: in Europe, comfort before winter is typically measured by whether storage gets filled high before the heating season; the European target is 90 % by 1 November. So 65.09 % on its own is neither panic nor victory. The key is the pace of refilling: when storage fills briskly, the market tends to be calmer; when filling slows because of heat, weaker LNG flows or more expensive purchases, the same 65 % starts to look much less comfortable.

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The plus side falls mainly on gas producers and companies with a large LNG portfolio. Examples: Equinor (EQNR) as a major Norwegian gas supplier to Europe, and Shell (SHEL) and TotalEnergies (TTE) thanks to their global LNG activities. For these companies, a higher European gas price typically improves realised prices on part of their production or their trading margins, although the specific impact depends on hedging, contracts, volumes and whether pricier transport eats up part of the gain.

The minus side is more precise than energy-intensive companies in general. The most sensitive are chemicals and fertilisers, where gas is not just energy but also a feedstock. Watch, for example, BASF (BAS.DE), Yara International (YAR.OL) or CF Industries (CF). In steel, glass and building materials, pricier gas can squeeze margins through heat and electricity; examples are ArcelorMittal (MT) or glass packaging makers such as Verallia (VRLA.PA).

Utilities such as RWE (RWE.DE) or Uniper (UN01.DE) have a mixed profile: part of their costs can be passed on to customers or hedged in advance, but sharp price swings make managing cash and collateral harder. Oil refiners such as Valero Energy (VLO) are more of an indirect case: European gas is not their main driver, but pricier energy can change hydrogen costs, operating margins and wider energy sentiment.

With news like this, do not look only at the headline "gas price rising". A better checklist: 1) the state of European storage and the speed of refilling relative to the path to 90 % before winter, 2) demand driven by weather and industrial activity, 3) LNG flows from Qatar and other exporters, ship waiting times and LNG tanker rates, 4) insurance and risk premiums for voyages near Hormuz, 5) the gap between short-term and winter gas contracts, 6) company comments on hedging, outages and passing on costs. The risk is simple: the market often punishes the companies least able to pass on costs before it fully shows up in the income statement.

The Strait of Hormuz is like a narrow bridge on the main road to a big warehouse. The warehouse may be full and the trucks may exist, but when everyone starts fearing something will happen on the bridge, transport gets pricier even before any accident: drivers want a surcharge, the insurer raises its price and customers would rather order early. For the market, this means that even Iran, which does not send Europe its main volume of gas directly, can make European gas more expensive through Qatari LNG and maritime logistics. For stocks, it helps LNG producers and traders but hurts companies that burn gas or make things from it. For the ordinary person's wallet, the impact is indirect: pricier energy can over time seep into the prices of goods, heating and inflation expectations.

## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Researchers have turned the “old-book smell” from a collector's intuition into a subject for analytical chemistry and heritage science.

**What it means.** A book slowly releases volatile organic compounds as paper, adhesives, inks, cloth and leather age. Some evoke vanilla, almonds, grass, acid or an old room. The blend is also shaped by light, heat, moisture, pollution and previous owners.

**Risks and impact.** Pleasant age and damaging damp can overlap in the vocabulary of smell. A musty note does not prove mould, while a sweet nostalgic scent does not prove that a book is healthy.

**What can be done.** Treat scent as a clue. Check for visible spotting, fuzzy growth, dampness, distortion and changes in the storage area before deciding whether a volume merely smells old or needs conservation attention.

**What to watch.** A sudden change across several books is more informative than the familiar aroma of one volume. It may point to new moisture, heat or ventilation problems in the room.

## FACTS

Books smell because molecules small enough to enter the air reach receptors in the nose. Chemists call many of them volatile organic compounds, or VOCs. New books emit them too, from fresh inks, solvents, coatings and adhesives. An old book's mixture is different because years of chemical change have joined its original materials.

Paper is built largely from cellulose, long chains that give plant fibres strength. Many wood-pulp papers also contain lignin, a complex material in plant cell walls. Acids, oxygen, light and heat can break these structures down. The reactions produce smaller airborne compounds.

The 2009 study led by conservation scientist Matija Strlič used headspace analysis—sampling the air around paper—to identify volatile degradation products. The researchers correlated the chemical profile with properties that matter in preservation, including paper acidity, lignin, rosin and the degree of polymerisation of cellulose. The work was not a parlour trick for dating first editions. It explored a non-destructive way to assess degradation.

## EVIDENCE

Some molecules help explain familiar descriptions. The Science History Institute's account notes that degrading cellulose can emit furfural, perceived by many people as sweet or almond-like. Lignin-related breakdown can produce benzaldehyde and vanillin, contributing almond and vanilla associations. Hexanal can add an earthy or musty “old room” note.

None of these molecules works alone. Paper may be joined by animal glue, synthetic adhesive, printing ink, leather dressing, cloth, dust, tobacco smoke or the remains of something once pressed between the pages. A book stored in a dry cabinet and an identical copy kept near a damp wall are no longer chemically identical twins.

In 2017, Cecilia Bembibre and Strlič combined chemical sampling with human descriptions to create a historic-book odour wheel. Their research framed smell as part of heritage, not simply a defect. It also exposed the translation problem: an instrument records compounds, while a person reports “woody,” “chocolate,” “smoky” or “grandfather's study.” Both are information, but they are not interchangeable.

## PERSPECTIVES

The romantic view says the reader is inhaling history. Chemically, that is almost true: the molecules carry information about materials and their ageing. Psychologically, however, much of “the past” is supplied by the reader. The same odour can summon a childhood library for one person and a damp basement for another.

Conservators have a more cautious relationship with the nose. Odour can direct attention to a problem without identifying it. Mustiness may be intensified by mould, but mould is a biological finding, not a poetic synonym for old paper. Conversely, harmful deterioration can proceed without a dramatic smell.

This is the strongest limit on the appealing idea of sniffing a book's age. Manufacturing recipes changed across eras, but storage conditions and repairs can overwhelm that signal. Human sensitivity and vocabulary vary. Chemical profiles may help compare condition, yet the nose alone cannot supply an exact date, provenance or diagnosis.

## CONTEXT

The science belongs to a broader change in conservation. Traditional inspection often requires taking a sample from an object. Sampling air around an object offers the possibility of learning something without cutting a page. That is valuable for rare books, archives and collections where even tiny losses matter.

The heritage work goes one step further. If the smell of a historic library shapes how visitors experience it, conservation may need to document an atmosphere as well as bindings and shelves. That raises a peculiar question: should preservation slow the very degradation that helps create the cherished aroma? The sensible answer is yes. The object takes priority; nostalgia is not a licence to let paper decay.

## DEEPER

Old-book smell is a compact lesson in evidence. The signal is real, but its interpretation depends on context. One molecule can have several sources; one description can cover several molecules; and memory can make the result feel more precise than it is.

That is why a change over time matters. A stable vanilla-and-paper scent in one inherited volume is different from a new musty smell spreading through a shelf after a leak. In the second case, the room is part of the specimen.

## PRACTICAL TAKEAWAY

For ordinary books, use a four-part check:

1. Smell the surrounding shelf or room, not just one page. A shared new odour suggests an environmental change.
2. Look for visible mould, powdery or fuzzy deposits, staining, dampness and warped paper. Avoid vigorous brushing of suspicious deposits.
3. Move valued or affected material away from moisture and direct sunlight. Keep storage cool, dry and reasonably stable.
4. If there is visible mould, do not experiment with household sprays or cleaners. The Canadian Conservation Institute advises isolating affected paper and contacting a conservator; mould can threaten other objects and health.

For a valuable book, conservation advice is cheaper than an enthusiastic cleaning method discovered beside a recipe for removing red wine.

## UNDERSTANDING CHECK

The smell is genuine chemical evidence, but it is broad evidence. It tells us that materials and environments have left volatile traces. It does not, by itself, tell us exactly when a book was made or whether a musty note is active mould.

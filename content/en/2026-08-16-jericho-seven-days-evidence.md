## BRIEFLY

**What happened.** Joshua 6 describes Jericho falling after seven days of silent marching, trumpets and a shout, not a conventional siege. Excavators have been digging at the site, Tell es-Sultan, on and off since the early 1900s.

**What it means.** The main archaeological question is not really whether ancient Jericho existed — it clearly did, for thousands of years — but whether a fortified Late Bronze Age city stood there at whatever date a conquest is placed, and that dating question has never been fully resolved.

**Risks and impact.** The disagreement matters mainly to specialists; it doesn't resolve the larger question of whether the conquest narrative is a battle report, a later tradition, or something else.

**What can be done.** Treat competing dating claims, from any side, with the same question: what layer, pottery type or lab result is this based on — and does it come from an excavation report or just a summary of one?

**What to watch.** Continued work at Tell es-Sultan keeps adding data that could shift the picture further either way.

## FACTS

According to Joshua 6, Jericho was shut up tight against the arriving Israelites; no one went out and no one came in. Joshua's forces marched around the city once a day for six days, seven priests carrying rams'-horn trumpets ahead of the ark, the rest of the army silent. On the seventh day they marched around seven times; at the seventh circuit the priests blew the trumpets, Joshua ordered the people to shout, and the text says the wall fell down flat, letting the army advance straight in.

The text describes the city and its contents as devoted to destruction, with silver, gold and vessels of bronze and iron set aside for the sanctuary treasury instead. Rahab, who had hidden two Israelite scouts, was spared along with her household, a story told earlier in Joshua 2. Joshua closes the account with a curse on anyone who rebuilds Jericho — a curse the book of 1 Kings later says was fulfilled, generations afterward, by a man named Hiel of Bethel.

## EVIDENCE

Some things about Jericho are not in dispute. Tell es-Sultan, the mound identified with biblical Jericho, is one of the longest-excavated sites in the Near East, with layers reaching back thousands of years before any conquest narrative. Excavators have worked there since the early twentieth century, most influentially the British archaeologist Kathleen Kenyon in the 1950s.

The contested part is the date of the city's last major Late Bronze Age destruction. An earlier excavator, John Garstang, connected a collapsed mudbrick wall to a destruction around 1400 BC, matching one traditional reading of the conquest date. Kenyon's later, more rigorous stratigraphy reassigned that wall to the much earlier Early Bronze Age, and dated the last significant Late Bronze Age town to closer to the mid-1500s BC. In the early 1990s, Bryant Wood published a reanalysis arguing Kenyon had misread the ceramic evidence and that it better supported 1400 BC after all. A later radiocarbon study on charred grain leaned toward Kenyon's earlier date rather than Wood's, though with its own margin of error, and the argument continues among specialists.

What the excavations agree on, regardless of date, is a destruction layer with a thick burn deposit and stores of grain left behind, unlooted — a detail researchers on both sides of the dating dispute flag as consistent with a quick fall rather than a long siege, though it doesn't by itself establish how the city fell.

## PERSPECTIVES

Archaeologists and biblical scholars read this evidence through genuinely different, defensible frames.

Wood and other "early date" researchers argue the destruction fits close to 1400 BC, matching a straightforward reading of the chronology implied by 1 Kings 6:1. Their strongest case is a direct challenge to Kenyon's ceramic typology; their weak point is that both mainstream ceramic dating and the later radiocarbon evidence lean earlier, not later.

Kenyon's successors, representing the mainstream consensus, weigh stratigraphy and radiocarbon results together. Their case is methodologically strong, but popular retellings sometimes push it further than the excavation record supports, into "nothing happened at Jericho at all" — the site's occupation history is long and uneven, and one destruction layer's date doesn't settle every question about the wider region.

A separate group of scholars places the whole conquest narrative later still, around 1200 BC, tied to the broader collapse of Late Bronze Age city-states across the eastern Mediterranean. For this view Jericho's specific dating argument matters less, since by then the site may have had little fortified settlement under any chronology — evidence, these scholars argue, that the story reflects later national memory rather than a contemporary report.

A fourth, literary approach reads Joshua 6 mainly as a composed narrative shaped by later theological concerns — the ritual sevens, the curse fulfilled only in 1 Kings — whose clearest value is what it shows about how Israel told this story about itself, independent of what the ground confirms.

## CONTEXT

The number seven does real work here. Six ordinary days, then a seventh with seven circuits and seven priests: it reads less like a siege log and more like a ritual building toward one moment. Ancient Near Eastern sieges typically relied on ramps, battering and starvation over months or years — the kind of engineering evident at sites like Lachish. Joshua 6 describes something categorically different, part of why scholars debate whether to read it as military history, ritual narrative, or both.

The practice of devoting a captured city's people and goods to destruction, called herem in Hebrew, was not unique to Israel. A comparable practice appears on the Moabite king Mesha's ninth-century BC inscription, which describes devoting an Israelite town to the god Chemosh in almost identical terms — showing the concept belonged to a shared regional vocabulary of war and religion, not a claim invented for this one story.

Jericho's deeper history adds weight independent of any single battle. Long before any conquest narrative, the site held a Neolithic settlement, including a stone tower now dated to roughly 8,000 BC, among the oldest fortified structures known anywhere. That long occupation is why the site keeps getting re-excavated and re-interpreted: every new dig, and every new technique from Kenyon's stratigraphy to radiocarbon calibration, can revise a previous generation's confident conclusion.

## PEOPLE

Kathleen Kenyon is the figure most responsible for how this debate is shaped today. Her excavation method, digging in careful vertical sections rather than clearing broad horizontal areas, became the standard for Near Eastern archaeology well beyond Jericho itself; the dating puzzle she left behind is, in a real sense, a byproduct of doing the work more rigorously than her predecessors had.

Bryant Wood spent much of his career trying to reopen a question Kenyon's generation considered settled, arguing the same pottery could support a different answer. Whether or not his conclusion holds up, re-litigating a consensus with its own methods is a normal, healthy part of how archaeology works.

Inside the text, the one figure given a personal story is Rahab, a Canaanite woman whose house was built into the city wall, and whose choice to shelter two strangers is treated, in Joshua and later New Testament references alike, as the single human thread in an otherwise military account.

## DEEPER

One version of this story resolves cleanly: the walls fell exactly as described, the date lines up, the debate is over. Another resolves just as cleanly the opposite way: the archaeology disproves it all, so the account gets filed away as legend. Neither reach is quite earned by the evidence as it stands.

What the text itself foregrounds is not the mechanism of the wall's collapse but the space before it: six days of marching around a closed city, seeing no visible result, with nothing to do but keep going. "On the seventh day they rose early... and marched around the city seven times in the same manner... and at the seventh time... the wall fell down flat" (Joshua 6:15–20). Whatever one makes of the historical question, that structure — instruction followed for its own sake, long before any outcome is visible — is what the passage spends most of its words on, not the moment of collapse itself.

That is a different question than "did this happen exactly as written," and it is worth sitting with on its own terms. Most of what people build or hold onto happens during unglamorous repetition, not the dramatic seventh circuit. The text doesn't resolve whether the ground will ever settle the historical argument. It does spend six-sevenths of its length on the part where nothing visible happens yet.

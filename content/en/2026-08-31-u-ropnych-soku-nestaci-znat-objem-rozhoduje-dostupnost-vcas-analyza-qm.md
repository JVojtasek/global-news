With oil, what decides is not only the volume of replacement supply, but above all whether it is physically, legally and operationally available in the same time window as the problem.

When the oil market is choking at the Strait of Hormuz, offering Venezuela as the rescue is a bit like sending a plumber to a fire — useful, but not in time. According to Rystad Energy, chief economist Claudio Galimberti says it will take Venezuela 'several years' to significantly increase oil production. The point: a possible deal with Venezuela may help in the long term, but it will not solve a short-term shock in a key shipping artery.

The market here is dealing not only with 'how much oil exists' but above all with the time compatibility of barrels. A barrel that may arrive in a few years is not the same as a barrel missing at a refinery next week. It is the oil version of guests arriving tonight and you replying: 'No problem, in three years I'll plant tomatoes.' Agriculturally lovely, useless for this evening.

So that it is not just a pretty metaphor: a better framework is barrel × time × certainty. Oil has market value according to whether it is physically, legally and operationally available at the moment the system needs it. Venezuela may have long-term volume potential, but according to Rystad Energy's statement the timeline is 'several years'. Hormuz, by contrast, is a 'what if shipping gets stuck now' type of problem. These are two different currencies: one is future capacity, the other is the immediate resilience of the supply chain.

A fresh detail: Venezuela is not an ordinary production switch. After years of underinvestment, sanctions, operational constraints and deteriorating infrastructure, bringing production back is more like renovating an old hotel than opening a tap. But what matters is not only 'can we produce?', but also how quickly the oil turns into a delivery for a specific buyer: the well, crude processing, transport, insurance, payments, licences, a refinery able to process that grade. Each step is like a connection at an airport; one delayed gate and the whole itinerary is beautiful only on paper.

Through the QMA lens, this is a clash of two pillars: geopolitics raises supply risk quickly, while fundamental supply responds slowly. The crowd often loves headlines like 'new deal = more oil', but the better question is: how much of it is a barrel for the next delivery cycle, and how much just a distant promise in a spreadsheet?

### Who it helps and who it hurts

The plus side: a higher risk premium on oil typically supports producers and integrated energy companies, for example Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX) or Shell (SHEL). Oil services providers such as SLB (SLB) or Halliburton (HAL) may also be watched thematically, because a long-term increase in production requires wells, servicing, equipment and people.

The minus side: pricier oil is a tax on movement. It weighs on airlines such as Delta Air Lines (DAL) and United Airlines (UAL), carriers, logistics and the chemical industry, where oil is an input into costs. For refiners such as Valero Energy (VLO) or Marathon Petroleum (MPC), the impact is mixed: pricier crude hurts, but refining margins also decide, that is, the difference between the price of the input and of products such as petrol or diesel.

Direct exposure to Venezuela is narrower than the overall energy story. Not every oil company automatically benefits from a Venezuelan deal; some impacts are more thematic and depend on licences, the sanctions regime, the quality of the crude and the ability to physically get it to market.

With news like this, watch out for the headline's timing trick. A better trader's checklist: 1) is the problem in hours, weeks or years? 2) does the solution add physical barrels or only political hope? 3) is it about a shipping route, production, refining or demand? 4) is the impact direct, or only mood-driven through the risk premium? 5) what does the price gap between near and more distant oil contracts say — is it mainly immediate delivery that is getting pricier, or the whole long end of the market? A practical guide: if the risk of an outage is immediate and replacement capacity is described as 'in several years', the market usually prices not the solution but the uncertainty.

Time compatibility of barrels means it is not enough to have oil 'somewhere'. It is like having winter tyres ordered for April when it snows in January: technically you are solving the problem, practically too late. For oil, this means that distant new production may not ease the current fear of a shipping outage. For the market, it can keep energy stocks higher, weigh on companies with expensive fuel and, for the ordinary person, show up through petrol, air tickets or pricier transport of goods.

In 2024 Norway lost 1.6 people per 100,000 to its roads and the United States lost 11.6. Those are the counts in the International Transport Forum's Road Safety Annual Report 2025, published on 12 December 2025, divided by population — and that fraction is the number this whole page is argued against. Sweden is at 2.0, Britain at 2.4, Germany at 3.3, the Netherlands at 3.8, Czechia at 4.5, Poland at 5.2. You can redo any of them with a calculator: Czechia had 494 deaths and 10.90 million people.

Now read the table a second time, because another one is hiding inside it. Count deaths per kilometre driven instead of per person and the order changes. Poland loses 7.3 people per billion vehicle-kilometres; the United States loses 7.8. Two countries whose per-head figures differ by more than a factor of two carry almost the same risk per kilometre. The difference is that Poles drive less. Whenever a government quotes one of these numbers, it is worth asking which of the two it picked, and why.

Roads are the big problem where the physics is least disputed and least acted on. Kinetic energy rises with the square of speed, so a body struck at 50 km/h absorbs nearly three times the energy of a body struck at 30. The power model — Rune Elvik's 2009 report for the Norwegian institute TØI is the standard version — puts the exponent linking speed to fatalities at about 3.0 in towns and about 4.6 on rural roads. None of this is contested. What follows is five places that acted on it and measured what happened, including one country that counted 331 saved lives and then had the measure repealed underneath it, and one that wrote a rule about fuel and changed the shape of the front of every new car.

  - country: fi
    what: "Finland tested a group, not a campaign. In the Circle of Friends trial, older people who said they were lonely were put into closed groups of seven or eight with two trained professional leaders, meeting twelve times over three months. Groups did one of three things: writing and group psychotherapy, exercise and discussion, or art. It was a randomised controlled trial — the rarest thing in this field."
    result: "Among 235 people aged over 74, survival at follow-up was 97% in the intervention group (95% CI 91–99) against 90% in the control group (95% CI 85–95), p = 0.047. Health care costs were €943 per person per year lower (95% CI −1955 to −127, p = 0.039), and subjective health improved significantly. By 2022 the programme had run for 12,000 participants in Finland with 1,200 trained facilitators, and a ten-year follow-up reported that nine in ten participants said their loneliness had eased."
    caveat: "One trial, 235 people, and a p-value of 0.047 is a hair inside the conventional line. It has not been repeated at that scale anywhere else. It also recruited older people who agreed to join a group twelve times, which is not the same population as everyone who is lonely — the least connected are the hardest to enrol, and the trial cannot say anything about them."
    source: "Pitkälä and colleagues, 'Effects of psychosocial group rehabilitation on health, use of health care services, and mortality of older persons suffering from loneliness', The Journals of Gerontology Series A, July 2009; Jansson, Circle of Friends, presentation to the European Commission Joint Research Centre"

In 2022, 11.2% of people in the European Union aged 16 or over said there was nobody they could ask for help if they needed it. In 2018 the figure was 7.7%. It is Eurostat's dataset `ilc_pw06`, it is free, and it runs from 2.4% in Norway to 29.3% in Romania. One warning before using it: several countries moved by more than ten points in either direction between the two rounds — Germany from 7.6% to 20.2%, Bulgaria from 18.0% to 4.6% — which is more than a country's social life plausibly changes in four years. Those are almost certainly the survey moving, not the population, which is why Germany and Bulgaria are not on the board.

The question that dataset asks is about isolation, not loneliness. Isolation is countable: does a person exist who could be asked. Loneliness is a feeling about the gap between the company someone has and the company they want, and the surveys that try to measure it disagree with each other. The OECD, using EU-SILC data for 2022 and publishing on 16 October 2025, found about 6% of people across 25 countries feeling lonely most or all of the time. The EU Loneliness Survey, fielded online in November and December 2022 with 25,646 respondents, found 13%. Both are competent. The instrument changes the answer.

One number in this field travels further than any other, and it is worth getting right. In 2010 Julianne Holt-Lunstad and colleagues pooled 148 studies covering 308,849 people and found that those with stronger social relationships had a 50% higher likelihood of surviving the follow-up period, which averaged 7.5 years. To show what that size of effect meant, they lined it up against other known risks — and the smoking comparison in that chart was for moderate smoking, defined as up to 15 cigarettes a day. That is where "loneliness is like fifteen cigarettes" comes from. Holt-Lunstad has since written that the original comparison was for lacking social connection in aggregate, of which loneliness was one part, and the underlying studies are observational: they show association, not proof of cause. Health is a subject on this page, not counsel; nothing here is a diagnosis or advice, and a doctor is the right address for that.

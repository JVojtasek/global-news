  - country: cz
    what: "Czechia has legislated its way at this twice. The 2004 Act on Pedagogical Staff obliged teachers without the qualification to obtain it. Then in 2017 the country changed school funding from money per pupil to money per taught hour, effective 2020, and schools prepared early by splitting classes and adding optional lessons. From 1 September 2023 an amendment allowed a holder of a relevant master's degree to teach for up to three years while completing the pedagogical study."
    result: "Unqualified teaching fell from more than 15% at the start of the 2000s to under 5% by 2016, then climbed back to 10% in basic schools and almost 7% in upper secondary schools by 2024. The funding change added more than 4,000 teaching posts a year after 2018: full-time-equivalent teachers passed 150,000 in 2024, and pupils per teacher fell from 12.9 in 2018 to 12.0 in 2023."
    caveat: "Demand was created faster than universities could supply it. Teacher-training graduates ran near 4,000 a year historically, fell to about 2,500 after 2010 and are back near 3,000 — over 2010 to 2024 that is roughly 15,000 fewer than replacing retirements alone required. And the shortage is not national: 22% of teaching in basic schools in the Karlovy Vary region is unqualified, about double the country as a whole.   - country: gb
    what: "England tried moving the money later in the career. From 2018 the Department for Education piloted a phased maths bursary: a smaller grant during training, with the rest paid out in the third and fifth years of teaching. The upfront bursary fell from £25,000–£27,500 to £20,000–£22,000, with £5,000–£7,500 paid later. A parallel scheme paid retention money to early-career maths and physics teachers."
    result: "The evaluation by CFE Research and FFT Education Datalab found that recruitment onto maths teacher training fell by 10 to 15 per cent — an estimated 275 fewer maths teachers entering state schools in 2019/20 — while the deferred payments retained an estimated 94. The pilot lost about three teachers for every one it kept. NFER's 2025 review of five such schemes put their average effect at 5.1% lower leaving rates, not significant at the 5% level (p = 0.06), costing roughly £43,000 per additional teacher-year against £9,000–£13,000 for a training bursary."
    caveat: "The two findings do not cancel out, and this is the interesting part: the retention payments did work on retention. Eligibility for the maths phased bursary is associated with a 10.9% lower leaving rate, and that estimate is statistically significant. What broke was recruitment. NFER also warns that results here are sensitive to how eligibility is defined — its own estimate for the maths and physics retention payment is lower than an earlier study's, from the same data."

  - country: de
    what: "Berlin stopped making teachers civil servants in 2004 to save money. Every other German state carried on doing it, and for nearly two decades Berlin lost staff to neighbours who could offer the status — Brandenburg is a commute away. Across the OECD in 2022/23, 7.1% of secondary teachers did not meet the training and certification standard their own country sets for the grade and subject they teach. That is the OECD's Table D8.2, published in Education at a Glance 2025, and it is the number this page is argued against. It runs from 0.0% in Hungary, Japan and Korea to 21.6% in Sweden. Two things about it before we use it. It is defined by each country's own rules, so a government can improve it by changing the rules. And it says nothing about the posts nobody filled at all: Austria had 6.0% unqualified teachers and 5.1% of secondary posts standing empty at the start of the year.

Czechia is not in that table, so here is the national equivalent, measured differently and not directly comparable: 10% of teaching in basic schools and almost 7% in upper secondary schools was done by unqualified teachers in 2024, up from under 5% in 2016. In the Karlovy Vary region the basic-school figure is 22%. That gap between 10 and 22 is the whole point of this page. National averages are where shortages go to hide.

The pressure is mostly demographic and it is easy to check. Over a third of OECD teachers were 50 or older in 2023, and only 9% of secondary teachers were under 30. In Lithuania the share of older secondary teachers went from 44% to 59% in a decade, in Portugal from 31% to 56%. Across 19 countries with data, 6.5% of qualified teachers left the profession in 2022/23, from under 3% in France, Greece, Ireland and Israel to over 10% in Denmark, Estonia and Lithuania. Below are four governments that did something and then measured — including one that moved the money from the start of a teaching career to the middle of it.
